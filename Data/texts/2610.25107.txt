The state of the art techniques in Machine Translation (MT) require large
amounts of symmetric resources from source and target languages. This is true
regardless of whether the approach is Transfer or Interlingua, Symbolic or Sta-
tistical. Symmetry within these approaches is necessary to ensure quality, ro-
bustness and retargetability. In reality, such symmetry, whether it is in terms
of structural transfer lexicons, interlingual dictionaries or parallel corpora, is a
major bottleneck in developing any MT system.
This dissertation presents an approach to MT that addresses the lack of sym-
metry by exploiting symbolic and statistical target language resources in source-
poor/target-rich language pairs. This approach is called Generation-Heavy Hy-
brid Machine Translation (GHMT). Expected source language resources include
a syntactic parser and a simple one-to-many translation dictionary. No trans-
fer rules or complex interlingual representations are used. Rich target language
arXiv:2610.25107v1 [cs.CL] 18 Oct 2026

symbolic resources are used to overgenerate multiple structural variations from a
target-glossed syntactic dependency representation of source language sentences.
Statistical target-language resource are then used to select amongst the overgen-
erated translations. The source-target asymmetry of systems developed in this
approach makes them more easily retargetable to new source languages.
The contributions of this research include: (1) a new model for machine trans-
lation that transcends the need for large amounts of symmetric knowledge while
maintaining a high degree of robustness, quality, and retargetability; (2) a sys-
tematic framework for handling translation divergences that uniformly accommo-
dates a wide range of seemingly diﬀerent divergence types and their interactions;
(3) a hybrid (symbolic-statistical) generation approach that expands the concept
of symbolic overgeneration to include conﬂation and head-swapping of structural
variations; (4) the introduction and use of structural n-grams on a large scale in
natural language generation; and (5) the creation of several resources that have
been used by other researchers including an extensible MT system for translating
into English and a large-scale categorial variation database for English.
An extensive evaluation suggests that GHMT is more robust and has superior
output quality, in terms of grammaticality and accuracy, relative to a primarily
statistical approach.

Chapter 1
Introduction
1.1
Motivation
Machine translation (MT) systems can be classiﬁed in terms of their complexity
and their approach. Complexity refers to the place of the system on the MT
pyramid: word-gist, transfer or interlingua (IL). Approach refers to the place of
the system on the hybridity continuum: symbolic, hybrid or statistical. These
classiﬁcations are theoretically equally valid choices for building an MT system.
Practically, however, other criteria are more inﬂuential in making decisions on
the kind of system to build. There are four such criteria: Quality, Cost, Retar-
getability and Robustness.
Quality refers to a system’s translation quality. It can be further dissected
into Accuracy (or Fidelity) and Fluency (or Intelligibility). Accuracy is a mea-
sure of how much of the information in the source language (SL) is carried in
translation to the target language (TL). Fluency is a measure of how TL-like
is the output of the MT system. Fluency can further be divided into speciﬁc
aspects of the TL output: grammaticality and lexical choice of TL sentences. A
prevalent MT problem relevant to translation quality is Translation Divergence,
1

cases where the underlying concept or “gist” of a sentence is distributed diﬀer-
ently in two languages. Examples include making improvements in one’s
life versus improving one’s life, swimming across the lake versus crossing
the lake swimming or passing the night laughing versus laughing the night
away.
The second criterion is the cost of building an MT system speciﬁcally in terms
of time it takes to build resources and use them. Third, retargetability refers
to how reusable the diﬀerent modules and resources of an existing system are.
Retargetability and cost are quite related. The more retargetable a system, the
more it is worth building and the more distributed the cost can be over several
future systems. Finally, robustness refers to how well a system does in terms of
quality when used on new kinds of input such as a new genre that the system is
not trained on or does not have resources for.
These criteria interact with each other and with the complexity and approach
of the MT system under consideration. A gist-based system allowing multi-word
translation can be built to provide high quality translation if enough phrases and
clauses are included in a translation dictionary. The size of the dictionary and the
cost of building it would be prohibitively high (theoretically inﬁnite). A transfer
approach producing the same level of quality would cost less to build although the
complexity of the involved resources would be higher. This is the tradeoﬀbetween
cost and complexity while maintaining a constant level of quality. Additionally,
a gist-based approach requires resources that are not retargetable for any other
kind of MT system or any other pair of languages. A transfer approach has a
similar problem at the level of transfer-lexicon building.
But SL parsers and
TL generators, both necessary modules for transfer MT, can be independent
2

of the translation lexicon and thus are retargetable. An approach with higher
complexity such as interlingua requires more complicated lexicons to translate
SL to IL and IL to TL. But such components are highly retargetable since they
are language-pair independent.
The complexity of an MT system is independent of its approach. The symbolic
approach has been used for all levels of complexity. Currently, most primarily
statistical systems are low in complexity (multi-word gist-based with movement).
Syntax-based statistical MT [140] is an exception. There is currently no IL-based
statistical MT. The attractive simplicity of statistical approaches comes at the
cost of development and annotation of training data, parallel corpora that are
not retargetable to other language pairs.
The cost-complexity tradeoﬀwhile maintaining quality applies to statistical
approaches as well. For a statistical system to be able to handle a wide range of
phenomena, it needs to train on such phenomena. Syntax-level information can
allow a statistical model to abstract linguistic knowledge with a smaller parallel
corpus. But such a corpus needs to be syntactically annotated, which comes at a
higher cost. Additionally, statistical systems have a negative eﬀect on robustness
since the bottom-up type of information derived from a parallel corpus is very
related to the speciﬁc genre of the corpus. Therefore, such information is less
likely to apply well to other input genre.
In the context of the work presented in this dissertation, it is necessary to
add another dimension to classifying MT systems: symmetry, which refers to
the amount of resources and modules used for analysis and generation in an MT
system. The MT pyramid assumes such symmetry to be inherent in any MT
system. At the word-gist level, a translation dictionary is a perfectly symmetri-
3

cal resource dedicated and coupled with the two speciﬁc languages it translates
between. At the transfer level, the output of SL parsing and the input of TL gen-
eration are expected to be of the same level of syntactic complexity. Additionally,
the transfer lexicon is a more sophisticated symmetrical translation dictionary.
This symmetry also carries to the interlingual level. As for statistical approaches,
parallel corpora are, by deﬁnition, symmetrical resources.
The symmetry assumption is a major bottleneck in developing any MT sys-
tem in a world of asymmetrical language resources. There are few languages that
have extensive resources and that have been well studied linguistically. Resource-
richness can be seen in the amount of available digital text, the size of lexical
semantic resources such as word-nets, the availability of IL lexicons, and the
existence of annotated tree-banks.
The problem of symmetry is twofold: re-
sources need to be built for resource-poor languages while already existing rich
resources for rich languages are not usable. The cost of enforcing symmetry af-
fects the diﬀerent criteria discussed above (with diﬀerent degrees depending on
the complexity and approach of the MT system): lower retargetability, quality
and robustness and higher cost.
The MT approach presented in this dissertation, Generation-Heavy Hybrid
Machine Translation (GHMT), is an asymmetrical hybrid approach to MT that
has a higher degree of retargetability and robustness compared to other symmet-
rical approaches that either have a much higher cost (symbolic approaches) or a
much lower quality on new input genre (statistical approaches).
4

1.2
Generation Heavy Machine Translation
GHMT addresses the lack of symmetry by exploiting symbolic and statistical
TL resources in source-poor/target-rich language pairs. Expected SL resources
include a syntactic parser and a simple one-to-many translation dictionary. No
transfer rules or complex interlingual representations are used. Rich TL symbolic
resources such as word lexical semantics, categorial variations and subcategoriza-
tion frames are used to overgenerate multiple structural variations from a TL-
glossed syntactic dependency representation of SL sentences. This symbolic over-
generation, which accounts for possible translation divergences, is constrained by
multiple statistical TL models including surface n-grams and structural n-grams.
The source-target asymmetry of systems developed in this approach makes them
more easily retargetable (re-source-able) to new source languages (provided a SL
parser and translation dictionary).
The basic intuition of the approach parallels the experience of most language
learners whose lack of symmetrical knowledge impairs their ability to translate
into their newly learned language but does not hinder them as much when trans-
lating from the foreign language into their native tongue (where they are assisted
by rich resources).

Figure 1.1: Generation-Heavy Machine Translation
Figure 1.1 presents an overview of this approach. The input is a SL sentence
that is analyzed to produce a dependency tree, a representation describing the
5

syntactic relations among the words in the sentence. For example, in a Spanish-
English GHMT, the Spanish sentence Maria puso la mantequilla en el pan (Mary
put the butter on the bread) is analyzed as follows:
(1)
(puso :subject Maria
:object
(mantequilla :modifier la)
:modifier (en :object (pan :modifier el)))

2.1
Natural Language Generation
Natural Language Generation (NLG) is the process of constructing natural lan-
guage output from non-linguistic input [74]. These inputs can be sophisticated
structures of conceptual representations (meaning) or simple numeric contents
from some database that are useless to a human unless synthesized into natural
language form. In its broadest deﬁnition, NLG is process-independent of other
applications that are usually associated with it: machine translation, summariza-
tion, etc. Moreover, general NLG is concerned not only with the generation of
single sentences, but also with the generation of proper discourse (the relation-
ship between sentences). However, for the purpose of this work, NLG will refer
only to sentence-level generation in the MT context.
2.1.1
Components of Natural Language Generation
NLG has been traditionally divided into three successive tasks [124]:
• Content Determination: Map the initial input into a semantic form. Deter-
mine which components of the input should be realized and which should
be left for the human consumer to infer [113].
• Sentence Planning: Map conceptual structures into linguistic structures.
This includes: text structuring, lexical selection, syntactic selection, gen-
erating referring expressions, choosing grammatical relations and grouping
information into clauses and sentences [113, 124].
• Surface Generation (Realization): Map abstract linguistic speciﬁcation (syn-
tax and function words) into surface form that communicates this informa-
tion.
12

Generation in MT does not require a determination stage since the analysis
stage of an MT system serves as the content determiner for the generation side
[113]. This work will focus on two aspects of NLG: Lexical Selection and Realiza-
tion. Lexical selection is concerned with the choice of open class words to use to
generate a concept or a source-language word (e.g. eat, devour or ingest; kick or
give a kick). Realization includes many components such as mapping thematic
structure into syntactic roles, controlling syntactic paraphrasing and alternations,
preventing overgeneration, providing defaults, propagating agreement features,
selecting closed class words, providing linear precedence constraints among syn-
tactic constituents, inﬂecting open class words, and linearizing the syntactic tree
into a string of inﬂected words [43].
Approaches to Natural Language Generation
The diﬀerent systems for NLG and MT can be divided into three classes based on
how much emphasis in the system building process was put on hand-crafted rules
and solutions versus statistically collected information: symbolic approaches, sta-
tistical approaches and hybrid approaches.
2.1.2
Symbolic Approaches
Symbolic solutions to NLG depend on manually creating a system with rules
reﬂecting an understanding of the relationship between input and output. The
most basic symbolic approach to NLG, and by far the most common, is Template
Filling. Templates can be the best choice for very domain-speciﬁc interfaces such
as ATM machines or junk mail labels; however, they are extremely limited for
13

large-scale domain-independent (or even domain-speciﬁc) applications.1
As for the more realistic systems concerned with larger domains and robust
behavior, two major approaches to symbolic NLG are Systemic Grammar and
Functional Uniﬁcation Grammar [75].
Systemic Grammar is a part of Systemic Functional Linguistics, a theory of
language as a means of communicating semantics, interpersonal meaning, and
contextual eﬀect [62, 109]. Within this approach, a generation grammar is con-
structed as a system network of realization statements (grammar rules) that
specify mappings from input features to syntactic positions and surface forms.
Two widely used examples of this approach are the Penman generation system
[117] and RealPro [95].
Functional Uniﬁcation Grammar (FUG) uses uniﬁcation of input represen-
tations, called Functional Descriptions (FD), and a Uniﬁcation Grammar as its
main process for generation decision making [41, 42]. The original functional
uniﬁcation formalism was put forward by Kay [78].
As far as both approaches go, there is no consensus on the format of the
input. It ranges from propositional content (functional speciﬁcation) to gram-
matical/lexical speciﬁcations. The complexity of selection and linearization de-
pends on the type of input expected. Both approaches require multiple passes
through the grammar to make decisions at multiple levels. Both use defaults and
functional categorizations to handle input underspeciﬁty.

template ﬁlling is discussed in [125].

2.1.3
Statistical Approaches
Statistical natural language processing is concerned with the creation of computer
programs that can perform language-processing tasks by virtue of information
gathered from (typically large) corpora. Usually this information is in the form
of statistics, but it may be distilled into other forms, such as dictionary entries,
or various kinds of rules [18]. In the context of statistical machine translation
and statistical natural language generation, the object is to create a statistical
translation model, a mathematical model in which the process of input to output
translation is statistically modeled. Two examples of statistical approaches are
Candide (MT) and IBM Air Travel Reports (NLG).
Candide
Candide is a system for French-English Machine Translation developed at IBM
[14, 13, 11]. Candide uses a probability model of the translation process developed
using methods from information theory and statistics. Its main tools are (1) the
noisy channel model of communication, (2) a parametric probability translation
model, (3) a parametric probability language model and (4) an assortment of
numerical algorithms for training such models.2 The basic idea is that the channel
converts a sequence of words in one language (English) into another (French). The
channel operations are movements, duplications, and translations applied at each
word independently. Movement is conditioned only on word classes and positions
in the string. Duplication and translation are conditioned only on word identity.

IBM Air Travel Reports
This project was produced at IBM as part of the generation component of their
automated air travel agent. This work is interesting because it was the ﬁrst to
automatically learn mappings from semantics to surface realization by searching
for parameters and learning them automatically without using a hand crafted
grammar [122]. What made this possible is a limited domain with simple semantic
representation (simple set of attribute-value pairs) and an available semantically
annotated corpus.
Three models were implemented with varying degrees of complexity: NLG1
used template frequency; NLG2 used sequential n-gram model; and NLG3 used
dependency n-gram model. All these models ignore attribute values and train
only on template words and attribute names. The results of a comparative eval-
uation of these models showed that the sequential n-gram model fared better
than the baseline model but the dependency n-gram model improved accuracy
the most.
This system is at best a proof of concept on the use of statistical techniques for
Semantic-to-Surface mapping. It works well for domains with similar complexity
to air travel. However, the techniques used in it are useless for large-scale domain-
independent NLG applications especially with the lack of large scale semantically
annotated corpora.

Structural Language Models
Three types of statistical language models are distinguished [66]: (1) surface n-
gram language models, (2) non-lexicalized syntax (structural) models and (3)
lexicalized syntax models. The ﬁrst type has been used as part of various hybrid
implementations of NLG and MT tasks [82, 15, 88, 7, 60]. There has been a lot of
research using structural language models in parsing [24, 18, 130]. In comparison,
only one large-scale system built with NLG in mind uses a structural language
model [7]. The dependency n-gram model mentioned in the previous section uses
templates and focuses on travel reports only [122]. However, a recent study using
the Charniak parser [18] as a lexicalized syntax model for generation purposes
demonstrated the usefulness of these models in a variety of NLG tasks [66].
2.1.4
Hybrid Approaches
Hybrid NLG systems combine symbolic and statistical techniques to maximize the
advantages and minimize the disadvantages of these diﬀerent approaches. Most
statistically-oriented systems sacriﬁce depth or accuracy to breadth of coverage.
They are powerful but do not provide deep analyses and may make occasional
mistakes that are hard to ﬁx.
Statistical techniques work as well as they do
because one can get a great deal of leverage from relatively weak (and easily col-
lected) pieces of information. Also, at the shallower linguistic depths, computers
seem better at collecting linguistic phenomena than people are at thinking them
up [18]. However, linguistically blind statistical operations such as permutations
and fertility are also problematic in that they can create search spaces that are
large and yet exclude correct answers. To statistically train for these more com-
plex phenomena requires very large (the more the better) corpora annotated with
19

semantic, syntactic or bilingual information.
Symbolic systems are good for small domains. Large scale systems, however,
need large amounts of knowledge – lexical, grammatical and conceptual. Most
available knowledge bases are ﬁlled with gaps that compromise their robustness
in handling input with missing pieces of knowledge [82]. Therefore, restrictive
defaults or large sets of rules are needed. These defaults and rules must be created
manually and are restricted by domain. Also defaults can often be wrong and
disﬂuent (e.g. deﬁniteness defaulting to the and tense to past).
Hybrid systems try limiting the space of ambiguity resulting from linguisti-
cally blind statistical hypotheses using linguistically motivated rules. But they
depend on statistical knowledge to rank and make the ﬁnal choice. One simple
example of the advantage of using hybrid techniques is the generation of an ar-
ticle for an English noun when there is some ambiguity about deﬁniteness. So,
the system is faced with making a choice among four options: (the, a, an and φ).
A purely statistical solution would be to overgenerate all possibilities and
depend on a bigram model for picking up the best option. Bigrams are excellent
at selecting between ’a’ and ’an’ since the important context window for this
choice is always a bigram. However, bigrams are not good at picking deﬁnite
versus indeﬁnite articles. Moreover, they are most likely to pick φ because shorter
sentences are more probable! That can be an extremely bad choice.
A purely symbolic system can distinguish between ’a’ and ’an’ with special
modules that will have to encode knowledge about the phonological represen-
tation of the following noun or adjective. Deciding on deﬁnite versus indeﬁnite
articles can be done, but it requires a lot of rules. Most symbolic systems use de-
faults that may not always be appropriate in such cases. However, a simple rule
20

using number (singular/plural) and conﬂated deﬁniteness (e.g. proper nouns)
can easily decide when to pick φ versus an article.
A hybrid approach takes advantage of both: for example, using a rule to
decide between φ and the articles. Then, it generates all three articles in the
second case and leaves the ﬁnal word to the statistical model.

Oxygen Linearization
The Nitrogen grammar formalism is uniﬁcation based and it provides a small
number of tools to recast and linearize AMRs. There are several limitations to the
use of this formalism. For example, the grammar is interpreted which results in
ineﬃcient time/space use. Another limitation is that the tools provided are rather
simple transformations which causes the linearization grammars to be long and
complex. The linearization engine, Oxygen addresses these issues [57]. Oxygen is
an eﬃcient language-independent linearization engine. Linearization grammars
for Oxygen are written using Oxyl, a powerful linearization grammar description
language that has the power of a programming language with the focus on natural
language linearization.
Oxyl grammars are compiled into programs that run
independently.
The power of Oxyl is accomplished by providing recasting mechanisms for the
most common needs of a linearization grammar and also by allowing embedding
of code in a standard programming language (Lisp).
The Oxyl linearization
grammars are also simple, clear, concise and easily extendible. The simplicity
of Oxyl grammars is apparent when one considers issues of redundancy: the

handling of ambiguities at every phrase rule is hidden from the linearization
grammar designer and is treated only in the compiler and support library. For a
detailed presentation of Oxyl’s syntax, see [58].
Halogen: Forest-Based Statistical Sentence Generation
A later version of Nitrogen, Halogen, addresses the issue of time-space ineﬃciency
by compressing the search space into forests [87, 91]. In this approach, the input,
the AMR semantic representation, is mapped into alternative phrases that are
represented as packed sets of syntactic constituency trees or forests. Similar sub-
trees are linked symbolically instead of being repeated as in the lattice approach
used in Nitrogen and described in [82]. These forests are then statistically ranked
using a bottom-up dynamic programming algorithm and the statistics are bigram
or trigram based.
The advantages of forests include (1) more compactness than lattices; (2)
representation of hierarchical information relevant to syntactic representation;
and (3) more eﬃcient statistical ranking with respect to space and time use.
Although Halogen is more eﬃcient, it still is unable to guarantee picking the
optimal solution in longer sentences.
Uniﬁcation Based Glossing
Uniﬁcation Based Glossing is a hybrid MT approach that combines syntax-based
uniﬁcation style interpretation with statistical processing [64]. Whereas Nitrogen
expects source-language text to be parsed and composed into semantic represen-
tation before it can be generated; glossing computes a target language word lattice
directly from the source-language parse tree.
25

The symbolic component of this approach, the glosser, takes as input a con-
stituency tree of the source language. The glosser features transfer-like rules that
map source words and syntactic structures directly into target lattices. The re-
sulting word lattice is passed through a uni/bi/trigrams statistical extractor to
select the most ﬂuent paths.
FERGUS
FERGUS (Flexible Empiricist/Rationalist Generation Using Syntax) extends the
use of n-gram models with a tree-based statistical model and a traditional tree-
based syntactic grammar [8, 9, 7]. Modeling syntax in FERGUS is done using
an existing wide-coverage grammar of English, the XTAG grammar developed at
University of Pennsylvania [139]. XTAG is a Tree Adjoining Grammar (TAG)
[73] that has been extended to include lexicalization and uniﬁcation-based feature
structures [139]. In TAG, the elementary structures are phrase tree structures
that are composed using two operations: (1) substitution (which appends one
tree at the frontier of another) and (2) adjunction (which inserts a tree into the
middle of another). Morphosyntactic constraints such as subject-verb agreement
and subcategorization can be speciﬁed within the tree structure associated with
the lexical items. There is no distinction between the Grammar and Lexicon in
lexicalized TAGs.
FERGUS is composed of three modules: the Tree Chooser, the Unraveler, and
the Linear Precedence (LP) Chooser. The input to FERGUS is an unordered
dependency tree labeled only with lexemes but no syntactic annotation. First
the Tree Chooser uses a stochastic tree model to choose TAG trees for the nodes
in the input tree. Then the Unraveler uses the XTAG grammar to produce a
26

lattice of all possible linearizations that are compatible with the semi-speciﬁed
input tree and XTAG. Finally, the LP Chooser selects the most likely traversal of
this lattice given the language model. The tree model used by the Tree Chooser
and the trigram model used by the Linear Precedence Chooser are both based
on one million words from the Wall Street Journal corpus.
FERGUS is diﬀerent from Nitrogen in many ways: First of all, the input to
FERGUS is much shallower and closer to the target-language surface form than
Nitrogen’s semantic representation.
Although both systems are hybrids, they
have a diﬀerent balance of statistical and symbolic components with FERGUS
employing more power both statistically and symbolically: FERGUS constrains
its lattice of possible paths by symbolically handling agreement and other mor-
phology issues using the XTAG component. Nitrogen, on the other hand, uses
an explosively overgenerating simple grammar. Statistically, FERGUS uses both
a stochastic tree model and a trigram language model whereas Nitrogen uses
bigrams only.
Syntax Based Statistical Translation
The IBM statistical MT models [14, 11] did not statistically model structural
or syntactic aspects of language or the translation process. This might not be
important for languages that are structurally close (e.g. English and French)
but it is necessary for structurally divergent language pairs such as English and
Japanese.
The ﬁrst attempt to a syntax-based statistical translation model was achieved
by [140]. In this approach, the noisy channel model is used. But instead of con-
verting sequences of words, it accepts constituency parse trees as input. The
27

channel performs the following operations on each node in the tree: (1) reorder-
ing of child nodes, (2) insertion of extra words at each node, and (3) translation
of leaf words. Reordering helps translating languages with diﬀerent word order;
while insertion allows for adding post-nominal case marker particles (for Japanese
and Korean). These operations are very reminiscent of the IBM noisy channel
operations of movement, duplication and translation. Just like Candide, some
preprocessing of the input is needed (in addition to syntactic parsing): since
relative order is important for the channel operations, sub-constituents are ﬂat-
tened by combining projections of phrase heads to make the relative order more
accessible: For example, (NP (VB NP)) is ﬂattened into (NP VB NP).
The system uses three types of parameter tables to collect frequencies for
reordering, insertion and translation operations. Reordering probabilities of the
children of a node are assumed to be dependent on the sequence of child node
labels. Insertion probabilities combine position (right, left, nowhere) and word
insertion likelihood and are dependent on parent and node labels. Translation
probabilities specify the likelihood of a word translating into another. The ﬁnal
probability of a tree equals the product of the total reordering, insertion and
translation probabilities each of which is computed as the product of all sub-tree
probabilities.
Parameter estimation was done on a corpus of 2,000 pairs of English and
Japanese sentences using the EM algorithm [28].
An evaluation on the task
of generating Viterbi alignments compared this syntax-based model with IBM
model 5. The results show that the syntax-based model fared better than model
5 and had more perfect sentences in general.
Although simple and ambitious, syntax-based statistical MT has a major
28

bottleneck: the availability of lots of correctly analyzed parallel data for training.
2.2
Lexical Semantics
Lexical Semantics is the linguistic study of the relations among words and their
meanings as well as the internal structure of individual words [75]. This includes
the study of argument structure, thematic roles, selectional restrictions, seman-
tic relations, ontologies, lexical conceptual representations, event structure and
qualia structure [128]. This review will address four sub-topics of lexical seman-
tics that are relevant to the this research: Lexical Relations, Predicate Argument
Structure, Syntax-Semantics Linking, and the use of lexical semantics to handle
translation divergences.
2.2.1
Lexical Relations
Lexical relations describe relative relationships amongst words.
According to
[26], lexical relations are either hierarchical taxonomic relations (such as hyper-
nymy, hyponymy and entailments) or non-hierarchical congruence relations (such
as identity, overlap synonymy and antonymy). Lexical relations are reviewed here
to highlight their relationship to the categorial expansion and word class compo-
nents in GHMT. Next, three distinct approaches to lexical relations are discussed:
WordNet, lexical functions and verb classes.
WordNet
WordNet is the most well-developed and widely used lexical database of English
[45]. In WordNet, both types of lexical relations are speciﬁed among words with
29

the same part of speech (verbs, nouns, adjectives and adverbs). The most used
feature of WordNet is its synsets (synonym sets), where synonyms are grouped
together.
They are useful in representing meaning (instead of hard-to-deﬁne
primitives) and in expanding the generative space of a given word. WordNet has
been used by many researchers to build or extend knowledge bases such as the
SENSUS knowledge base [83] and the Lexical Conceptual Structure database [53].
Both were used for Machine Translation. In [7], WordNet synsets were used to
fake meaning ambiguity as part of system evaluation. In the scope of this work,
one criticism of WordNet is its lack of cross-categorial links, such as verb-noun
or noun-adjective relations.
Lexical Functions
Mel’ˇcuk’s Meaning-Text-Theory [109] deﬁnes a lexical combinatorial zone that
speciﬁes semantically related lexemes through Lexical Functions (LF), simply
deﬁned as a correspondence between a key lexical item and a set of lexical items.
There are two types: paradigmatic and syntagmatic [121]. Paradigmatic LFs as-
sociate a lexical item with related lexical items. The relation can be semantic or
syntactic. Semantic LFs include Synonym(calling) = vocation, Antonym(small) =
big, and Generic(fruit) = apple. Syntactic LFs include Derived-Noun(expand)=
expansion and Adjective(female) = feminine.
Syntagmatic LFs specify collo-
cations with a lexeme given a speciﬁed relationship.
For example, there is a
LF that returns the light verb that takes the LF’s key as its object: Light-
Verb(attention) = pay. Other LFs specify certain semantic associations such as
Intensify-Qualiﬁer(escape) = narrow and Degradation(milk) = sour.
Lexical Functions have been used in Machine Translation and Generation. For
30

example, [121] used LFs to determine coreference in text generation. For example,
in John was painting his walls. Suddenly (a,*the) ﬂy got stuck on (the,*a) brush,
there is an LF that relates brush (but not ﬂy) to paint and thus help determine
that one is deﬁnite but not the other. The use of LFs in [121] is a classic example
of symbolic techniques for natural language generation but it is beyond the scope
of this work (even to consider it in a hybrid context).
Verb Classes
Linguists have long noted the expression and interpretation of a verb’s arguments
is largely determined by its meaning. So, verb behavior can be used to understand
pertinent aspects of verb meaning [103]. For example, the Locative Alternation
(X V Y on Z and X V Z with Y) appears with the verb spray but not cover or
put although all three are semantically related:
(7)
X spray Y on Z
X put Y on Z
*X cover Y on Z
X spray Z with Y
*X put Z with Y
X cover Z with Y
There are two speciﬁc components of the meaning that are both present only
in spray; whereas the other two verbs have only one of the two [102]. These
components are: (1) causing stuﬀto go on a surface and (2) aﬀecting a surface
by applying stuﬀto it. Verbs that have both components are considered part
of the same verb class as spray (e.g. brush, drizzle, splash). They are expected
to allow both syntactic realizations. This is useful in analysis too. For example,
it drizzled all morning has a syntactic realization that is not shared with spray
verbs which means, correctly, its semantics is diﬀerent. In this case drizzle is part
of the Weather verb class such as rain and snow.
31

2.2.2
Predicate Argument Structure
Every predicate is associated with an argument structure that speciﬁes the num-
ber of arguments it requires [55]. Arguments are those elements which necessarily
must be involved in the action or state described by the predicate [128]. Adjuncts
are other elements that add to the meaning but are not necessarily involved. Ar-
guments headed by a preposition are called obliques. For example, in X give Y to
Z on A for B, give is a predicate, X, Y and Z are all arguments, Z is an oblique
and A and B are adjuncts.
There are many levels of complexity to the representation of predicate argu-
ment structure. The simplest form is the speciﬁcation of the arity of a predicate
without any semantic or syntactic information about the arguments [128]. For
example, give:3, think:2, sleep:1. Next in complexity is the speciﬁcation of the dif-
ferent relations between a predicate and its arguments. There are two approaches
that describe such relations: logical and thematic [128].
Logical Approach: Subcategorization Frames
The logical approach orders arguments with a rigid underlying meaning. This
order, also called a subcategorization frame, relates arguments across diﬀerent
syntactic variations and alternations. For example, clear has the subcategoriza-
tion frame (arg1 arg2): arg1 cleared arg2, arg2 clears easily, arg2 was cleared by
arg1, etc.
The problem with this approach is that it makes no generalizations about
the behavior of arguments across verbs.
Arg1 can only be understood in the
context of its verb head, e.g. the clearer, giver, etc. This information is very
important for cross-lingual applications where two verbs that are translations of
32

each other would deﬁne the meaning of their args diﬀerently, e.g. gustar (please)
and like. Another example involving obliques and diﬀerent alternations is the
English John(1) ﬁlled the glass(2) with water(3) but not *John(1) ﬁlled water(2?)
in the glass(3?) both of which are allowed in Korean.
Although this representation is limited, its simplicity makes it theory and
system-independent.
This is the reason why the PropBank project uses this
approach [115].
Thematic Approach: Thematic Roles
In the thematic approach, arguments are assigned thematic labels (also called
thematic roles, semantic roles or theta roles) that abstract away from the speciﬁcs
of deep semantic roles by generalizing over similar roles across classes of verbs
[75]. This is by far the most common approach in the ﬁeld to represent predicate-
argument structure.
There are a lot of variations with little agreement even
on terminology: e.g. case frames [46], theta-grids [131], and thematic relations
[67]. However, all thematic role lists are based on the same set of ideas: (1)
they are semantically unanalyzable, (2) they are deﬁned independently of the
verb (predicate), and (3) they are small in size [102]. Unlike subcategorization
frames, thematic roles have no signiﬁcant order. They are intended to describe
the relationships between arguments and predicates. For example, give has three
thematic roles associated with its three arguments: agent (giver), theme (given)
and goal (givee). Since theta roles are abstractions, their actual meaning is hard
to pin down.
For example, Agent is doer or causer, Patient is undergoer or
aﬀected, Theme is changing participant or undergoer and Goal is target or ﬁnal
state.
33

There are several problems with thematic role lists [102]. First, there lacks
a reliable diagnostic for determining the role and real meaning of an argument.
Syntactic positions (subject, object, and prepositions) are not helpful: For exam-
ple, a subject can be an agent John killed Paul, a theme Paul died, a goal Paul
received a bullet in the chest. Prepositions can limit the possibility of the the-
matic roles to be assigned but are not unambiguous. For example, the object of
to is never a source, but the object of with can be an instrument John killed Paul
with a gun, a theme John loaded the gun with bullets or a companion John left
with Paul. Second, determining the proper granularity level for the deﬁnition of a
role is hard. Cruse decomposes agent into four types (volitive, eﬀective, initiative
and agentive) to distinguish between the agents of encourage, avoid, facilitate
and conﬁrm [27], whereas Dowty suggests deﬁning roles by means of clusters of
features rather than criteria [40]. He deﬁnes a continuum of opposing features
between what he calls proto-agent and proto-patient. Properties contributing
to proto-agency include volition, sentience, causation and independent existence.
As for proto-patiency, they include change of state, causal aﬀectedness, relative
movement and dependent existence. Third, plain lists of thematic roles lack in-
ternal organization. For example, source, goal, route and direction theta roles
are related to each other in that they all involve a path: e.g. respectively come
to, come from, come through and come toward. However no organization exists
in the theta roles themselves that shows such a relationship.
Faced with these problems, some researchers concluded that thematic roles
should be avoided altogether [123]. However, other researchers attempted to deal
with some of these issues by creating more complex representations of predicate-
argument structures: Event Structures.
34

Event Structure
Event structures attempt to explain/deﬁne thematic roles of arguments by enrich-
ing predicates with a complex internal structure. There are three kinds of event
structure schemata: the Localist, Aspectual and Causal [102]. All of them share
distinction between semantic structure and semantic content in event structure
decomposition [56]. For example, Run (a race) and jog share the same struc-
ture but diﬀerent content, whereas Run (a race) and Run (a store) share the
same manner content while diﬀering in structure. Predicate event structures are
composed using two basic blocks of primitive predicates and constants. Prim-
itive predicates are used to deﬁne the semantic structure of the predicate and
constants are used to deﬁne its semantic content.
Localist Approach
The Localist approach claims that events involving motion
and location in space are central to the construction of all events [67, 68, 69, 70].
Jackendoﬀ’s Lexical Conceptual Structure (LCS) uses motion/location primitives
such as GO, BE, STAY, TO, TOWARD, FROM and also CAUSE and LET.
Jackendoﬀalso deﬁnes diﬀerent ﬁelds of action to diﬀerentiate between verbs
that would share similar localist structure such as identiﬁcational, possessional,
circumstantial, etc. For example, John went into the room and John changed into
a frog both share a similar structure of [GO John [TO John [AT room/frog]]], but
the ﬁrst is in the locational ﬁeld while the second is in the identiﬁcational ﬁeld.
The LCS approach uniﬁes substructures of diﬀerent verbs to give a consistent
account of what brings about a thematic role. For example, the rock fell and
john dropped the rock both share a substructure that describes the rock’s move-
ment: [LET john [GO rock [TOWARD down]]]. Dorr’s implementation of LCS
35

to handle machine translation divergences is discussed further in Section 2.2.5.
Aspectual Approach
In the aspectual approach, events are classiﬁed into 4
classes according to their lexical aspect or aktionsart: states, activities, achieve-
ment, and accomplishment. This approach can make predictions about the be-
havior of diﬀerent verbs and can help constructing better semantic interpretations
for ambiguous cases [102]. For example, accomplishments but not activities can
take an /in/ time adverbial: *Sam worked in an hour and Sam wrote the report
in an hour.
Causal Approach
The Causal approach decomposes events into simple non
branching causal chains: Sam broke the window = (1) Sam acted on window (2)
window changed state (3) window is in the result state broken. This approach
can capture subtle distinction between X load Y with Z and X load Z on Y [25].
The rest of this dissertation will focus on the localist approach using Dorr’s
implementation of Jackendoﬀ’s Lexical Conceptual Structures only. This is for
the practical reason that LCSes have been shown to be useful in solving Machine
Translation and generation problems (see Section 2.2.5) and they also have a lot
of resources available in the LCS formalism. Currently, there are no translation
or generation implementations using the other approaches.
2.2.3
Linking Semantics and Syntax
Linking semantics and syntax is a complex problem where any solid solution
must consistently explain a wide variety of syntax-semantic interaction phenom-
ena such as alternations and divergences (monolingual and cross-lingual). First,
36

alternations are variant ways in which the arguments of a predicate are realized
on the surface. A linking algorithm should be able to predict this many-to-many
mapping:
(8)
Johnagent opened the doortheme with a keyinstr
the doortheme opened
a keyinstr opened the doortheme
In the examples above, the theme maps into subject or object positions, and
agent and instrument both can map into a subject position. Note that systematic
transformations on the syntactic level such as passivization and wh-questions are
not included in the discussion of alternations.
Second, divergences are variant syntactic realizations of concepts with related
semantics. Monolingually, a semantics-syntax linking theory is preferred to be
consistent over semantically related predicates.
For example, Psych verbs al-
ternate the positions of their arguments John fears Death and Death frightens
John. Another example is verbs related on a deeper level through an exchange
relationship such as buy and sell. Cross-lingual (translation) divergences will be
discussed in detail in the next section.
The amount of research on the semantics-syntax interface is sizable yet with
little agreement. The following are the main dimensions of variations of diﬀerent
approaches in the ﬁeld.
• Representation: The ﬁrst dimension of variation of linking approaches
is the semantic and syntactic representations being linked to/from. These
representations vary in form and depth. For example, syntactic representa-
tions can be dependencies or constituencies (form) and they can be shallow
37

or deep with traces or without traces (depth). Here is a list of diﬀerent
semantic representations ordered from deep to shallow and complex to sim-
plistic: decompositional semantics [77, 123], lexical conceptual structure
[67, 68, 69, 70, 31], abstract meaning representation [88, 89], logical form,
semantic roles list, and lexico-syntactic structure [61].
• Thematic Relevance: In most of the approaches that link semantics to
syntax, out of all semantic information available, only thematic roles are
considered relevant to the linking process. Such approaches adhere to two
principles: Principle of Syntactic Relevance (only theta roles out of all se-
mantics are interesting for syntax) and Correlation Principle (theta roles of
a predicate determine syntactic conﬁgurations) [123]. A more speciﬁc state-
ment of these two principles is Baker’s Uniformity of Theta Assignment
Hypothesis(UTAH): Identical thematic relationships between items are rep-
resented by identical structural relationships between those items a the level
of d-structure [6]. Two examples of thematic-based linking are Fillmore’s
Case theory [46, 47] and Chomsky’s Government Binding Theory [20].
Alternatively, Ravin claims that the principles of Syntactic Relevance and
Correlation are too severe and fail to explain a lot of syntactic phenom-
ena. She states that all theories using thematic roles exhibit a variety of
syntactic problems that are addressed by implicitly or explicitly depending
on decompositional analysis of meaning that ends up rendering thematic
roles as mere labels of superﬂuous value [123]. Her proposed approach is
a non-thematic model based on Katz’s theory of semantic decomposition
[77]. The approach uses a two level representation model, one of syntax
and one of semantics.
38

• Mapping Scheme: The type and complexity of a mapping scheme depend
heavily on the starting semantic/lexico-syntactic representation. Mapping
schemes are classiﬁed on two dimensions: modularity (direct versus medi-
ated) and preserving constraint type (prominence versus equivalence-class)
[102] .
Direct mapping approaches are simple reassignments of thematic or struc-
tural information.
For example, agents are mapped into subjects and
themes into objects.
Mediated mapping approaches can be broken into
two classes: thematic-hierarchy-based and intermediate-role-based. A the-
matic hierarchy is a ranking of possible semantic roles chosen because it
ﬁgures in the statement of linguistic generalizations [102].
Intermediate role mapping approaches map ﬁner semantic roles into coarser
ones that are easier to map to syntax. For example, in role and reference
grammar the more semantic roles are mapped using a thematic hierarchy
into two macro roles: Actor and Undergoer which map directly into subject
and object [133]. Dowty’s feature-based proto-roles are another example of
intermediate-role mapping. Proto-agent and proto-them map directly to
subject and object positions.
Prominence preserving mappings assume a hierarchy of prominence in syn-
tax and semantics that must be preserved. For example, agents are more
prominent than themes semantically and therefore are linked to subjects
which are more prominent than objects on the syntactic level.
Promi-
nence of arguments can be explained in predicate structural terms in of a
relativized UTAH (Identical relationships are represented by identical rela-
tive hierarchical relations between items at D-structure) [93]. The simplest
39

example of this approach is Thematic Hierarchies. Equivalence class pre-
serving mappings assume that each distinct class of arguments is associated
with a distinct syntactic realization. Equivalence classes can be structural
conﬁgurations (e.g. leftmost child of VP is subject) or semantic features
(e.g. agent is subject). The diﬀerence between the prominence and equiv-
alence constraints is what they consider important: relation of argument
to verb individually (equivalence class) or the whole hierarchical structure
(prominence).
• Mapping Direction Linking from syntax to semantics is more compli-
cated than linking from semantics to syntax since the former maps from a
shallower representation to deeper one. The two processes are asymmetri-
cal.
There is a tradeoﬀamong the granularity of the representation, the complexity
of the mapping scheme, and the coverage of linguistic phenomena. The deeper a
representation is the more phenomena it can unify and explain, but at the cost
of a more complex mapping. For example, deeper semantic representations that
obviate the need for thematic roles have a ﬁner granularity that is powerful but
at a much higher complexity cost. Similarly, shallower representations cannot be
consistent in explaining the diversity of linguistic phenomena, but the mapping
schemes are very simple.
2.2.4
Lexical Conceptual Structure
Lexical Conceptual Structure (LCS) is a compositional abstraction with language-
independent properties that transcend structural idiosyncrasies. LCS has been
40

formulated by Dorr [31, 32] based on work by Jackendoﬀ[69, 70, 71]. This repre-
sentation has been used as the interlingua of several projects such as UNITRAN
[30] and MILT [33].
Formally, an LCS is a directed graph with a root. Each node is associated
with certain information, including a type, a primitive and a ﬁeld. The type of
an LCS node is one of Event, State, Path, Manner, Property or Thing. There are
two general classes of primitives: closed class (also called structural primitives,
e.g., cause, go, be, to) and open class primitives (also called constants, e.g.,
john+, reduce+ed, jog+ingly). Suﬃxes such as +, +ed, +ingly are markers
of open class primitives, signaling also the type of the primitive (thing, property,
event, etc.). The structural primitive go, denoting generalized movement, is dis-
tinguished from the constant go+ingly: the ﬁrst appears in many lexical entries
but the second appears only in speciﬁc lexical entries such as the one for the
English verb “go”. Examples of ﬁelds include Locational, Possessional, and
Identificational. Structurally, an LCS node has zero or more LCS children.
There are three ways a child node relates to its parent: as a subject (maximally
one), as an argument, or as a modiﬁer.
An LCS captures the semantics of a lexical item through a combination of
semantic structure (speciﬁed by the shape of the graph and its structural primi-
tives and ﬁelds) and semantic content (speciﬁed through constants). The seman-
tic structure of a verb is something the verb shares with a semantic verb class
whereas the content is speciﬁc to the verb itself. For example, all the verbs in
the semantic class of “Run” verbs (e.g., run, jog, walk, zigzag, jump, roll, etc.)
have the same semantic structure but vary in their semantic content. The LCS
Verb Database (LVD) [35] extends the classiﬁcation in English Verb Classes and
41

Alternations (EVCA) [101] by reﬁning the class divisions and deﬁning the un-
derlying meaning components of each class in the LCS representation. LVD also
provides a relation between Levin’s classes and both thematic role information
and hand-tagged WordNet synset numbers.

Lexical Semantics and Translation Divergences
Since translation diver-
gences require a combination of lexical and structural manipulations, they are
traditionally handled minimally through the use of transfer rules [63, 94].
A
pure transfer approach is a brute force attempt to manually encode all transla-
tion divergences in a transfer lexicon [37]. Very large parsed and aligned bilingual
52

corpora have also been used to automatically extract transfer rules [96, 136]. This
approach depends on the availability of such resources, which are very scarce.
Alternatively, more linguistically-sophisticated techniques that use lexical se-
mantic knowledge to detect and handle divergences have been developed. One
approach uses Jackendoﬀ’s Lexical Semantic Structure (LCS) [69, 70] as an in-
terlingua [31]. LCS is a compositional abstraction with language-independent
properties that transcend structural idiosyncrasies by providing a granularity of
representation much ﬁner than syntactic representation.
As an example, the
Spanish sentence Juan cruza el r´ıo nadando can be “composed” as the following
LCS using a Spanish LCS lexicon as part of the interlingual analysis:
(19)

[event GO JOHN [path ACROSS JOHN [position AT JOHN RIVER]]]
[manner SWIM+INGLY]]
In the generation phase, this same LCS is “decomposed” using English LCS
lexicon entries to yield John swam across the river.
Another approach enriches lexico-structural transfer at Mel’ˇcuk’s Deep Syn-
tactic Structure (DSyntS) level [109] with cross-linguistic lexical semantic fea-
tures [112]. Transfer lexicon rules are written to capture generalizations across
the language pair instead of addressing speciﬁc paired instances. As an example,
the following transfer rule can be used to handle the head swapping divergence
discussed in the last example.

2.3
Evaluation
The evaluation of machine translation and natural language generation systems
is more of an art than a science. Evaluation of generation systems is diﬃcult,
because the ultimate criteria is translation quality, which can, itself, be diﬃcult to
judge. Moreover, it can be hard to attribute speciﬁc deﬁcits to the analysis phase,
the lexical resources, or the generation system proper. A wide range of metrics
and techniques have been developed over the last ﬁfty years to assess ‘how good’
a system is. Evaluation schemas vary in their focus from addressing the system’s
interface to system scalability, faithfulness, space/time complexity, etc. Another
dimension of variation is human versus automatic evaluation. Fully automatic
evaluation, a task that is AI-complete (i.e., encompassing all components of any
system that would be deemed “intelligent”), is the ultimate goal in the ﬁeld.7
Evaluation is complex, labor-intensive, time-consuming and less interesting
than doing research. But it is a very important irreplaceable component of any
healthy research practice [23]. In the area of machine translation, it has been
pointed out that as much has been written about MT evaluation as about MT
7For an excellent survey of machine translation evaluation metrics and techniques, see [65].
54

itself [138]. The following three sections discuss general evaluation types, MT
evaluation metrics, and translation quality evaluation metrics.
2.3.1
Evaluation Types
Three kinds of evaluations are deﬁned based on appropriateness for diﬀerent goals
[23]:
• Adequacy Evaluation (aka Evaluation proper): The goal of this kind of
evaluation is to determine the ﬁtness of a system for a purpose, i.e., does
it do the job required?, how well?, at what cost? Adequacy evaluations are
focused on the users and their needs.
• Diagnostic Evaluation (aka Diagnosis proper): The goal of this kind of
evaluation is to examine the system’s performance proﬁle with respect to
a space of possible inputs. To that extent, diagnostic evaluations require
the creation of a test suite that enumerates all the elementary linguistic
phenomena addressed in the input domain.
Once the test suite is con-
structed, comparisons of two generations of the same system can be done
automatically. This type of Diagnostic evaluation is called Regression test-
ing. Diagnostic evaluations are typically done by/for system developers.
• Performance Evaluation (aka Assessment, Progress Evaluation, Summari-
tive evaluation or Technology Evaluation): The goal of this kind of evalu-
ation is to measure the systems performance in one or more speciﬁc areas.
Performance evaluations are typically done for developers and Research and
Development program managers.
55

A fourth type of evaluation, Formative Evaluation, is a hybrid of adequacy
and performance evaluations. In formative evaluations, system design is guided
by the needs of the users. During system development, user trials of prototypes
can help guide pinpoint directions for research and development. Other evalu-
ation type distinctions are Black-box/Glass-box and intrinsic/extrinsic evalua-
tions. Black-box evaluations are interested in the behavior of the system as a
whole, while Glass-box evaluations are component-wise evaluations. Within a
Glass-box evaluation, one can distinguish between intrinsic and extrinsic evalua-
tions: intrinsic evaluations focus on how a particular component is behaving in its
own terms, while extrinsic evaluations focus on how that component contributes
to the performance of the whole system.
To evaluate a system, it is important to distinguish between three interde-
pendent levels of speciﬁcity [23]:
• Criterion: what is the evaluation interested in? Speed, error rate, precision,
recall, ﬂuency, accuracy, etc.
• Measure: which speciﬁc property of system performance should be reported
to get the chosen criterion? For example, for speed report processes per
second, for precision report the number of retrieved relevant data to all
retrieved data, for recall report the number of retrieved relevant data to all
relevant data, etc.
• Method: how to determine the appropriate value for a given measure and
a given system. For example, post-analytic measurement of the system’s
behavior over some benchmark task.
56

2.3.2
MT Evaluation Metrics
There are three categories of MT evaluation metrics: system-based, text-based
and cost-based [21].
System-based Evaluation Metrics
System-based metrics count internal resources: size of lexicon, number of gram-
mar rules, etc. These metrics are easy to measure although they are not compa-
rable across systems. Their value is questionable since they are not necessarily
related to utility.
In [43], a list of qualitative criteria that ﬁt within this class of metrics was
provided to compare diﬀerent realization systems.
It included comprehensive
coverage, prevention of overgeneration, encapsulation of syntactic knowledge, ex-
pecting regular input, allowing partially canned input, compact input, extensi-
bility, eﬃciency, robustness, user-friendliness, and versatility.
Text-based Evaluation Metrics
Text-based metrics can be divided into sentence-based and comprehensibility-
based.
Sentence-based metrics examine the quality of single sentences out of
context. These metrics include Accuracy, Fluency, Coherence, etc. Typically,
subjects evaluating sentences are given a description of the metric with examples
and are asked to rate the sentences on an x-point scale. These scales range from
3-point to 100-point. Comprehensibility metrics measure the comprehension or
informativeness of a complete text composed of several sentences. The subjects
are typically given questionnaires related to the processed text. Text-based met-
rics are much more related to utility than system-based metrics, but they are
57

also much harder to measure. There are some automatic text-based evaluation
metrics that measure the amount of post-editing needed for a sentence given a
gold standard. These are variations on edit-distance, i.e., the number of dele-
tions, additions or modiﬁcations measured by words or keystrokes per page or
sentence. These techniques are not necessarily related to utility; however, it was
recently shown that the smarter tree-based edit distance might actually correlate
better to human judgment [7].
Cost-based Evaluation Metrics
Cost-based metrics evaluate a system on how much money/time it saves/costs
per unit of text, say a page.
These are secondary metrics since they depend
on other metrics to evaluate how much post/pre-processing is necessary for a
commercially functional system.
2.3.3

Automatic Text Quality Evaluation Metrics
Automatic text quality evaluation metrics allow us to evaluate without human
intervention, objectively and automatically. A common goal of such metrics is
to show that they correlate to human judgment. There are two types of these
metrics: Sentence Error Rate (or String Error Rate) and Word Error Rate [44].
Both are measured relative to some gold standard reference. Sentence error rate
checks whether the whole generated sentence matches the reference sentence fully
or not. It is a coarse metric since no partial credit for partially correct answers
is given. Word error rate is much more commonly used. There are many ways
to calculate this metric which tries to capture how diﬀerent a generated string
is from the reference string. Two common metrics are string edit distance and
n-gram precision variation.

N-gram precision variation compares the
generated string and the reference string by taking the ratio of n-gram sequences
in the generated string that appear in the reference string to the total number
of n-gram sequences in the generated string [116]. The most recent automatic
evaluation system using n-gram precision is Bleu from IBM.
Bleu (BiLingual Evaluation Understudy) is a method for MT evaluation that
is quick, inexpensive and language independent and most importantly correlates
highly with human evaluation [116].
Bleu’s major contribution is the use of

certain domains or certain linguistic phenomena. This feature also makes Bleu
more robust and consistent compared to the techniques presented earlier.

Chapter 3
Generation-Heavy Hybrid Machine Translation
The diﬀerent approaches to Machine Translation (MT) whether symbolic, statis-
tical or hybrids to varying degrees share an assumption of symmetry between
analysis and generation in the algorithms, the resources or both.
Symbolic
approaches utilizing transfer or interlingua require symmetrical amounts of re-
sources and algorithms. Statistical approaches demand large parallel corpora to
achieve any good results. The research presented here does not question the the-
oretical validity of symmetrical approaches, but proposes an alternative approach
for translation between language pairs with diﬀerent densities of resources. Here,
the intent is to translate from a low density language (with minimally a parser)
to a high density language (with ample resources of all kinds) using a simple
translation lexicon that does not address complex structural variations between
the two languages.
This chapter presents an approach to MT that addresses the lack of symmetry
by exploiting symbolic and statistical target-language (TL) resources in source-
poor/target-rich language pairs. This approach is called Generation-Heavy Hy-
brid Machine Translation (GHMT). Expected source-language (SL) resources in-
clude a syntactic parser and a simple one-to-many translation dictionary. No
68

transfer rules or complex interlingual representations are used. Rich TL symbolic
resources such as word lexical semantics, categorial variations and subcategoriza-
tion frames are used to overgenerate multiple structural variations from a TL-
glossed syntactic dependency representation of SL sentences. This symbolic over-
generation, which accounts for possible translation divergences, is constrained by
multiple statistical TL models including surface n-grams and structural n-grams.
The source-target asymmetry of systems developed in this approach makes them
more easily retargetable (re-source-able) to new SLs (provided a parser and trans-
lation lexicon).
The next section motivates this hybrid approach to translation divergences
and relates it to previous research. Then, an overview of GHMT is followed by
detailed discussion of its diﬀerent components.
3.1
A Hybrid View of Translation Divergences
LCS-based MT provides a framework for handling both monolingual alternations
and translation divergences using the LCS representation as an Interlingua and
constraining generation options by setting diﬀerent parameters for each language.
A hybrid view of divergences would consider them uncommon alternations: vari-
ations that are symbolically compositionally determinable but whose probability
of occurrence is idiosyncratic and thus can only be statistically modeled. For
example, the Spanish tener hambre (have hunger) and the English to be hungry
are translation divergences even though to have hunger is correct English. The
diﬀerence between to be hungry and to have hunger is that the former is more
common than the latter. While lexical semantic knowledge can help link the two
as possible variants, only a statistical model can determine which to pick as the
69

most likely translation. Diﬀerent statistical models within the same language
and other genre-speciﬁc constraints can participate in selecting the best choice in
translation. For example, a model of English poetry with restrictions on rhyme
would be expected to produce diﬀerent choices from a Wall Street Journal model.
The approach presented here is closely related to work on Hybrid Natural
Language Generation [82, 88, 87] and it draws heavily on resources from research
in Lexical Conceptual Structure (LCS) based MT [31, 35, 34, 60]. The idea is
to combine symbolic and statistical knowledge in generation through a two step
process: (1) Symbolic Overgeneration followed by (2) Statistical Extraction (or
Ranking). The hybrid approach has been used primarily for lexical choice (includ-
ing morphology and tense selection) [90, 7] and for linearization from semantic
representation [88] or from shallow unlabeled dependencies [8].
GHMT extends the hybrid approach to handle translation divergences with-
out the use of a deeper interlingual representation or transfer rules.
This is
accomplished by extending the symbolic overgeneration component to include
structural and categorial expansion of the SL lexico-structural representation us-
ing lexical semantic knowledge from the TL (derived from LCS databases). The
overgeneration is constrained by linguistically motivated rules that utilize TL
lexical semantics and subcategorization frames and are independent of SL prefer-
ences. The overgenerated lexico-structural combinations are then ranked by the
statistical ranking component.1

3.2.1
Symbolic Components
GHMT utilizes three major TL resources. First, the word-class lexicon deﬁnes
verbs and prepositions in terms of their subcategorization frames and lexical
conceptual primitives.
A single verb or preposition can have multiple entries

for each of its senses. For example, among other entries, run1, as in (Johnagent

together. So are crossV and acrossP; and stabV and stabN. Finally, the syntactic-
thematic linking map relates syntactic relations (such as subject and object) and
prepositions to the thematic roles they can assign. For example, while a subject
can take on just about any thematic role, an indirect object is typically a goal,
source or benefactor. Prepositions can be more speciﬁc. For example, toward
typically marks a location or a goal, but never a source.
3.2.2
Statistical Components
In GHMT, a surface n-gram model is used together with a structural n-gram
model, which is a new type of language model not accounted for in the three-way
distinction presented earlier [66] (Section 2.1.3). A structural n-gram language
model characterizes the relationship between words in a dependency representa-
tion of a sentence without taking into account the overall structure at the phrase
level. Such a model is not useful for parsing purposes since the goal of parsing
is to determine an overall structure. However, in generation, where the structure
is already determined (albeit ambiguously), this model is very useful for making
lexical selection choices even though it is blind to the structural relation (e.g.
subject versus object).
In addition, it is less complex and less sparse than a
lexicalized syntax language model.

mentational details will be described in chapter 4.

3.3
Analysis
The analysis component of a GHMT system is a SL deep syntactic dependency
parser. Syntactic dependencies transcend SL syntactic idiosyncrasies such as SL
surface word order without delving into semantic analysis and its challenges.
Dependencies are, therefore, deeper representations (and thus more language-
independent) than constituencies yet they are shallower than semantic represen-
tations. The variety of syntactic dependency used normalizes over syntactic phe-
nomena such as passivization, morphological variations including de-agglutination
(Turkish) or segmentation (Chinese), and case/order-driven resolution of syntac-
tic relations (subject-hood, object-hood,etc.). These are purely SL issues that
should be dealt with before the translation step. Morphological analysis is also a
simplifying factor for the level of complexity of the translation lexicon since in-
ﬂectional variants of a single word are numerous in some languages (e.g. Arabic
or Turkish).
Verbs in the SL that violate the Universal Thematic Hierarchy (such as Psych
verbs) need to be thematically normalized (or in other words, de-externalized: the
subject and object are reversed). Analysis is the only logical place for handling
thematic divergence since it is dependent on idiosyncratic SL features. Normal-
ization allows such verbs to be translated correctly into TL verbs that follow or
violate the Universal Thematic Hierarchy.
3.3.1
Why Parse at All?
In statistical MT models that view the SL sentence as a sequences of words
with no constituent dependence, relations among these words are limited to a
contextual window that varies from a few words to the whole sentence. With
73

the smaller contextual window, the search space is too restricted, which excludes
valid outputs; and with the larger full-sentence context, the space is so large that
it becomes hard to search [85].
Eﬃciency and correctness of operations such as lexical selection and lineariza-
tion are aﬀected by the size of the space of possibilities. For example, statistical
techniques ignore the fact that linguistically dependent structures move together
in translation: the adjectives modifying a noun are semantically dependent on
the noun and that fact is reﬂected in the dependency relationship between the
adjectives and their head noun. A brute-force statistical approach to lineariza-
tion overgenerates all possible permutations of the TL sentence and orders them
using n-gram models which can only capture local context likelihood.
With dependency parsing, the number of permutations is exponentially smaller
because it is restricted to a parent and its children (but not the grand-children).
Moreover, the process of lexical selection can be dependent on long-distance de-
pendencies which are hard to capture in a ﬂat sentence structure. A syntactic
parse is necessary for reducing the space of possibilities without compromising
correctness (compare with taking all possible permutations into consideration).
Parsing ambiguity is a problem of course, but it does not compare to the
complexity of ambiguity in ﬂat sentences. The latter is actually an upper bound
on ambiguity in syntactic parsing: the worst parse possible has all the words in a
sentences as sister nodes. In other words, a parse (shallow or deep) always does
better compared to no parsing. The parsing process helps reduce the space of pos-
sibilities through deﬁning smaller structural dependencies and also by resolving
categorial ambiguities such as part of speech.
Although parsing is a relatively source-heavy module, new developments in
74

the ﬁeld of statistical parsing allow the creation of parsers using a parallel corpus
of a poor language and a rich language (see section 4.4 for more details).

3.5.1
Restructuring
The goal of this step is to handle the cases when a SL word is translated into
a word with a diﬀerent POS that violates the wellformedness of the input. For
example, if a SL verb dominating another verb is translated into an adverb or
auxiliary, then the dependency will be restructured such that the dominated
verb is promoted and the auxiliary or adverb is demoted. In Spanish-English
translation, this step is where verbs like deber (should/owe), poder (can/be able)
and soler (usually/tend) are handled.
3.5.2
Thematic Linking
The generation algorithm uses thematic dependencies as an internal representa-
tion. A thematic dependency is shallower than a typical interlingua but deeper
and more language-independent than a syntactic-dependency. It is important to
note that the SL analysis comes before the mapping into the thematic represen-
tation. The thematic linking step is done in the generation process using the TL
77

resources only. Therefore, it is a loose linking algorithm that is constrained by the
thematic grids of the predicates in the TL (verbs and prepositions). The output
of this phase is a thematic dependency in which the edges linking children to
parents are marked with thematic roles (and modiﬁers) together with syntactic
relations.
The goals of this phase are many:
• Reduction of ambiguity: since each verb can have multiple verb class mem-
berships (some of which have multiple thematic grids), this step reduces
the verb/verb-class/grid possibilities.
• Normalization: this step normalizes over structural variation and thus ap-
proaches a solution to the structural and thematic divergences on a thematic
level.2
• Accurate thematic assignment: this is essential for handling structural vari-
ations.
The basic idea here is to use prepositions as syntactic relations, or case mark-
ers, that constrain the option of thematic roles that can be assigned to the prepo-
sitions’ objects. Some limitation of ambiguity is possible here using the number
and nature (obligatory, optional) of the thematic roles determined by the verb
thematic grid together with additional constraints which will be discussed in fur-
ther detail in section 5.4. Prepositions used as syntactic relations are kept in the
representation because some contain semantic information, such as direction of
motion, that is used in the next step of structural expansion.

3.5.3
Structural Expansion
This step overgenerates alternative structural conﬁgurations of the input’s the-
matic dependency. There are three concepts relevant to this step.
1. Basic Operations
There are two basic types of operations applied here: Conﬂation and Inﬂa-
tion. Each type has two forms: Node-Node and Node-Relation. Nodes refer
to words in a thematic dependency and Relations refer to the prepositions
labeling the edges that connect these words.
• Node-Node Conﬂation occurs when the semantic content of two nodes
conﬂates into one node as in give stab becoming stab.
• Node-Relation Conﬂation occurs when the semantic content of a node
and the label marking one of its relations to a child node conﬂate as
in go across X becoming cross X.
• Node-Node Inﬂation occurs when the semantic content of a node is
split over two nodes as in stab becoming give stab.
• Node-Relation Inﬂation occurs when the semantic content of a node
is split between itself and the label marking one of its relations to a
child node as in cross X becoming go across X.
Whereas Node-Node operations change the structure of a thematic depen-
dency, Node-Relation operations only modify the content of its nodes and
79

edges. However, this change in content implies a structural change at the
syntactic level. This change is not realized until the next step of Syntactic
Assignment.
Lexical-semantic information from the verb class lexicon, such as theta grids
and lexical conceptual primitives, is used to determine the “conﬂatability”
and “inﬂatability” of combinations of nodes and relations in the thematic
dependencies.
2. Semantic Granularity
This is the level of semantic reﬁneness of the underlying lexical semantics
model. For example, when using LCS as the underlying model, conﬂations
such as (put+butter →butter) are possible but not (drink+fast →guzzle).
The former is de/composable in LCS but the latter is not. In principle,
the GHMT approach is independent of the lexical semantic representa-
tion.
Other implementations of GHMT using diﬀerent lexical semantic
models will have diﬀerent degrees of granularity and thus expressibility and
structural expansion-ability. Idiomatic expressions are, by deﬁnition, non-
composable and thus cannot be addressed by a lexical semantics approach
of any granularity.
3. Degree of Expansion
The basic operations can be applied compositionally to create more complex
structural variations. The degree of expansion is measured by the number
of operations applied. It is restricted by the system’s semantic granularity
which determines the maximum number of basic operations that can take
place. When using LCS, only ﬁrst and second degree expansion is possible.

An Operational View of Divergences
The constrained overgeneration approach, as applied in GHMT for handling
translation divergences, presents a diﬀerent view from the previous lexicon-based
parameter-driven divergence classiﬁcation ﬁrst deﬁned by Dorr [31].
The op-
erational view implemented in this dissertation reduces translation divergence
types/classes to four simple compositional operations:
• Categorial Variation: This is a change of the POS of a word.
The re-
source associated with this operation is the categorial variation database.
The modules associated with this operation are structural expansion and
syntactic assignment. In terms of graph (dependency tree) modiﬁcation,
it only applies to node labels. This operation is the most common of all
operations since it is applied in conjunction with other operations most of
the time. For example, a conﬂation always includes a categorial variation.
• Case Variation: This is a change to the relation between two words. The
resources associated with it are the thematic-syntactic linking map and
the word-class lexicon.
The modules associated with case variation are
input normalization, restructuring, thematic linking, node-relation conﬂa-
tion/inﬂation and syntactic assignment. The case variation operation com-
bines the thematic divergence and the structural divergence since both can
be seen as a change in assignment of a syntactic case to a thematic role. In
83

terms of graph modiﬁcation, it only applies to edge labels.
• Conﬂation: This is a change where two words are combined into one. The
resource associated with this operation is the word-class lexicon. The mod-
ule associated with this operation is structural expansion. Conﬂation always
occurs with categorial variation. In terms of graph modiﬁcation, conﬂation
is the joining of two nodes into one.
• Inﬂation: This is a change where one word is split into two. The resource
associated with this operation is the word-class lexicon. The module as-
sociated with this operation is structural expansion.
In terms of graph
modiﬁcation, inﬂation is the splitting of one node into two.
These divergence operations are applied after word translation and are fully
driven by target-language resources. A lexical-semantic framework is necessary
to deﬁne how some of these operations are conditioned. In the implementation
presented in this dissertation, the lexical-semantic frame work is LCS. But this is
not an inherent restriction on these operations, only an implementational choice.
The granularity of the LCS representation sets a limit on the number of operations
that can be applied. For example, no more than two inﬂation operations can be
applied on a verb since the LCS representation doesn’t go beyond two levels
of decomposition: X butter Y can be represented in LCS as [CAUSE X [GO
BUTTER [TO [AT Y]]]] which maximally allows inﬂations such as X made the
butter go on Y.

result from applying multiple operations in new ways. For example, the following
multiple head-swap case is predicted even within the LCS framework:
(27) John spent December across Europe
John went in December across Europe (Inﬂation, Categorial, Case)
John crossed Europe in December (Conﬂation, Categorial, Case)
Using a diﬀerent lexical-semantic framework with ﬁner granularity such as the
large-scale CYC knowledge base [100, 99] is likely to predict even more complex
cases.
The divergence operations are reminiscent of the noisy channel operations ap-
85

plied in Yamada’s syntax-based statistical MT (SSMT) [140]: child-reordering,
node-insertion and node-translation (see discussion in Section 2.1.4). Divergence
operations are diﬀerent from Yamada’s operations in many ways: (1) they apply
to dependencies not constituents; (2) they operate on a deeper level that is inde-
pendent of surface order (e.g. no child-reordering); and (3) they are generation-
driven not translation-driven. The fact that the GHMT approach and the SSMT
approach are on opposite end of the hybridity continuum (GHMT more symbolic
than statistical and SSMT more statistical than symbolic) is not an inherent re-
quirement of the implementation of the four divergence operations. It is possible
to deﬁne these operations over a noisy channel model that transforms TL depen-
dencies into SL dependencies. An annotated parallel corpus would be required
for training in this approach, although the resulting model would fall outside of
the generation-heavy spirit.

proach in a language independent manner. The next chapter focuses on imple-
mentation issues of an instantiation of GHMT for Spanish-English translation,
Matador.

2. Reﬂexive Clitic “se”
The Spanish reﬂexive clitic “se” is used to indicate a variety of phenomena
such as passivization, or emphasis [49, 105]. In some cases, the meaning is
changed in a pragmatic non-compositional manner. For example acordar
is to agree but acordarse is to remember. Another example is acusar to
accuse and acusarse to confess. Cases where meaning change occurs are
indicated in the translation lexicon as separate entries from basic verbs.
In Connexor’s output, the reﬂexive clitic “se” is always separated from its
verb and in some cases, it is not attached at all. If the reﬂexive “se” is
not attached to anything, it is forced to be attached to the closest Verb.
Currently, the reﬂexive clitic is treated in one of two ways depending on
whether the inﬁnitive form of the verb appears in the translation dictionary

with the reﬂexive clitic attached.
• If the reﬂexive form of the verb appears, the clitic is attached to the
verb.
• Otherwise, the clitic is deleted and the feature (:voice passive) is

3. Prepositional Phrases (PP)
The government relation between prepositions and their objects is reestab-
lished: prepositions are to dominate their objects rather than modifying
them. All PPs are related to their parents as :mod.
4. Coordinating Conjunctions
Coordinating conjunction structures are modiﬁed to be consistent with the
general input speciﬁcation described in Section 4.1.
5. Punctuation Handling
Structures involving punctuation are modiﬁed to be consistent with the
general input speciﬁcation described in Section 4.1. This includes reattach-

the largest or smallest source-order position. The :placement feature is set
to pre or post accordingly. An exception is PX nodes placed under *root*
where they get the feature (:placement rel).

In pro-drop languages such as Spanish, the subject pronoun of a verb can
be dropped but is indicated in the morphology of the verb. Every verb must
have a subject. So, a place holder for the pro-dropped subject is added.

• If the verb has no subject and it is not a child of *root*, then a subject

• If a verb has the feature (:voice passive), then
(a) Change :subj to :obj and :obj to :obj2
(b) Add *pro* as :subj

4.3.2
The Translation Algorithm
Translation is accomplished through a simple word replacement algorithm. Match-
ing is done using the word and POS. If the word-POS pair is not available, the
translation algorithm attempts to back oﬀto a union of all the translations of the
word for all available parts of speech. If this fails, the ﬁnal back-oﬀis invoked and
the unmatched word is subjected to regular expressions modiﬁcations speciﬁed
in the OTHERWISE rules in the order they appear in.
There are two additional features of the translation algorithm that are im-
plemented but are not currently used in Matador. First, the translation algo-
rithm allows the speciﬁcation of translation weights indicating the probability of
a word being translated into a gloss.2 Weights must be normalized. The weight
is speciﬁed with the % marker attached to the gloss (e.g.
deber V owe%0.1/
should::AUX%0.9).
This information is passed on through the system and is
used in the last statistical ranking step in Halogen.
The second additional feature is the Translation Memory mode allowing phrase-
to-phrase translation. This mode detects the appearance of the :surface feature.
If a translation is matched, the feature is translated; otherwise it is deleted.

Resources for Really Poor Languages
The approach used here to create the translation dictionary and the choice to
use an of-the-shelf parser might not be possible for other languages. Eﬀorts like
the Translingual Information Detection, Extraction and Summarization program
(TIDES) are focused on fast acquisition of bilingual resources, even in low-density
100

languages such as Cebuano.
See, for example, the large (80K line) bilingual
resource extracted in 4 days by the University of Maryland [127] at the TIDES

As explained in Section 3.4, the only restrictions on the translation dictionary
are (1) the form of the SL words needs to be consistent between the parser and
the translation dictionary and (2) the TL words need to be lexemes. Therefore,
it is possible to use an aligned bilingual parallel corpus together with a TL mor-
phological analyzer to create a dictionary that maps SL words in surface form to
TL lexemes. The same bilingual parallel corpus can be used to infer the structure
of parses on the TL side to build a treebank of the SL side. This treebank can
be used to train statistical parsers for the SL language [17, 24]. Statistical parses
typically do not provide lexemes. This is not a problem for this approach since
the SL word form in the parse and the dictionary would still be consistent.
The technique described above for creating parsers and translation dictionar-
ies using parallel corpora is an excellent example of the generation-heavy approach
applied to the problem of resource creation as it pertains to GHMT.

Exerge — the core generation engine of Matador — consists of seven steps (Fig-

the linearization generates a lattice of word sequences, they are ranked using a

Given the lack of large-scale resources containing categorial variations, re-
searchers frequently develop and use alternative algorithmic approximations of
such a resource. These approximations can be divided into Reductionist (An-
alytical) or Expansionist (Generative) approximations. The former focuses on
the conversion of several surface forms into a common root. Stemmers such as
the Porter Stemmer [118] are a typical example. The latter, or expansionist ap-
107

proaches, overgenerate possibilities and rely on a statistical language model to
rank/select among them. The morphological generator in Nitrogen is an example
of such an approximation [89].
There are two types of problems with these approximations: (1) They are
uni-directional and thus limited in usability—A stemmer cannot be used for gen-
eration and a morphological overgenerator cannot be used for stemming; (2)
The crude approximating nature of such systems cause many problems in qual-
ity and eﬃciency from over-stemming/under-stemming or over-generation/under-
generation.
Consider, for example, the Porter Stemmer, which stems communicationN,
communeN and communismN to commun. However, it does not produce this
same stem for communistN or communicableAJ (stemmed to communist and
communic respectively).2
Another example is the expansionist Nitrogen mor-
phological generator, where the morphological feature +nominalize −verb ap-
plied to develop returns eleven variations including ∗developage, ∗developication
and ∗developy. Only two are correct (development and developing). Such over-
generation multiplied out at diﬀerent points in a sentence expands the search
space exponentially, and given various cut-oﬀs in the search algorithm, might
even appear in some of the top ranked choices.
Given these issues, the goal is to build a database of categorial variations that
can be used with both expansionist and reductionist approaches without the cost
of over/under-stemming/generation.
The CatVar database was developed using a combination of resources and
algorithms including the LCS Verb and Preposition Databases [35], the Brown

5.2
Input Normalization
The input syntactic dependency is converted into a normalized dependency rep-
resentation in which prepositions are treated as edge labels (henceforth syntactic
relations) rather than node labels. Edges in this normalized dependency have
two simultaneous speciﬁcations (1) syntactic relation (as in :subj, :obj, or a
preposition or list of prepositions) and (2) thematic role (as in ag, th, or goal).
While the syntactic relations are speciﬁed by the input, the thematic roles are
generated in the thematic linking step (Section 5.4). The target-correct syntactic
relations are reassigned in the later step of syntactic assignment (Section 5.6).
This representation allows access to both levels simultaneously yet hides them if
necessary. Figure 5.4 exempliﬁes what the normalized dependency looks like for
the input sentence in Figure 4.4.

5.5
Structural Expansion
This step is for exploring alternative structural conﬁgurations of the input. Sev-
eral operations are applied here:
• First Degree Structural Expansion
– Node-Node Conﬂation
– Node-Relation Conﬂation
– Node-Node Inﬂation
– Node-Relation Inﬂation
• Second Degree Structural Expansion
– Node-Node Inﬂation followed by Node-Relation Conﬂation
– Node-Relation Inﬂation followed by Node-Node Conﬂation
In all these cases, new created alternatives are OR’ed to the original structure.

generated: in example (46), the prepositions to, toward, towards, into, at, and by
are generated as possible realizations of the theta role goal. This overgeneration
is addressed in the statistical dependency pruning step next.
Second, verb class speciﬁcations are enforced. These include verb aspect (prog
or perf), verb form (fin or inf), and argument categorial speciﬁcations (N or
126

AJ). If a speciﬁed categorial variation is not available for the argument instance,
it is kept as is as a back-oﬀ. For example, the main verb for the Spanish tengo
hambre (I have hunger) translates into (have, own, possess, and be). For the
last verb (be), there are diﬀerent classes that have diﬀerent speciﬁcations on the
verb’s second argument: a noun and an adjective. This of course results in I am
hungry and I am hunger in addition to I (have/possess/own) a hunger. That is
where statistical extraction is most valuable; to decide which sequence is more
likely.
Third, to combat the explosive overgeneration, a rudimentary statistically
driven clean up of syntactic relations for verbs and nouns is done here. This step
uses dependency bigram and unigram counts of words to eliminate prepositions
in word-preposition pairs unseen in the corpus. The goal of this step is not to
rank per se but rather to reduce the search space.
This process is done as follows. For every parent node, if POS is V then
for each parent-child node pair, drop every preposition P in the syntactic rela-
tion that does not have a (parent.instance P) or (P child.instance) dependency
bigram. parent.instance refers to any of the ambiguous instances of the node
parent.
If the resulting list of prepositions is empty, restore it to include all
prepositions as a back-oﬀ.
If POS is N, then check if the parent-child pair has a preference for a :mod
relation (that is no prepositions). If not, a similar trimming of prepositions as
done for verbs is applied here. A parent-child pair is determined to prefer a :mod
relation if its highest (parent.instance child.instance) dependency bigram count
is larger than the highest projected dependency trigram of (parent.instance P
child.instance). A projected dependency trigram is calculated as the lowest of
127

the bigrams (parent.instance P) and (P child.instance).
For example, the preposition in the Spanish el mundo en desarrollo (the
world in development/developing) is replaced by a :mod relationship since the
dependency bigram (developing world) is more common than (world in) and (in
development/developing) in English.
5.7
Statistical Dependency Pruning
This step prunes the ambiguous nodes in the SynDep forest produced by the
last step using dependency bigram statistics (see Section 5.1.3).
For each SynDep tree in the SynDep forest, a dynamic programming algorithm
with memoization is used to calculate the dependency bigram scores over all the
nodes of that tree. The tree is traversed bottom up and each subtree’s score is
maximized: for each parent instance a child instance is picked that maximizes
the sum of the parent-child dependency bigram and dependency bigram score of
the child. The child unigram count is used as a tie breaker. Once the scoring
is completed, selecting the best unambiguous tree is straightforward. The scores
are not length normalized since all the possible ambiguous trees have the same
structure and number of nodes. Figure 5.13 illustrates the pseudocode for this
step.
Associated with dependency pruning is the parameter *depgram Max Tree
Selection* which speciﬁes the number of trees in the input SynDep forest to
pass on to the next step. The default is to select everything. If a speciﬁc number
is used, the same dependency bigram scores described earlier are used to rank
the trees.

John hunger in example (50). Here, the symbolic rules are not enforcing any
subject-verb agreement, which results in allowing the sequence John hunger in

model fails to rank John hunger lower than John hungers, which does not even
make it to the top-ten sequences.
This failure is likely due to the smoothing
model used for handling unseen bigrams, which depends on the word unigrams
instead. Hunger is a more common unigram than hungers.

the preposition by selected for I gave a stab by John has the locational not the

example (50), John is a hunger ranks higher than the poetic John has a hunger.

6.1
Overall Evaluation
The goal of this evaluation is to determine the output quality and robustness of
Matador. For purposes of comparison, four systems are evaluated using test sets
from three corpora with diﬀerent genre.
6.1.1
Evaluation Metrics
Three automatic metrics are used:
• BLEU
As described in Chapter 2, Bleu (BiLingual Evaluation Understudy) is a
method of automatic translation evaluation that is quick, inexpensive and
142

language independent and most importantly correlates highly with human
evaluation [116]. Bleu is used as a metric for translation quality.1 Bleu is
used with all 1 to 4-grams with no context sensitivity. Conﬁdence intervals
are computed with a sample size of 50 for 95% conﬁdence level. The Bleu
speciﬁc version number is v1.03.
• Reference Modiﬁed Precision (RMP)
This is the modiﬁed precision value of the gold standard (or “reference” in
Bleu’s lingo) given one or more test sets. Bleu allows multiple references to
evaluate a single test. RMP is the exact opposite: the precision of a single
reference, or gold standard, is determined given one or more test sets. The
goal is to understand how much of the reference is covered by which test sets
and to measure the overlap among these test sets. Bleu is used to calculate
RMP with the gold standard used as test and diﬀerent combinations of
the evaluated systems’ output as references. The RMP value is not a Bleu
score, since no length penalty is applied. The value is simply the geometric
mean of the 1 to 4-gram precision (which itself is a sub-score in Bleu). The
speciﬁc variation of this metric used in this chapter is RMP-1, which is the
reference unigram precision.
• Runtime (in seconds) is used as a measure of system eﬃciency.

6.1.4
Bleu Bias
One problem with Bleu scoring when only a single reference is used is that lexical
choice becomes more important for evaluation than other criteria that are de-
pendent on the correct lexical choice. For example, the generation of a synonym
of a word in the correct relative word order to another word scores less than the
correct lexical choice for the two words in the wrong relative order. Moreover, all
“incorrect” lexical choices are treated equally regardless of how close or diﬀerent
the chosen words are to the correct words. Even morphologically related words
are not considered correct. There were many instances of such cases in the UN
test data:
(51) SP: Los programas de ajuste estructural se han aplicado rigurosamente.
EN: Structural adjustment programmes had been rigorously implemented.
IBM4: structural adjustment programmes have been applied strictly.
MTDR: Programmes of structural adjustment have been added rigurosa-
ment.

7Except for SYST, all other systems ran on a SparcIII, with 750Mhz and 1GB of memory.

MTDR but the gold standard used it in a diﬀerent tense (had). The diﬀerence,
a single tense feature with minimal eﬀect on understanding is penalized as much
as failing to translate a word or mistranslating a word. In the same example, no
distinction in penalty is made between strictly and the incorrect back-oﬀtrans-

(52) SP: Se instalaron tres nuevos mercados rurales.

correct although no Bleu credit is given. Possible cases are those perfectly un-
derstood cases in which no divergence handling takes place or a diﬀerent kind of
divergence handling takes place. For example, one system generated to be object
of attention instead of the reference’s to receive attention. Finally, incorrect cases
are totally wrong cases that are not understandable. For example, the output
this is not dable contains an untranslated Spanish word (dable meaning possible).
The reference translation is this is impossible.
Table 6.8 displays the number of divergence cases that fall in each of these
three categories for both IBM4 and MTDR. The classiﬁcation was done by one
speaker of English. The results show that in terms of divergence handling, MTDR
and IBM4 have a comparable overall performance.
Structural expansion issues fall into two categories: thematic linking and over-
expansion. Regarding thematic linking, the system has no information about the
statistical distribution of thematic roles and the prepositions that assign them.
For example, whereas the preposition with is mostly associated with instrument
and companion, it appears in the lexicon with theme (ﬁll the cup with water) and
164

goal (mix the sugar with the eggs). This leads to cases of incorrect assignment of
theta roles. A possible solution is a sophisticated language model accounting for
thematic relations. A corpus that could be used as training data is currently under
development at the University of Pennsylvania—Proposition Bank (PropBank)
[115].

inappropriate expansions. In the following two examples, destroy totally and pub-

The use of SN-grams decreases runtime by 59.45% with no negative eﬀect on
text quality. Running the SN-gram pruning doubles the runtime of the Expansion
module. However the payoﬀis a 50% decrease in runtime of linearization and
ranking, both of which are signiﬁcantly costlier time-wise than expansion.
There are two issues with respect to the application of SN-grams: lack of
166

linear-order information and lack of syntactic knowledge. First, SN-gram prun-
ing is unaware of linear order or the relation between sister words. Linguistic
phenomena that depend on such relations are not captured. For example, the
selection of the form of the indeﬁnite article in English (a versus an) is dependent
on the phonology of its following word. The decision, however, in the SN-gram
model is dependent on the parent-child relationship.
This leads to errors in
Matador’s output such as it is an possible exception.
Regarding the lack of syntactic knowledge, the current implementation of
SN-grams is blind to part-of-speech information, which often leads to confusing
relationships and erroneous selections, especially in a language like English where
Verb/Noun homographs are common.
In addition, the current implementation of SN-grams is only aware of bigram
relations. This, together with the lack of part-of-speech information can lead to
erroneous selections such as they continued to their eﬀorts.
Finally, a possible problem that needs to be investigated is the SN-grams
unawareness of structure in a Probabilistic Context-Free Grammar (PCFG) sense.
This may lead to erroneous selection of prepositions which are dependent on the
selection of case/prepositions of sister nodes.
6.2.6
Surface N-gram Language Model Evaluation
The eﬀect of the language model is evaluated using the RMP-1 metric, which gives
a sense of lexical selection independent of linear order. The goal is to calculate
how well a “perfect” language model could have done given the lattice produced
by the previous system modules and to compare that to what the language model
does. To determine the former, a ﬁle is created where, for each sentence, all the
167

unique words in the lattice are listed.
This ﬁle was used as a Bleu reference
to determine the 1-gram precision coverage of the human gold standard. The
result is 68.91. To determine the latter, the ﬁle containing the lattice words is
substituted with Matador’s output. The 1-gram precision for this case is 55.39.
The ratio of the score of Matador’s output relative to a “possible output” given
the data, in this case 80.38%, is a lower limit on how bad the language model is.
That is, the language model could not have possibly reduced the 1-gram scores by
more than 19.26%. This evaluation was conducted using the UN sample described
earlier.
One issue relevant to the language model is the use of bigrams instead of
trigrams, which are known to perform better. This decision is purely based on
technical issues, namely that Halogen’s runtime performance with trigrams is
prohibitively long [66].
This chapter described a thorough evaluation of Matador and its various com-
ponents. The results conclude that Matador is a cross-genre robust translation
system with superior output quality, in terms of grammaticality and accuracy,
relative to a primarily statistical approach. The evaluation of Matador’s compo-
nents revealed a higher degree of linkability in CatVar database relative to Porter
stemmer and a superior eﬃcient performance associated with using structural n-
grams.

Chapter 7
Conclusion
This thesis has presented a novel machine translation approach, Generation-
Heavy Hybrid Machine Translation, and an implementation of it for Spanish-
English, Matador. An evaluation of Matador’s translation quality was conducted
using the IBM Bleu metric and comparing against three systems — simple gist-
ing, primarily statistical (IBM Model 4) and purely symbolic (Systran) — over
three corpora (UN, FBIS and Bible). The evaluation shows that although Mata-
dor scores lower than IBM-model 4 on the corpus where all language models were
trained (UN), Matador has a higher degree of robustness and scores higher when
tested on text with new genre (Bible). Additionally, Matador’s output quality,
in terms of grammaticality and accuracy, is superior to IBM Model 4. Systran
and gisting are the best and worst respectively for all corpora. A second evalua-
tion veriﬁes the advantages of using structural n-grams, which increase Matador’s
speed by 60% with no negative eﬀect on text quality.
The rest of this chapter describes the contributions of this research and its
future work.

7.1
Contributions
The following are the contributions of this research:
• A new model for machine translation that transcends the need for large
amounts of symmetric knowledge such as transfer rules, interlingual lexi-
cons, or large annotated bilingual corpora. This generation-heavy model
banks on the availability of target-language resources to allow for large-scale
machine translation from resource-impoverished source languages (high re-
sourceability).
• A systematic framework for handling translation divergences that uniformly
accommodates a wide range of seemingly diﬀerent divergence types and
their interactions. This approach is distinct from all previous approaches to
translation divergences in that it depends fully on target-language resources
(lexical semantics, categorial variations, statistical language models).
• A hybrid (symbolic-statistical) generation approach that expands the con-
cept of symbolic overgeneration to include conﬂation and head-swapping of
structural variations.
• A general reusable and extensible model for the evaluation and development
of other researchers’ analysis systems and lexicons; the aim is to allow re-
searchers to test long-standing hypotheses about translation or generation.
Some modules may be swapped out or used separately by other researchers
(i.e. Oxygen and Categorial Variation Database)
• A model for language-independent syntactic-to-thematic linking that obvi-
ates the need for monolingual alternation and cross-lingual argument/oblique
170

realization variations, thus eliminating hard-coded/language-speciﬁc mech-
anisms for argument linking. Inverted thematic hierarchies and preposition-
to-role mappings are used to constrain the linking.
• A structural n-gram model for natural language generation that improves
speed with no loss in quality.
• An evaluation of the translation quality of four models of machine transla-
tion with varying levels of complexity.
7.2
Direction of Future Work
This section describes future directions associated with diﬀerent parts of the
system.
7.2.1
Applying Lessons Learned
The extensive evaluation conducted in Chapter 6 has brought to light a number
of important issues regarding the implementation of Exerge and Matador. The
ﬁrst objective of future work is to apply these lessons learned to the system and
reevaluate it.
7.2.2
Categorial Variation Database
Future work includes (1) improving the word-cluster ratio; (2) absorbing more
of the single-word clusters into existing clusters or other single-word clusters;
(3) enriching the clusters with types of derivational relations such as “nominal-
event” or “doer” to complement part-of-speech labels; (4) measuring the applied
171

contribution of using the CatVar in natural-language applications, speciﬁcally
information retrieval; and (5) incorporating CatVar into new applications such
as parallel corpus word alignment.
7.2.3
Structural Expansion
Future work on structural expansion includes extending the work on generation-
heavy verb-divergence to noun-divergences in an eﬀort to create a general model
for divergence handling.
Additionally, the implementation of verb divergence handling in Matador was
limited to main verbs in a dependency tree and only then to cases where con-
ﬂated/inﬂated nodes had no children. This is a major limitation that should
be removed and a general implementation of the basic structural expansion op-
erations should be created to truly be able to test their contribution. Another
extension is to make the system remember the scores used in earlier operations
in order to make better judgments later. For example, currently, the scoring to
decide which thematic linking sets to use is destroyed once the step is over.
Finally, structural expansion can be improved with better thematic linking.
Once PropBank is both available and large-scale enough to provide appropriate
training data, it will be used to collect statistics on the assignment of thematic
roles by prepositions to substitute the symbolic heuristics currently used with
statistical models.
7.2.4
Structural N-grams
SN-grams were shown to be very helpful in Matador. Therefore, some of their
limitations, which were described earlier, will be addressed to exploit their use-
172

fulness more fully.
Other extensions of the use of SN-grams include combined modeling with
surface n-grams. Within this approach, a dependency tree is linearized “in-tree”
(by rotating the children to the left or right of parent and relative to each other).
Long-distance dependencies and short-distance dependencies would be modeled
simultaneously.
An evaluation comparing the performance of SN-grams to other structural
models from the parsing community, speciﬁcally Tree-grams [130], is planned to
evaluate the eﬀect of not accounting for phrasal structure in SN-grams.

7.2.6
Human Translation Quality Evaluation
There are issues with automatic evaluation techniques such as the Bleu metric.
These issues can be better understood by comparing how humans would respond
to the translation quality of the output of the four systems tested in Chapter 6. To
accomplish this, two evaluations will be conducted: one for Accuracy (Fidelity)
and one for Fluency (Intelligibility). Both tests would use a set of 25 sentences
to be randomly selected from the test sets used earlier. The total number of
subjects will be 50 (25 for Accuracy and 25 for Fluency). All subjects are to be
native speakers of English. Two evaluations will be performed online using a web
interface:
• Accuracy Evaluation
This evaluation measures the Accuracy or Fidelity of the translation system,
i.e., how well a system preserves the meaning of the original text whether
the target language is ﬂuent or not. For each of the 25 sentences evaluated,
subjects will be given the gold standard human translation and the machine
translation output of each of the four evaluated systems. Subjects will be
asked to rate the translation accuracy of each machine translation sentence
on a 5-point scale that is loosely based on Nagao’s 7-point scale for Fidelity
[111] (see table 7.1). A score of 5 is given where the content of the original
sentence is fully conveyed (might need minor corrections). A score of 1 is
given where the content of the original sentence is not conveyed at all.

The purpose of this evaluation is to measure the Fluency (or Intelligibility)
of the translation systems. In the ﬂuency evaluation, subjects will be given
