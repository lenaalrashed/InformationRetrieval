Abstract
Large language models have improved dia-
logue systems, but often process conversational
turns in isolation, overlooking the event struc-
tures that guide natural interactions. Hence
we introduce EventWeave, a framework that
explicitly models relationships between conver-
sational events to generate more contextually
appropriate dialogue responses. EventWeave
constructs a dynamic event graph that distin-
guishes between core events (main goals) and
supporting events (interconnected details), em-
ploying a multi-head attention mechanism to
selectively determine which events are most
relevant to the current turn. Unlike summa-
rization or standard graph-based approaches,
our method captures three distinct relationship
types between events, allowing for more nu-
anced context modeling. Experiments on three
dialogue datasets demonstrate that EventWeave
produces more natural and contextually appro-
priate responses while requiring less computa-
tional overhead than models processing the en-
tire dialogue history. Ablation studies confirm
improvements stem from better event relation-
ship modeling rather than increased informa-
tion density. Our approach effectively balances
comprehensive context understanding with gen-
erating concise responses, maintaining strong
performance across various dialogue lengths
through targeted optimization techniques.

Introduction
Large language models have significantly advanced
dialogue systems, driving impressive progress in
both research and industry (Sakurai and Miyao,
2024; Xu et al., 2024; Du et al., 2024). However,
many existing methods process each conversational
turn as an isolated text snippet, potentially over-
looking the underlying event structure that guides
natural interactions (Lv et al., 2023). Drawing

2020) we propose that effective conversations bal-

for dialogue systems is not merely recalling these
events but understanding their relevance to generate
natural, contextually appropriate responses.
Current approaches to dialogue coherence in-
clude summarization-based memory (Araujo et al.,
2023; Zhong et al., 2022) and graph-based methods
(Yang et al., 2020; Ong et al., 2024). While sum-

marization methods implicitly capture events, they
often condense information uniformly without ex-
plicitly modeling the relationships between events
or their varying importance to the current dialogue
turn (Kirstein et al., 2025). Similarly, graph-based
approaches merge conversation segments into high-
level nodes but may not effectively distinguish be-
tween core and supporting events (Walker et al.,
2022). Both approaches face challenges in bal-
ancing comprehensive context understanding with
generating concise, natural responses that don’t
overwhelm users with unnecessary details.
To address these challenges, we introduce Even-
tWeave, a framework that explicitly models event
relationships to improve dialogue response gen-
eration by constructing a dynamic event graph
that identifies and organizes conversational events
based on their semantic relationships. Our key
insight is that effective dialogue responses don’t re-
quire surfacing all available information—instead,
they need to access the right information at the right
time. Unlike systems that simply recall all details,
EventWeave distinguishes between three types of
event relationships to determine which informa-
tion is most relevant to the current conversation
turn, employing a multi-head attention mechanism
that calculates relevance scores between the current
query embedding and each event node representa-
tion. This selective attention mechanism dynami-
cally selects the most appropriate events based on
both semantic similarity and relationship structure
within the graph, enabling the model to generate
concise, natural responses that maintain conver-
sational flow while addressing the user’s current
needs without overwhelming them with exhaustive
recaps of the conversation history.
We evaluate EventWeave on three benchmark
datasets: Conversation Chronicle (CC) (Jang et al.,
2023), Multi-Session Conversations (MSC) (Xu
et al., 2021), and LoCoMo (Maharana et al., 2024).
Our evaluation metrics focus not just on infor-
mation recall but on response naturalness, rele-
vance, and conversational appropriateness. Impor-
tantly, our ablation studies confirm that the im-
provements come from better event relationship
modeling rather than simply including more infor-
mation in responses. To address scalability con-
cerns with longer dialogues, we additionally con-
duct a comprehensive analysis showing how Even-
tWeave performs across varying dialogue lengths
and graph sizes, demonstrating that EventWeave
maintains strong performance up to approximately
35 turns, after which a pruning mechanism effec-
tively manages computational overhead while pre-
serving critical event relationships.
Our main contributions can be summarized as
follows:
• We introduce a framework for dialogue response
generation that explicitly models relationships
between events, enabling nuanced understanding
of conversation context.
• We develop a dynamic event graph construction
method that organizes events into semantic clus-
ters with three relationship types, helping distin-
guish between information that should be sur-
faced in responses and information that should
remain in the background.
• We demonstrate that our approach generates
more natural and contextually appropriate re-
sponses while requiring less computational over-
head compared to models that process the entire
dialogue history.
• We provide a detailed scalability analysis show-
ing how our approach can be optimized for dia-
logues of varying lengths, with specific optimiza-
tion techniques for long-term conversations.

Related Works
2.1
Dialogue Context Management Systems
Multi-turn dialogue systems traditionally employ
hierarchical architectures or memory-augmented
networks to maintain contextual understanding
across interactions (Firdaus et al., 2021; Li et al.,
2024; Joko et al., 2024). While these approaches
effectively handle short-to-medium dialogues, they
struggle with lengthy or multi-session conversa-
tions due to “information explosion” and “informa-
tion conflict” issues. Graph-based alternatives (Tu
et al., 2024; Zhang et al., 2021; Tuan et al., 2022)
offer structured representations of dialogue knowl-
edge and relationships, but often focus only on
high-level events while neglecting subtle yet con-
textually important utterances (Yang et al., 2020;
Ong et al., 2024). This limitation reduces their abil-
ity to distinguish between irrelevant exchanges and
crucial context-supplying details that justify core
conversational events.

Results on Event Correlation
Beyond general response quality metrics, we next
examined how effectively each model detects and
leverages events in multi-session dialogues. We
evaluate the outputs according to four aspects:
• Core Event Awareness (CEA): Measures how
well a response pinpoints the main purpose or
topic in the dialogue.
• Supporting Event Coverage (SEC): Checks if
a model captures the correct and relevant support-
ing events that influence the core event.
• Event Coherence (EC): Assesses how naturally
a model combines core and supporting events in
a single response, rather than listing fragments.

5.6
Case Study
To illustrate how EventWeave operates in practice,
we present a detailed case study from the MSC
dataset. Figure 4 shows a conversation where a
user discusses their missing cat, with explicit an-
notations of core and supporting events. Further
study cases are in Appendix E.
EventWeave’s reasoning process for generating
the system’s response at Turn 5 proceeds as follows:
(1) Event Identification: The model identifies the
core event trajectory (pet missing -> pet returned)
and the supporting event (cat likes tuna). (2) Graph
Traversal: When generating the response for Turn
5, EventWeave: (i) Retrieves the most recent core
event: “Pet returned” from Turn 4 (following the
sequential edge). (ii) Links this to the relevant sup-
porting event: “Tuna preference” from Turn 3 (via
a reasoning link, where the cosine similarity be-
tween embeddings exceeds the threshold θ). (3)
Response Generation: The model integrates both
events to generate a response that acknowledges
the cat’s return (core event) while incorporating
the contextually relevant detail about tuna (support-
ing event). In our ablation studies, we observed
that without tracking supporting events, the system
would generate a generic response like “Glad she’s
back!” without the personalized suggestion about
tuna. Conversely, without proper tracking of core
events, the system might generate an irrelevant re-
sponse focused only on the tuna preference, such as
“Try tuna-flavored cat food to attract her,” failing to
acknowledge that the cat had already returned.

Conclusion
In this paper, we introduced EventWeave, a novel
framework for enhancing dialogue systems through
dynamic event graph construction and hierarchi-
cal maintenance. By explicitly identifying and
updating both core and supporting events, Even-
tWeave enables more nuanced understanding and
context-aware responses across multi-turn and
multi-session dialogues. Experimental results on
two benchmark datasets demonstrate that Even-
tWeave not only surpasses state-of-the-art models
on standard response quality metrics but also ex-
cels in event-centric evaluations, highlighting its
8

capacity to capture and integrate supporting details.
Limitations
While our proposed framework demonstrates
promising results in enhancing dialogue systems
through dynamic event graph construction and hi-
erarchical maintenance, we acknowledge certain
computational considerations common to graph-
based approaches. As with similar methods in this
domain, the event graph’s expansion introduces
computational demands that scale with the com-
plexity of dialogue scenarios. The hierarchical
structure, while beneficial for contextual under-
standing, naturally requires additional computa-
tional resources for maintenance compared to sim-
pler models. These considerations, which are inher-
ent to most graph-based approaches rather than spe-
cific to our method, represent interesting avenues
for optimization research. Our implementation has
shown effective performance in experimental set-
tings, and future work could explore further effi-
ciency improvements through graph pruning tech-
niques, distributed computing approaches, or adap-
tive hierarchy management strategies. Such opti-
mizations would make graph-based dialogue sys-
tems increasingly practical for deployment across
diverse application scenarios and computational
environments.
Ethical Considerations
Our experimental methodology prioritizes ethical
research practices through several key measures.
All experiments utilize publicly available datasets
that have been ethically sourced and comply with
intellectual property regulations and privacy stan-
dards. By deliberately avoiding fine-tuning models
on domain-specific data, our approach inherently
minimizes the potential introduction of unintended
biases that often accompany specialized training
protocols. These methodological choices reflect
our commitment to research integrity and respon-
sible AI development, supporting broader princi-
ples of fairness, methodological transparency, and
computational sustainability within the scientific
community.

Multi-Session Chat.
The Multi-Session Chat

Persona-Chat dataset (Zhang et al., 2018) by in-

consists of conversations that span five sessions be-

is based on personal profiles, and the conversations
build on prior interactions, providing a long-term,
multi-session context. This dataset is designed to
study long-term conversational dynamics, focus-

and continuity over extended dialogues. Table 6
demonstrates the sample from MSC.
Conversation
Chronicle.
The

Chronicle (CC) dataset (Jang et al., 2023) is sim-
ilar to MSC but introduces additional complexity
by incorporating relationships between speakers,
such as “employee and boss” or “teacher and stu-
dent.” These relationships provide a richer context
for studying long-term interactions, where the con-
versational dynamics are influenced not only by
the ongoing dialogue but also by the roles and re-
lationships between the participants. CC is useful
for exploring how relationship-based factors shape
multi-session conversations. Table 7 demonstrates
the sample from Conversation Chronicle.
LoCoMo.
The Long Conversation Modeling (Lo-
CoMo) dataset (Maharana et al., 2024) focuses on
extended dialogue interactions with significantly
longer conversation sessions. The dataset is specif-
ically designed to challenge memory mechanisms
by incorporating natural topic shifts, references
to earlier dialogue content, and information that
must be tracked across long temporal spans. This
makes LoCoMo particularly valuable for evaluat-
ing a system’s ability to maintain coherence and
contextual awareness in extended conversations
where traditional context window approaches are
insufficient. LoCoMo represents real-world sce-
narios where conversations develop over extended
periods with substantial content between related
references.

A.4
Implementation Details
For fair comparison, all methods use the same con-
text window size (4k tokens) and the same back-
bone LLMs. Event graphs in our approach are con-
structed incrementally as conversations progress,
with a maximum of 30 nodes per graph. For each
test case, we use all previous sessions to build the
event graph, while keeping the final session for test-
ing. For each turn in the test session, the model is
provided with the current query and must generate
an appropriate response utilizing information from
past sessions. We use a maximum of 5 previous
sessions as context.

• Event Coherence (EC): Percentage of re-

B.1.4
Analysis of Scalability Performance
The results in Table 9 demonstrate a clear pattern:
while vanilla GPT-4o’s performance degrades with
increasing dialogue length by dropping from 5.2 to
3.8 representing a 27% decrease, EventWeave’s
performance actually has a 40% improvement).
The Event Coherence (EC) metric shows an even
more pronounced divergence, with vanilla GPT-4o
declining from 24.8% to 15.3% as conversations
lengthen, while EventWeave improves dramatically
from 40.2% to 72.6%. This stark difference is at-
tributed to the structure of EventWeave. Rather
than processing the entire dialogue history, Even-
tWeave’s graph structure enables selective attention
to the most relevant events for the current dialogue
turn, reducing the cognitive load and information
overload that affects vanilla approaches. Besides,
the hierarchical event graph preserves critical re-
lationships between core and supporting events
even as the dialogue extends, allowing the model
to maintain coherence across longer time spans.

allergy from turn 7 when discussing restaurant op-

B.5
Impacts of Graph Expansion
A critical consideration for graph-based approaches
in dialogue systems is their scalability as conver-
sations grow longer. As event graphs expand with
additional dialogue turns, both storage require-
ments and computational complexity increase, po-
tentially limiting practical application. This section
analyzes EventWeave’s computational efficiency
and scalability characteristics compared to baseline
methods.
B.5.1
Storage Efficiency
EventWeave achieves relative storage efficiency
through its event representation approach. Rather
than storing large chunks of summarized dialogue
history, our framework maintains compact event
triplets that capture essential semantic information.
Our analysis shows that a typical 10-turn dialogue
requires only 15-20 event nodes in EventWeave,
compared to the more substantial storage require-
ments of full-text summarization methods. This
compact representation helps mitigate memory con-
cerns as conversations grow longer.
B.5.2
Computational Considerations
The selective attention mechanism in EventWeave
provides computational advantages that partially
offset the costs of graph maintenance. By calcu-
lating relevance scores between the current query
and each event node, our model focuses computa-
tional resources on the most relevant portions of
the dialogue history. This approach means that in-
ference computation scales more with the number
of relevant events than with the total graph size.

B.5.4
Efficiency Justification for Embedding
Models
While modern LLM-based embedding models (e.g.,
Qwen3-Embedding) undeniably offer superior se-
mantic resolution, they introduce a strict trade-off
with inference latency. In practice, these advanced
models often exhibit a 5× to 10× slowdown during
embedding generation compared to legacy bidirec-
tional models. Because EventWeave is designed
for dynamic dialogue environments, it fundamen-
tally requires real-time graph updates and high-
frequency retrieval operations at every conversa-
tional turn.
To maintain the system’s scalability, we delib-
erately prioritized low-latency interactions in our
design choices. Our empirical observations demon-
strate that BERT provides sufficient semantic dis-
tinctiveness for accurate Event Graph construction
and node clustering. By utilizing a lighter-weight
embedding model, we successfully circumvent the
prohibitive computational costs associated with
LLM-based embeddings. This architectural deci-
sion ensures that the structural advantages of Even-
tWeave do not become an operational bottleneck,
keeping the overall overhead strictly manageable
even during rapid, multi-turn interactions.

with previous findings that most conversational

sequential progression, temporal references, and
reasoning connections. We acknowledge that cer-
tain specialized contexts (e.g., highly rhetorical
discourse) might benefit from additional relation-
ship modeling, which we discuss as a limitation
and direction for future work.

# Guidelines for Human Evaluation
Overview.
Each annotator will be presented with a short user query or a multi-turn dialogue, followed by a response generated by
one of several models. We ask you to rate each response on the four dimensions described below. Each dimension
should be assigned a numerical score from 1 (very poor) to 10 (excellent). You do not need to compare responses across
models directly; instead, evaluate each response on its own merit with respect to the conversation.
1.
Coherence: Measures how logically consistent and contextually relevant the response is in relation to the
conversation history. A coherent response should follow smoothly from the user’s query or previous dialogue turns,
without contradictions or abrupt topic changes.
Example.
Poor (Score 2):
User: “Where did Marie Curie do most of her work?” Response: “You can buy apples on sale at the corner store.”
(Off-topic, unrelated, incoherent)
Strong (Score 9):
User: “Where did Marie Curie do most of her work?” Response: “She conducted the majority of her research at the
University of Paris, and later at her own Radium Institute in France.” (Fully aligns with the user’s question, consistent
and relevant)
2. Correctness: Measures the factual accuracy and reliability of the response. A correct response should adhere to
established or verifiable facts when they are presented in the conversation or are widely known.
Example.
Poor (Score 3):
User: “Who painted the Mona Lisa?”
Response: “It was painted by Vincent van Gogh in 1889.” (Factually incorrect—Mona Lisa was painted by Leonardo da
Vinci.)
Strong (Score 10):
User: “Who painted the Mona Lisa?”
Response: “It was painted by Leonardo da Vinci in the early 16th century.” (Facts are accurate and relevant)
3. Style: Refers to the language clarity, fluency, and appropriateness of tone. A good style conveys the message in a
clear, succinct, and context-appropriate manner.
Example.
Poor (Score 2):
User: “What’s your favorite book?”
Response: “Book good me is me read.” (Unclear grammar, lacks readability)
Strong (Score 9):
User: “What’s your favorite book?”
Response: “I really enjoy reading ‘To Kill a Mockingbird’ for its thoughtful perspective on justice and morality.” (Clear,
fluent, properly toned)
4. Helpfulness: Measures how effectively the response addresses the user’s needs or queries. A helpful response may
offer additional clarification, relevant examples, or suggestions for next steps. Example.
Poor (Score 2):
User: “Could you recommend a good fitness routine for beginners?”
Response: “Sure, have fun.” (Provides no actionable suggestions or relevant details)
Strong (Score 10):
User: “Could you recommend a good fitness routine for beginners?”
Response: “A balanced program could include three days of light cardio, such as walking or jogging for 20–30 minutes,
and two days of bodyweight exercises like push-ups and squats. Starting slowly and gradually increasing intensity can
help you avoid injury.” (Directly addresses the user’s request with clear suggestions)
