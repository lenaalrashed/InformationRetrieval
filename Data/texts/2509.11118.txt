We Argue to Agree: Towards Personality-Driven Argumentation-Based
Negotiation Dialogue Systems for Tourism
Priyanshu Priya, Saurav Dudhate*, Desai Vishesh Yasheshbhai∗, Asif Ekbal
Department of Computer Science and Engineering, Indian Institute of Technology Patna, India

Abstract
Integrating argumentation mechanisms into ne-
gotiation dialogue systems improves conflict
resolution through exchanges of arguments and
critiques. Moreover, incorporating personal-
ity attributes enhances adaptability by align-
ing interactions with individuals’ preferences
and styles. To advance these capabilities in
negotiation dialogue systems, we propose a
novel Personality-driven Argumentation-based
Negotiation Dialogue Generation (PAN-DG)
task. To support this task, we introduce PACT,
a dataset of Personality-driven Argumentation-
based negotiation Conversations for Tourism
sector. This dataset, generated using Large Lan-
guage Models (LLMs), features three distinct
personality profiles, viz. Argumentation Pro-
file, Preference Profile, and Buying Style Profile
to simulate a variety of negotiation scenarios
involving diverse personalities. Thorough auto-
matic and manual evaluations indicate that the
dataset comprises high-quality dialogues. Fur-
ther, we conduct comparative experiments be-
tween pre-trained and fine-tuned LLMs for the
PAN-DG task. Multi-dimensional evaluation
demonstrates that the fine-tuned LLMs effec-
tively generate personality-driven rational re-
sponses during negotiations. This underscores
the effectiveness of PACT in enhancing person-
alization and reasoning capabilities in negoti-
ation dialogue systems, thereby establishing a
foundation for future research in this domain1.

Motivated by these considerations, we introduce
a novel Personality-driven Argumentation-based
Negotiation Dialogue Generation (PAN-DG) task
aimed at generating negotiation dialogues tailored
to individuals’ personalities while integrating au-
thoritative argumentation techniques for personal-
ized, rational, and collaborative negotiation out-
comes. The PAN-DG task is structured to reflect
real-life personalized negotiation scenarios and is
divided into three core sub-tasks: (i) Personality
Recognition, (ii) Dialog Act Prediction, and (iii)
Response Generation. These sub-tasks collectively
address key phases of negotiation, from identifying
negotiators’ personalities, predicting next dialog
act, to generating responses that align with these
personalities and dialog acts.
To support the PAN-DG task, we present a novel
dataset of Personality-driven Argumentation-based
Negotiation Conversations for Tourism, named
PACT. Recognizing the labor and time-intensive na-
ture of manually creating high-quality personalized
conversational datasets (Zhang et al., 2018; Zhong
et al., 2020; Wu et al., 2020; Jang et al., 2022),
PACT is automatically generated using LLMs, with
human oversight through a three-stage pipeline:
(1) PACT Generation, where the LLM Gemini-1.5
(Team et al., 2023) is prompted to generate nego-
tiation dialogues tailored to diverse personalities.
Considering the multifaceted nature of individual’s
personality (Bayram and Aydemir, 2017), this work
delineates three distinct categories of personality
profiles: Argumentation Profile that elucidates how
individuals negotiate and argue, Preference Profile
that encapsulates their trip preferences, and Buy-
ing Style Profile that characterizes their purchasing
behaviors; (2) PACT Filtering, which employs a
GPT-4-based (OpenAI, 2024) mixture-of-experts
approach to ensure high-quality dialogues; and (3)
PACT Quality Assessment, involving automated and
manual evaluations to assess the dialogue quality.
This pipeline results in a first-of-its-kind dataset,
comprising 8K+ personality-driven argumentation-
based negotiation dialogues for tourism.
Using this dataset, we conduct comprehensive
comparative experiments involving pre-trained and
fine-tuned LLMs for aforementioned sub-tasks, es-
tablishing strong baselines for future investigation.
Our dataset construction approach and task method-
ology are readily extensible to various LLMs, tasks,
languages, and domains, offering a valuable tool
for addressing challenges in data synthesis, even-
tually facilitating the development of more robust
and scalable ABN dialogue systems.
In summary, our contributions are: (i) Introduce
a novel personality-driven argumentation-based ne-
gotiation dialogue generation task to generate dia-
logues aligned with negotiators’ distinct personal-
ities; (ii) Develop an efficient multi-stage dataset
generation pipeline using LLMs with human su-
pervision to automate dataset creation; (iii) Curate
a new dataset, PACT, comprising 8K+ personality-
driven argumentation-based negotiation conversa-
tions focused on tourism domain; (iv) Define a rich
set of three personality profiles: Argumentation
Profile, Preference Profile, and Buying Style Pro-
file, capturing diverse negotiation behaviors; (v)
Establish strong LLM-based baselines for three
sub-tasks: personality recognition, dialog act pre-
diction, and response generation, emulating real-
life personalized negotiation scenarios.

Related Work
Negotiation Dialogue Systems. In recent years,
negotiation dialogue systems have garnered sig-
nificant attention (Zhan et al., 2024; Priya et al.,
2025). Early studies focused on strategic model-
ing to facilitate negotiation (Lewis et al., 2017)
and used dialogue acts to track negotiators’ behav-
ior (He et al., 2018). Strategic negotiation frame-
works have also been applied to specific scenar-
ios, including campsite item negotiations (Chawla
et al., 2021a), job offers (Yamaguchi et al., 2021),
and electronic items (Ahmad et al., 2023). Lately,
LLMs have been employed in negotiation tasks,

enabling more sophisticated strategies and interac-
tions. Balloon price negotiations were explored us-
ing LLM-based agents (Fu et al., 2023), and a plug-
and-play policy planner was introduced to optimize
strategy formulation in LLMs (Deng et al., 2023).
Strategic reasoning has also been enhanced through
chain-of-thought (CoT) prompting (Gandhi et al.,
2023), which also demonstrated success in facil-
itating multi-agent agreements during interactive
games (Abdelnabi et al., 2023).
While these studies primarily model negotiation
through the lens of game theory and action selec-
tion (Nash Jr, 1950; Baarslag et al., 2013), our
research takes a novel approach by adopting an
argumentation-based framework for collaborative
negotiation within the tourism domain. To our
knowledge, this work pioneers the exploration of
argumentation-based negotiation dialogue systems.
Personality-driven Dialogue Generation. Per-
sonalization is critical in enhancing the effective-
ness of dialogue systems (Weston et al., 2018;
Zheng et al., 2019, 2020). Early research focused
on utilizing user profiles to generate tailored re-
sponses across various applications like chit-chat
(Firdaus et al., 2022), persuasive communication
(Tiwari et al., 2023), therapy (Mishra et al., 2023),
and healthcare (Dou et al., 2023; Liu et al., 2024).
However, research on personalization in negotia-
tion dialogues is relatively sparse. Drawing on
psychological evidence that personality (Sharma
et al., 2013) significantly influences negotiation
dynamics, researchers estimated opponents’ per-
sonalities to improve negotiation outcomes (Zhang
et al., 2020; Yang et al., 2020) and proposed rank-
ing their preferences to achieve successful nego-
tiations (Chawla et al., 2022). Priya et al. (2024)
leverages travel persona information to generate
personalized responses during negotiation. Other
research provided empirical insights into strategic
impact of Big-Five personality traits on negotia-
tion outcomes (Huang and Hadfi, 2024; Noh and
Chang, 2024). Strategic planning in negotiations
has been improved by incorporating diverse simu-
lators and integrating psychological factors, such
as Big-Five personality traits, and decision-making
styles, leading to a deeper understanding of human-
like negotiation dynamics (Zhang et al., 2024).
While existing studies primarily focus on Big-
Five personality traits and opponent modeling in
negotiation, our research adopts a more comprehen-
sive approach by defining three distinct personal-
ity profiles - Argumentation, Preference, and Buy-
ing Style to broaden the application of personality-
driven dialogue generation models in negotiation.
Dialogue Generation using LLMs.
Recent
research highlights the efficacy of LLMs in dia-
logue generation (Kim et al., 2022; Abdullin et al.,
2023; Soudani et al., 2024). Some studies utilized
expert-crafted dialogues as in-context examples
to guide LLMs in synthesizing social conversa-
tions (Abdullin et al., 2023; Chen et al., 2023).
However, the manual creation of these dialogues
is labor-intensive and can lead to inconsistencies
in quality. To mitigate this, other works (Lee et al.,
2022; Jandaghi et al., 2023; Han et al., 2024) pro-
posed a framework for generating persona-based
chit-chat dialogues without manual intervention.
Building on these efforts, we introduce a novel
LLM-based multi-stage pipeline that generates
personality-driven, argumentation-based negotia-
tion conversations and incorporates a robust fil-
tering process complemented by comprehensive
quality assessments to ensure quality dialogues.

PACT Dataset Construction Pipeline
To develop the PACT dataset with high-quality
personality-driven argumentation-based negotia-
tion conversations for tourism domain, we leverage
the advanced capabilities of LLMs and design a
dataset construction pipeline with three stages: (1)
PACT Generation, (2) PACT Filtering, and (3) PACT
Quality Assessment, as shown in Figure 2.
3.1
PACT Generation
The PACT dataset consists of conversations be-
tween a travel agent and a traveler, focusing
on argumentation-based negotiation (ABN) over
tourism package elements such as price, destina-
tion, accommodation, etc. These dialogues include
offers, justifications, counterarguments, and rea-
soning to reach mutually beneficial agreements,
reflecting realistic negotiation dynamics. The gen-
eration process involves four key steps:
Travel Packages Information Collection. We
compile a database of travel packages that serves
as the foundation for negotiation dialogues. The
package information is sourced from websites such
as MakeMyTrip, TripAdvisor, and Yatra.com, se-
lected based on popularity, destination types, price
ranges, package inclusions, and provider diver-
sity to capture a broad spectrum of traveler prefer-
ences. This data is then used to create 10 distinct
travel packages, each with a unique name, descrip-

Negotiators Profile Setting. In real-world nego-
tiation, negotiators exhibit varied personalities that
influence their interactions. Experiments show that
when a negotiator’s personality profile is absent,
LLMs generate dialogues with repetitive themes.
To ensure the dialogues reflect distinct personalities
and simulate realistic negotiation dynamics, each
party is assigned a unique personality. These pro-
files dictate how they negotiate, respond to offers,
and articulate their reasoning.
While the Big-Five Personality Traits framework
(Roccas et al., 2002) offers a broad view of hu-
man behavior, it lacks the domain-specific granu-
larity and may misrepresent individual differences
(Block, 1995), which is required for negotiation
in the tourism context. In particular, it does not
model domain-dependent factors like tourism pref-
erences, argumentation stances, or purchasing be-
havior, which are essential for shaping meaningful
negotiation in the tourism domain. To model these
critical aspects, we introduce three domain- and
task-specific profiles that directly influence differ-
ent facets of negotiation behavior. For both travel
agent and traveler, we define an Argumentation Pro-
file, and for travelers, we introduce two additional
profiles: Preference Profile and Buying Style Pro-
file. For both travel agent and traveler, we define
an Argumentation Profile, and for travelers, we in-
troduce two additional profiles: Preference Profile
and Buying Style Profile.
The Argumentation Profile defines how a nego-
tiator persuades, accepts, or rejects offers and argu-
ments during negotiation, influencing their stance
and responses to opposing views. In our work, we
delineate four argumentation profiles based on ne-
gotiators’ typologies (Amgoud and Parsons, 2001;
Heras et al., 2013): Agreeable (Ag), Disagreeable
(Di), Open-minded (Om), and Argumentative (Ar).
During dataset generation, travelers are ran-
domly assigned either an Ag or Di profile, while
travel agents receive an Om or Ar profile. This
assignment ensures a balanced and realistic negoti-
ation environment by incorporating diverse negotia-
tion behaviors. Travelers may either prioritize ease
and cooperation (Ag) or adopt a more selective,
value-driven, and uncooperative approach (Di), fre-
quently challenging offers, expressing skepticism,
and rejecting proposals unless presented with com-
pelling justifications. In contrast, travel agents can
take on a user-centric, flexible role (Om) or adopt a
more assertive, aggressive, and profit-driven stance
(Ar), actively pushing for higher gains and re-
sisting concessions to maximize their advantage
in negotiations. This strategic assignment decou-
ples agent behavior from traveler characteristics,
thereby preventing unintended correlations, en-
hancing dialogue diversity, and the dataset’s ap-
plicability across various negotiation scenarios in
the tourism domain.
Preference Profile is specific to the tourism do-
main and delineates travelers’ trip preferences that
are critical for understanding their travel behav-
iors. A trip comprises both essential features like
price, destination, accommodation, and meals, as
well as supplementary features like shopping areas
or monuments. While essential features are rele-

Conversation Flow Generation. The genera-
tion of conversation flows is a multi-step process de-
signed to capture complex dynamics of personality-
driven ABN. It includes ABN-specific dialog acts
formulation and ABN pathway encoding.
ABN-specific Dialog Acts Formulation. ABN
conversations necessitate careful formulation of
dialogue acts addressing the intricacies of argu-
mentation, persuasion, and structured reasoning
integral to negotiation. To effectively capture these
nuances and represent the interactive dynamics be-
tween traveler and agent for developing a robust
ABN dialogue system, we devise a set of 23 ABN-
specific dialogue acts categorized into three types:
(i) negotiation dialog acts, (ii) argumentation dialog
acts, and (iii) general dialog acts.
Negotiation dialogue acts capture the transac-
tional dynamics in proposing, modifying, or ac-
cepting deal terms, including offers, counterof-
fers, and concessions related to price, features,
or services. These acts include Negotiate-price-
increase,
Negotiate-price-decrease,
Negotiate-
price-nochange, Negotiate-add-X, and Negotiate-
remove-X. Argumentation dialogue acts address
reasoning, persuasion, and justifications when ne-
gotiators present arguments to support proposals or
counter opposing views, aiming to convince each
other. These acts include Concern-price, Disagree-
price, Justify-price, Assurance-price, Disagree-X,
Justify-X, and Assurance-X. General dialogue acts
encompass broader interactions, such as greetings,
questioning, clarifications, and so on to facili-
tate smooth communication. These include Greet-
Ask, Inform, Elicit-preference, Ask-price, Tell-
price, Ask-clarification-X, Provide-clarification-X,
Provide-consent, Consent-response, Accept, and
Acknowledge-acceptance. Descriptions of these
dialogue acts can be found in Appendix C.
ABN Pathway Encoding. It involves systemat-
ically generating a structured sequence of dialog
acts to simulate a rational and coherent negotiation
pathway between a traveler and a travel agent. This
process begins with the simulation of the traveler’s
behavior, where their preference, buying style, and
argumentation profiles are randomly selected to re-
flect diverse negotiation scenarios. A probability
distribution over amenities determines the likeli-
hood that the traveler accepts or rejects specific
proposals, while their budget and tolerance limits
are modeled using a normal distribution. These
parameters define the initial constraints of the trav-
eler and adapt dynamically based on the decisions
made during the negotiation. Together, the agent’s
behavior is simulated by defining its argumentation
profile, which is influenced by the traveler’s pref-
erences and budget. The agent initially assumes a
uniform probability distribution for traveler’s pref-
erences and refines it based on their responses, al-
lowing dynamic adjustments to align with traveler’s
preferences, such as adding or removing amenities.
To illustrate the flow, consider a typical negoti-
ation. The conversation begins with the agent ex-
ecuting a ‘Greet-Ask’ act to welcome the traveler
and inquire about their preferences. The traveler
responds with an ‘Elicit-preference’ act to articu-
late their preferences. Subsequently, the agent uses
the ‘Inform’ act to propose a package detailing
amenities, services, and costs. If the traveler raises
concerns about the price (‘Concern-price’) or dis-
agrees with the offer (‘Disagree-price’), the agent
may justify its proposal (‘Justify-price’) or make
concessions (‘Negotiate-price-decrease’). When
necessary, the conversation transitions to an argu-
mentation or negotiation phase, where both par-
ties present counter-offers or arguments to reach
mutually beneficial agreement. The negotiation
concludes when an agreement is reached or the
predefined turn limit is exhausted, marked by the
agent’s execution of an ‘Accept’ act. The gener-
ation of these conversation flows ensure that con-
versations adhere to rational progressions, closely
aligning with real-world negotiation patterns. Be-
sides, this approach facilitates scalability by en-
abling the adaptation of conversation flows across

To further ensure rationality, this module em-
ploys specific constraints to maintain focus dur-
ing negotiation.
First, it monitors negotiation
specifics on the ongoing deal for each travel pack-
age, including the agent’s minimum and current
prices, traveler’s proposed price, and tolerance
limit (ϕ). Second, to promote mutually beneficial
outcomes, price negotiations are capped at a prede-
fined number of conversational turns. When this
limit is reached, the conversation shifts to nego-
tiation phase with acts like ‘Negotiate-add-X’ or
‘Negotiate-remove-X’. If the traveler is dissatisfied
or needs further discussion on the price, the conver-
sation enters the argumentation phase. This phase
is also limited to a specified number of turns based
on the argumentative profiles of both parties to
keep the dialogue focused and prevent excessive
prolongation of negotiation process.
Third, to determine pricing in subsequent turns,
we adopt a strategy that assumes a gradual reduc-
tion in price disparity, with increments for traveler
and decrements for agent across turns following
Faratin et al. (1998). The equations governing
price adjustment between traveler (Pt) and agent
(Pa) are: Pak = Ptk−1 + (Pak−1 −Ptk−1)e−ck and
Ptk = Pak−1 + (Pak−1 −Ptk−1)e−ck, where c is a
constant regulating the rate of price adjustments be-
tween conversational turns. A higher c indicates a
more aggressive concession strategy, while a lower
c denotes a conservative approach to price changes
by the agent. In our approach, the agent is assigned
a higher c to reflect its flexibility in adjusting prices,
whereas the traveler is given a lower c to signify a
stronger adherence to budget constraints. The value
of c for the agent is also contingent upon the initial
price difference between two parties; specifically,
in low-budget scenarios characterized by a signifi-
cant price gap, a higher c is adopted, whereas, in
high-budget situations with a narrower gap, a lower
c is applied (c.f. Table 7 in appendix). Throughout
negotiation, agent will execute an ‘Accept’ dialog
act if traveler’s proposed price is ≤(Pak −ϕ∗Pak),
signaling the conclusion of negotiation.

3.2
PACT Filtering
A thorough examination of the PACT dataset reveals
four distinct categories of erroneous conversations:
(1) Empty utterances; (2) Repetitive utterances; (3)
Insufficient interaction rounds; (4) Insufficient dia-
log act annotations; and (5) Improper conversation
opening/closing. To enhance the dataset quality, we
remove these undesirable conversations. Besides
this initial post-processing, we implement a filter-
ing mechanism to eliminate conversations that do
not adhere to specified prompt conditions. Given
the cumbersome nature of manual filtering, we
capitalize on the evaluative capabilities of GPT-4
(Liu et al., 2023). Specifically, we design a fil-
tering pipeline for the PACT dataset, employing a
mixture-of-experts approach based on GPT-4 (Ope-
nAI, 2024) to assess the multifaceted nature of the
generated conversations. We devise five experts,
each evaluating different facets of the conversation:
(i) General Conversation Quality Expert (GCQE)
to assess dialogue quality, including Coherence,
Consistency, Diversity, Topic Depth, Understand-
ing, Flexibility, and Likeability using Fine-Grained
Evaluation of Dialog (FED) metrics (Mehri and Es-
kenazi, 2020); (ii) Personality Consistency Expert
(PCE) to evaluate the alignment of generated con-
versation with the traveler’s and travel agent’s per-
sonalities; (iii) Negotiation Efficacy Expert (NEE)
to evaluate the effectiveness of negotiation between
traveler and travel agent to ensure a win-win sce-
nario; (iv) Argumentation Efficacy Expert (AEE)
to evaluate the ability of negotiating parties to man-
age arguments and present persuasive rationale to
achieve mutually beneficial outcomes; and (v) Tox-
icity Expert (TE) to detect harmful, biased, or of-
fensive conversations.
These experts are implemented by prompting
GPT-4 in a zero-shot setting to rate the conversa-
tions on a 1-3 scale (1: No, 2: Somewhat, 3: Yes)
except for Consistency, using a 0-1 scale (0: Consis-
tent, 1: Inconsistent). Each rating is accompanied
by an explanation to incite the rationale behind the
assessment. The prompt templates for expert eval-
uation are furnished in Table 12 and Table 13 in

3.3
PACT Quality Assessment
Automatic Assessment. Given that dialogues are
generated through prompting the LLM, a signif-
icant concern arises regarding the limited lexical
and semantic diversity of the synthetically gener-
ated conversations. To address this, we evaluate
lexical diversity (differences in word choice) us-
ing the BLEU-1/2 (B-1/2) (Papineni et al., 2002),
METEOR (MET) (Banerjee and Lavie, 2005),
ROUGE-L (R-L) (Lin, 2004), DISTINCT-1/2 (D-
1/2) (Li et al., 2015) and Self-BLEU-1/2 (SB-1/2)
(Zhu et al., 2018) metrics following (Wang et al.,
2021; Chen et al., 2023). As reported in Table 1, the
generated conversations yield low scores for B-1/2,
MET, R-L, and SB-1/2 coupled with high D-1/2
scores. These findings demonstrate that dialogue
utterances exhibit substantial lexical diversity.
Recognizing that utterances containing similar
words may convey different meanings, while those
using different words can express similar meanings
(Yarats and Lewis, 2018), we also assess seman-
tic diversity (diversity of the content) through the
Sem-Ent (Han et al., 2022) metric. As shown in
Table 1, the high Sem-Ent score suggests that dia-
logues maintain significant semantic diversity, re-
flecting varied and contextually appropriate mean-
ings across utterances.
For evaluating the quality of text generated by
LLMs, we employ FED (Mehri and Eskenazi,
2020) and G-Eval (Liu et al., 2023) metrics to
assess the quality of dialogues generated by the
LLM. FED and G-Eval evaluate dialogues across
18 and 4 aspects, respectively. The list of eval-
uated aspects for FED and G-Eval are furnished
in Appendix E. We compute the average across all
evaluated aspects to constitute the final ‘FED Score’
and ‘G-Eval Score’. The result presented in Table

Experiments
Tasks. Upon the construction of PACT, we can
support an entire PAN-DG task mirroring the
real-life negotiation scenario. We split the entire
personality-driven ABN dialogue generation pro-
cess into 3 sub-tasks: Personality Recognition (PR)
that aims to identify the personality profiles of in-
terlocutors through dialogue context; Dialog Act
Prediction (DAP) that aims to infer the future dia-
log act for a given dialogue context; and Response
Generation (RG) that aims to generate travel agents’
probable response based on the dialogue context.
To ensure factual consistency and mitigate hallu-
cinations for RG task, we integrate background
knowledge through a Retrieval-Augmented Gener-
ation (RAG) technique (Lewis et al., 2020). Specif-
ically, we leverage the curated package database
containing verified travel details, including a com-
prehensive list of amenities, services, and their cor-
responding pricing (§3.1).
Further, to align responses with personality and
dialogue act, we integrate outputs from the PR
and DAP sub-tasks. We use a multi-task learn-
ing (MTL) approach during training, where the
model learns the relationships between personality,
dialogue act, and response generation. During in-
ference, PR and DAP provide dynamic personality
and dialog act information for contextually accu-
rate, personality-driven response generation. We
discuss RAG and MTL in detail in Appendix G.2.
Models. We evaluate 7 LLMs - LLaMA mod-
els (Llama-2-7b, Llama-2-7b-chat-hf, Llama-3-8b,
Llama-3.1-8b) (Touvron et al., 2023), along with
Mistral-8b (Jiang et al., 2023a), Vicuna-7b (Chiang
et al., 2023), and GPT-4.1-mini (OpenAI, 2024),
across three sub-tasks.
Task Setups. Following (Han et al., 2024), we
evaluate the effectiveness of PACT on the PAN-
DG task under five setups: (i) Pre-trained Mod-
els (PMs), (ii) Pre-trained Models with Personality
(PMswP), (iii) Fine-tuning on a Human-Human
Negotiation Dialogue Dataset (FT-H2HNego), (iv)
Fine-tuning on PACT (FT-Vanilla), and (v) Fine-
tuning on PACT with background knowledge, per-
sonality, and dialog act information (FT-Ours). We
follow a 70:20:10 train-validation-test split for all
setups. Setups details are given in Appendix G.1.
Evaluation Metrics. For PR and DAP tasks, we
use Accuracy (Acc) and Macro F1 (F1).
For
RG task, we employ Perplexity (PPL), BLEU-
1/2 (B-1/2), METEOR (MET), ROUGE-L (R-L),
DISTINCT-1/2 (D-1/2), BERTScore-F1 (BS-F1),
Response Length (R-LEN), Personality Accuracy
(P-ACC), and Dialog Act Accuracy (D-ACC). Fur-
ther, to measure faithfulness of generated response
with dialogue context and package information, we
employ FEQA and QuestEval (QE). Details on task
setups, implementation specifications, and evalua-
tion metrics are given in Appendix G.

Evaluation Results
Table 5 reports LLM performance across tasks and
setups. Pre-trained models perform poorly on PR,
DAP, and RG tasks, lacking sensitivity to personal-
ity and negotiation nuances. Fine-tuning on PACT
yields notable gains, matching or outperforming
fine-tuning on human-human negotiation data, for
instance, Vicuna achieves 84.56% BS-F1 on PACT
vs. 81.03% on FT-H2HNego, establishing PACT as
a strong fine-tuning alternative.
Our proposed approach (FT-Ours), which incor-
porates package knowledge along with personal-
ity and dialog act information, shows the highest
improvements across all sub-tasks. Notably, for
RG task, it shows substantial improvements across
multiple metrics, including response quality, di-
alogue coherence, and factual accuracy. A sig-
nificant reduction in perplexity reflects enhanced
model confidence and fluency. Moreover, integrat-
ing personality and dialog act information results
in increased P-ACC and D-ACC. Specifically, com-
pared to FT-Vanilla counterpart, it obtains a 27.2%
increase in P-ACC and an 17.6% increase in D-
ACC for Vicuna, highlighting the importance of
these aspects. The notable improvements in FEQA
and QE scores further underscore enhanced factual
grounding and response relevance. These results
demonstrate that background package knowledge
reduces the generation of generic responses while
improving relevance and accuracy.
Furthermore, compared to ChatGPT, Vicuna un-
der our proposed setup (FT-Ours) achieves signifi-
cantly better performance across all tasks, with a
20.4% increase in F1 for PR, 19.6% for DAP, and
12.3% in BS-F1 for RG. Despite ChatGPT’s strong
general language capabilities, it underperforms on
the PAN-DG task, highlighting the necessity of
domain-specific fine-tuning. The low scores on PR
and DAP suggest limited sensitivity to personality
traits and dialog acts, which are critical for struc-
tured negotiation. Its weaker RG performance fur-
ther reflects challenges in generating contextually
relevant, factual, dialog-act, and persona-driven
responses. Overall, these findings show that our
dataset significantly improves the model’s ability
to generate responses that not only reflect personal-
ity but also align with structured dialog dynamics,
thereby emphasizing the importance of structured
dialog acts, personality adaptation, and knowledge
enrichment in developing advanced, personality-
driven ABN dialogue systems. Human evaluations
for RG are provided in Appendix H.

Conclusion
This work introduces a novel Personality-driven
Argumentation-based Negotiation Dialogue Gen-
eration (PAN-DG) task, focused on generating
personality-driven negotiation dialogues in the
tourism domain. For PAN-DG task, we curate
PACT dataset through a multi-stage pipeline using
LLMs. PACT integrates multi-dimensional person-
ality profiles and ABN-specific dialog acts to en-
hance personalized negotiation interactions. We
also establish strong LLM baselines for this task.

Limitations
Controllability. In this study, we outline a prompt-
ing technique for Large Language Models (LLMs)
to generate the PACT dataset, a process that requires
substantial computational resources and advanced
LLMs. Our approach demonstrates promising re-
sults in terms of several manual assessment criteria.
However, the quality of the generated dataset is
inherently limited by the capabilities of the LLM,
as prompting remains an uncontrolled generation
process. Future research could explore incorporat-
ing additional controls in the prompt or employing
weak supervision techniques, as demonstrated by
Chen et al. (2022), to further improve the quality
of synthetic data.
Negotiation Setting. A notable limitation of our
method lies in its constrained ability to fully repli-
cate realistic negotiation conversations, as it as-
sumes participants are fluent, the conversation flow
is coherent, and there are no interruptions caused
by external factors or technical issues. Moreover,
the approach faces challenges in capturing subtle
cultural nuances in personality profiles, such as
humor or user-specific traits, which often become
apparent only through repeated interactions. Be-
sides, the scope of our work is restricted to the
tourism negotiation domain, which involves com-
plex, multi-faceted negotiations, such as balancing
customer preferences, needs, pricing, and services,
thereby providing a rich context for evaluating the
effectiveness of ABN in fostering collaborative and
mutually beneficial outcomes.
Further, while our current framework models
negotiations as self-contained sessions, consis-
tent with many real-world tourism scenarios, we
acknowledge that real-world negotiations are of-
ten iterative, occurring over multiple interactions.
Extending our framework to support such multi-
session dialogues represents a promising future
direction. Also, our current approach models trav-
eler personality using a single dominant trait, which
may oversimplify real-world behavior. Future work
could explore incorporating multi-faceted person-
alities that reflect combinations of traits, enabling
more nuanced preference modeling and improv-
ing the system’s effectiveness in complex negotia-
tion scenarios. Nevertheless, the proposed method
holds significant potential for adaptation to other
negotiation domains, offering opportunities for
broader applicability and deeper investigation into
diverse negotiation scenarios.
Prompt Design. In this work, we do not claim that
our prompt design is novel, nor do we assert that
it is the optimal method for generating negotiation
conversations. Our prompt is structured in a con-
versational way, inspired by the work of Chen et al.
(2022). Rather than focusing on the novelty of our
prompt, we emphasize its application in synthe-
sizing argumentation-based negotiation dialogue
datasets. The idea of generating argumentation-
based negotiation dialogues from scratch remains
underexplored and presents significant opportuni-
ties to advance research in argumentation-based
negotiation dialogue systems.
Computational Costs and Reproducibility. It
is important to note that we used the publicly ac-
cessible free-tier API of LLM Gemini-1.5-Flash
for generating negotiation dialogues due to the fi-
nancial and computational limitations commonly
faced in academic settings. While LLMs like Chat-
GPT and other advanced models have achieved
significant progress in generating fluent and co-
herent responses, they are not a one-size-fits-all
solution. These models also struggle with personal-
ization, primarily due to the lack of diverse datasets
and limitations in effectively modeling user per-
sonas (Chen et al., 2024; Han et al., 2024). Nev-
ertheless, research has demonstrated that LLMs
are capable of embodying personas or personality
traits when provided with appropriately designed
prompts (Jiang et al., 2023b; Salewski et al., 2024).
In this context, achieving satisfactory performance
using smaller (fewer parameters) or openly acces-
sible LLMs presents several advantages: (i) these
models are substantially more cost-effective, en-
abling broader participation in research and devel-
opment even within resource-constrained settings;
(ii) their open accessibility ensures reproducibility,
a cornerstone of academic research, as such models
can be freely shared and reimplemented by others
to validate findings and extend the work further.
This approach not only addresses resource con-
straints but also aligns with the academic objective
of fostering inclusive and collaborative progress in
the field. Thus, simply relying on closed-source
models like GPT-4 (OpenAI, 2024) or other closed-
source powerful LLMs for dataset generation, re-
sponse generation or personality recognition is nei-
ther practical nor essential when smaller, openly
available LLMs can deliver comparable outcomes
in a cost-efficient and reproducible manner. Never-
theless, future studies could examine the effective-
ness of using more advanced, closed-source LLMs,

Ethics Statement
Datasets. Ethical considerations play a critical role
in the construction and use of the dataset. Given
that the dialogues are synthetically generated, it
is important for users to engage with the data re-
sponsibly, with awareness of the potential risks,
such as cultural appropriation or misrepresentation.
Since language models are trained on web-based
data, there is a risk of perpetuating biases that may
reinforce stereotypes, contribute to discrimination,
or marginalize certain communities. Previous re-
search has shown that synthetic data can exacer-
bate these biases, leading to feedback loops that
increase the likelihood of generating biased lan-
guage (Taori and Hashimoto, 2023). Hence, we
collaborate with linguists, and language experts,
who are core members of our research team to filter
and manually evaluate generated conversation to
prevent unintentional perpetuation of stereotypes
or cultural insensitivity. Human evaluators are com-
pensated in accordance with institutional policies.
To safeguard proper usage, access to the dataset
will be granted only upon signing an agreement
that stipulates its use solely for research purposes.
Negotiation Systems. In light of the inherent
bargaining dynamics present in negotiations, the
development and deployment of argumentation-
based negotiation dialogue systems must be guided
by strict ethical standards.
This study was re-
viewed and approved by our Institutional Review
Board (IRB). Our approach to personality-driven
argumentation-based negotiation emphasizes user
flexibility and aims to foster win-win, mutually
beneficial outcomes. Unlike zero-sum negotiations,
where one party’s gain is another’s loss, our system
is designed to maximize benefits for both parties.
Crucially, users retain full autonomy, ensuring they
are never pressured into accepting deals that do not
meet their needs.
LLMs.
We utilized large language models
(LLMs) exclusively within their designated frame-
works, adhering to all relevant licensing agree-
ments. Our approach aligns with a range of contem-
porary initiatives focused on assessing the varied
capabilities of LLMs in generating synthetic con-
versations, thereby guaranteeing compliance with
ethical standards and operational protocols.

Travel Packages
The database consists of information about 10
distinct travel packages, namely Tranquil Escape,

B
Personality Profiles Description
B.1
Argumentation Profiles
• Agreeable characterizes interlocutors who tend
to accept offers and arguments with minimal
conflict, promoting a cooperative and consensus-
driven approach. They prioritize reaching mutual
agreements and are less likely to challenge or
reject proposals, demonstrating flexibility and
accommodation throughout the negotiation pro-
cess.
• Disagreeable characterizes interlocutors adopt-
ing a more critical and skeptical stance toward
offers and arguments. They tend to reject pro-
posals unless presented with compelling reasons
to accept them. They challenge positions fre-
quently, leading to more difficult negotiations as
they prioritize ensuring their own interests are
satisfied.
• Open-minded characterizes interlocutors engag-
ing constructively in negotiation, critically eval-
uating offers and arguments without immediate
acceptance or rejection. They strike a balance
between cooperation and skepticism, accepting
or countering arguments based on rational evalu-
ation. These agents focus on maintaining a pro-
ductive dialogue and are more willing to explore
alternative solutions.
• Argumentative characterizes interlocutors who
indicate a strong tendency to challenge and
counter opposing offers or arguments. They are
more confrontational and are quick to reject or at-
tack propositions, actively engaging in debates to
assert their own position. Their negotiation pat-
tern often involves intense argumentation, seek-
ing to dominate the dialogue through persuasive
counterarguments.
B.2
Preference Profiles
• Culture Creature prefers cultural experiences
during trips, drawn to theater performances, mu-
seums, monuments, art exhibitions, and local
festivals.
• Action Agent prefers dynamic and lively envi-
ronments, seeking out nightclubs, upscale restau-
rants, and various entertainment venues to en-
hance their travel experience.
• Avid Athlete prefers staying active during va-
cations, focusing on engaging in favorite sports
such as golf, tennis, or other athletic activities.
• Thrill Seeker prefers adrenaline-pumping, high-
risk activities that provide excitement and an
emotional high, such as skydiving, bungee jump-
ing, or extreme sports.
• Trail Trekker prefers outdoor experiences, ac-
tively seeking opportunities for hiking, walking,
exploring parks and forests, and enjoying nature
in mountains or through birdwatching.
• Escapist prefers vacations that offer a retreat
from everyday life, seeking tranquility and relax-
ation in quiet, peaceful environments.
• Shopping Shark prefers destinations with vi-
brant shopping opportunities, including shopping
areas and local markets.
• Boater prefers water-based destinations where
their boat serves as a home, valuing the experi-
ence of being on the water and exploring coastal
or lakefront areas.
• Sight Seeker prefers to explore and pause for
landmarks, events, scenic vistas, and attractions,
always eager to take in the sights and experiences
along the journey.
• Beach Lover prefers to relax and sunbathe in

G.2
Response Generation Details
G.2.1
Retrieval-Augmented Generation
We design our response generation framework
based on the Retrieval-Augmented Generation
(RAG) model (Lewis et al., 2020) to integrate back-
ground knowledge into LLMs and mitigate the is-
sue of hallucination during RG task. We utilize
the curated database of travel packages to collect
the package details. To enable efficient retrieval,
we generate dense vector representations of these
details and the context using BERT-based embed-
dings (Devlin et al., 2019). Then, we retrieve the
top-most package details that closely align with
the dialogue context, ensuring that responses re-
main coherent and factually accurate. For selecting
top-most package details, we then compute the
cosine similarity between the dialogue context em-
beddings and the package embeddings stored in the
vector database to retrieve the package exhibiting
the highest similarity to the target context. After-
ward, we extract the available amenities, services,
and their corresponding prices from the retrieved
package and incorporate them into the generated re-
sponses, thereby ensuring the factual accuracy and
reducing the likelihood of exaggerated or mislead-
ing descriptions. If a requested amenity or service
is unavailable in the retrieved package, we first
check for similar alternatives within the same pack-
age. If no suitable options are found, the traveler
is informed about the unavailability and offered al-
ternative packages to ensure accurate and relevant
interaction.
Once the most relevant package is retrieved, we
extract the amenities, services, and their corre-
sponding prices to gather the necessary information
for response generation. If a requested amenity or
service is unavailable in the retrieved package, our
framework incorporates a fallback mechanism to
ensure relevant alternatives are identified. Specifi-
cally, we search within the same package for ameni-
ties or services that are conceptually similar, lever-
aging semantic similarity using cosine similarity
measure to identify the closest match. For creating
the embeddings of amenities and services, we again
employ BERT model. If no suitable alternatives
are found, the agent politely informs the traveler of
the unavailability and suggest other packages that
might meet the traveler’s requirements. This fall-
back mechanism further ensures that the response
remains accurate and relevant.
G.2.2
Multi-task Learning
Acquiring personality information and inferring
the future action to be taken from the dialogue
context are critical for providing personalized and
contextually relevant travel recommendations in
real-time. To ensure this, we propose to capture the
personality and dialog act information on-the-fly
from the dialogue context, which are then used aa
a signal for enhanced response generation. The
sub-tasks, PR, DAP, and RG tasks are inherently
interconnected, as they all require a deep under-
standing of the dialogue context. To leverage this
interdependence and ensure more accurate and con-
sistent response generation, we propose adopting
a multi-task learning (MTL) approach (Argyriou
et al., 2006) by training the PR, DAP, and RG mod-
els simultaneously. The rationale behind this multi-
task setup is rooted in the shared nature of the
dialogue context input. Each of these models pro-
cesses the same context and outputs a latent vector,
which captures key aspects of the conversation. By
training the models together, the model can learn
more robust and generalized features that enhance
its performance across all three sub-tasks.

G.4
Implementation Details
We split the dataset into train, validation, and test
with the ratio of 7:1:2. All the results are com-
puted on the test set. We prompt the LLMs in
zero-shot setup. For conversation generation, we
adopt nucleus (Top-p) sampling (Holtzman et al.,
2019) with p = 0.95 and temperature T = 1.0.
We fine-tune the LLMs with LoRA adaption (Hu
et al., 2021) using 4*V100 GPUs, with batch size
of 2 per GPU, and learning rate of 5e−5 for three
epochs. The fine-tuning of all the LLMs are based
on the SFT trainer of Transformer Reinforcement
Learning (TRL)2 (von Werra et al., 2020). All the
implementation is done using the PyTorch3 frame-
work. The transformer-based models from Hug-
gingFace (Wolf et al., 2019) are utilized in all the
experiments.

H.1
Evaluation Metrics and Instructions
Human evaluation for the RG task is conducted
by the same four evaluators involved in the dataset
creation process. They assess 50 randomly selected
responses based on six criteria: Personality Rele-
vance (PR), Bargaining Efficacy (BE), Outcome
Fairness (OF), Fluency (F), Coherence (C), and En-
gagingness (E), using a 1-4 scale (low to high). Fur-
ther, they assess the faithfulness of the generated
responses (evaluating whether the responses adhere
to the to the dialogue context and grounded pack-
age information) using Faithful (Faith.) and Hal-
lucinated (Hal.). For each sample, evaluators are
provided with the dialogue context, ground truth
response, and the LLM-generated response to rate
each response across these metrics adhering to the
instructions mentioned below .
1. Personality Relevance (PR) evaluates whether
the generated response aligns with the estab-
lished personality profile.
Instruction: To what extent does the generated
response reflect the established personality pro-
file of the traveler and travel agent?
Scale: 1 (not at all aligned with the personality
profile) to 4 (perfectly aligned with the person-
ality profile).
2. Bargaining Efficacy (BE) measures the effec-
tiveness of the generated response in presenting
compelling offers and arguments that influence
the other party’s decisions, ultimately achieving
favorable outcomes.
Instruction: How effectively does the generated
response present persuasive offers and argu-
ments during the negotiation?
Scale: 1 (ineffective and unpersuasive) to 4
(highly effective and persuasive).
3. Outcome Fairness (OF) evaluates the equity of
the negotiation results by analyzing how well
the generated response reflects an agreement
that meets the interests of both the traveler and
the agent.
Instruction: To what extent does the generated
response indicate a fair and equitable agree-
ment between the parties?
Scale: 1 (entirely unfair, heavily favoring one
party) to 4 (perfectly equitable, achieving a bal-
anced win-win outcome).
4. Fluency (F) evaluates the grammatical correct-
ness of the generated response.

Instruction: How grammatically correct and
fluent is the generated response?
Scale: 1 (severely flawed with numerous errors)
to 4 (fluent and grammatically correct).
5. Coherence (C) examines how well the gener-
ated responses align with the dialogue context.
Instruction: To what extent does the generated
response maintain logical flow and relevance
within the conversation?
Scale: 1 (completely incoherent and irrelevant)
to 4 (perfectly coherent and contextually rele-
vant).
6. Engagingness (E) measures the extent to which
the generated response is engaging, compelling,
and capable of retaining users’ attention.
Instruction: How captivating and interesting is
the generated response in retaining the user’s
attention?
Scale: 1 (not engaging at all) to 4 (extremely
engaging and captivating).
7. Faithfulness (Faith.) and Hallucinated (Hal.)
evaluate whether the response is faithful or hal-
lucinated.
Instruction: Whether the generated response is
faithful or hallucinated? A response is faith-
ful if it aligns with the dialogue context and
package information, whereas it is labeled as
hallucinated if it either contradicts the provided
sources or lacks corroboration from them.

lucination only.

H.2
Evaluation Results
Table 10 presents the results of human evaluation.
We conduct human evaluation for FT-Vanilla and
FT-Ours setups only, as manual evaluation is ex-
pensive. From the results, we observe that the
findings closely align with automatic evaluation
results, providing a comprehensive assessment of
model performance under FT-Vanilla and FT-Ours
setups. In the FT-Vanilla setup, Vicuna achieves
the highest scores across all key metrics - PR, BE,
OF, F, C, and E, with values of 3.52, 3.45, 3.17,
3.39, 3.41, and 3.55, respectively. This mirrors
its strong performance in automatic evaluations,
reinforcing its ability to generate fluent, coherent,
and engaging responses during negotiation. Addi-
tionally, Llama-2-chat and Llama-3.1 demonstrate
competitive performance, particularly in fluency
(F = 3.56, 3.67) and coherence (C = 3.28, 3.01),
indicating their effectiveness in maintaining con-
textual relevance. However, models trained under
the FT-Ours setup with background package, per-
sonality and dialog act information exhibit substan-
tial improvements across all evaluation dimensions.
Notably, Vicuna outperforms all models with PR
= 3.92, BE = 3.81, OF = 3.91, F = 3.93, C = 3.82,
and E = 3.79, showing consistent gains over FT-
Vanilla. These results highlight the effectiveness of
our fine-tuning approach with background package,
personality and dialog act information in enhanc-
ing response personalization, negotiation efficacy,
coherency, fluency, and engagingness. Compared
to its FT-Vanilla counterpart, Vicuna achieves ab-
solute improvements of 10.20%, 9.45%, 18.93%,
13.74%, 10.73%, and 6.33% across PR, BE, OF, F,
C, and E, respectively.
The faithfulness (Faith.) and hallucination (Hal.)
metrics further underscore the reliability of models
under FT-Ours. Across all models, Faith. scores
increase significantly, with Vicuna achieving the
highest Faith. score of 77.98 and the lowest Hal.
score of 22.02. Further, the proposed FT-Ours
setup outperforms ChatGPT across all metrics for
the RG task. These results indicate that our fine-
tuning method not only enhances content quality
but also improves factual accuracy and reduces
hallucinations, making negotiation responses more
trustworthy and consistent. Overall, the results
affirm that our fine-tuning approach (FT-Ours) sig-
nificantly enhances response quality across person-
alization, coherence, negotiation efficacy, and faith-
fulness. The substantial improvements across all
models, particularly for Vicuna highlight the im-
pact of our approach in refining dialogue generation
for negotiation scenarios. To assess the reliability
of human evaluations, Fleiss’s Kappa (McHugh,
2012) is computed to measure the inter-evaluator
agreement. The average Kappa scores of 0.83, 0.83,
0.80, 0.78, 0.77, 0.85, 0.83 are obtained for PR, BE,
OF, F, C, E, Faith. (or Hal.), respectively, implying
substantial agreement among the evaluators.

the travel packages used in the dataset, including
