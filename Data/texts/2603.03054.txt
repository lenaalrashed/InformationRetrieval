PrivMedChat: End-to-End Differentially Private
RLHF for Medical Dialogue Systems
Sudip Bhujel
Abstract—Large language models are increasingly used for
patient-facing medical assistance and clinical decision support,
but adapting them to clinical dialogue often requires supervision
derived from doctor-patient conversations that may contain
sensitive information. Conventional supervised fine-tuning and
reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF) can am-
plify memorization, enabling membership inference and dis-
closure of rare training-set details. We present PrivMedChat
(Private Medical Chat), an end-to-end framework for differen-
tially private RLHF (DP-RLHF) for medical dialogue systems.
Our approach enforces differential privacy at each training
stage that accesses dialogue-derived supervision, combining DP-
SGD for supervised fine-tuning and reward model learning
from preference pairs, and DP-aware policy optimization for
alignment. To avoid costly clinician labeling, we introduce an
annotation-free preference construction strategy that pairs physi-
cian responses with filtered non-expert generations. We evaluate
PrivMedChat across medical dialogue tasks and assess utility,
safety, and privacy under consistent privacy accounting, thereby
providing a practical pathway to align medical chatbots while
offering formal privacy guarantees. We open-source our code at
https://github.com/sudip-bhujel/privmedchat.
Index Terms—Differential Privacy (DP), Reinforcement Learn-
ing from Human Feedback (RLHF), Membership Inference
Attack (MIA), Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO), Low Rank
Adaptation (LoRA).
I. INTRODUCTION
Large Language Models (LLMs) are increasingly used for
clinical decision support and patient-facing assistance, ranging
from diagnosis and symptoms to care navigation and patient
education. Recent medical-domain systems such as Med-
PaLM [1], ChatDoctor [2], and BioMixtral [3] demonstrate
that adapting general-purpose LLMs to biomedical knowledge
and clinical dialogue can improve response helpfulness, do-
main fluency, and adherence to medical communication norms.
These capabilities make medical chatbots promising tools for
scaling access to health information and reducing clinician
burden, especially in resource-constrained settings.
State-of-the-art alignment of such systems commonly fol-
lows a Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback
(RLHF) [4] pipeline. Training a medical LLM entirely from
scratch is both time-consuming and ineffective; instead, it
is common practice to fine-tune a strong pre-trained model
dialogue LLM. The process begins by adapting the base
model through Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT) on high-quality
clinical interactions to learn domain style and conversational
structure. Next, a reward model is trained on ranked preference
pairs to approximate expert judgments of clinical utility and

appropriate escalation) and for reducing unhelpful or overly

Despite these advances, effective medical adaptation and
personalization often rely on training signals derived from
doctor-patient dialogues, which can contain protected health
information (PHI) and other sensitive attributes governed
by regulations such as the Health Insurance Portability and
Accountability Act (HIPAA) and the General Data Protection
Regulation (GDPR). Conventional fine-tuning and RLHF may
inadvertently memorize training records, enabling membership
inference attacks (MIA) [6], attribute inference, or verbatim
leakage of rare patient details [7]. Importantly, even datasets
released in de-identified or “public” form can remain sensitive
in an adversarial setting: rare strings, uncommon symptom
combinations, or residual identifiers may still be memorized
and later recoverable. In high-stakes clinical contexts, such
leakage risks patient re-identification and undermines trust in
deployment.
Differential privacy (DP) provides a rigorous framework for
limiting the influence of any single training record on model

parameters [8]. Prior work has successfully applied DP to
language model pre-training and supervised fine-tuning (DP-
SFT) [9], [10], [11], showing that nontrivial utility can be
retained under meaningful privacy budgets. However, extend-
ing DP to the full RLHF pipeline remains challenging. Both
preference modeling and policy optimization are often highly
sensitive to noise, and naive DP mechanisms can significantly
degrade alignment quality—an issue that is especially critical
in healthcare, where correctness, safety, and calibrated uncer-
tainty are essential. Consequently, existing privacy-preserving
LLMs frequently exhibit pronounced performance drops at
privacy levels that meaningfully reduce empirical leakage
risk [12].
This paper addresses the challenge of extending DP beyond
supervised fine-tuning to the full preference-based alignment
pipeline for medical dialogue. We propose PrivMedChat, an
end-to-end differentially private RLHF framework that applies
DP-SGD [9] to (i) SFT on dialogue-derived supervision, (ii)
reward model training on preference pairs, and (iii) PPO policy
optimization. Because the PPO stage also accesses dialogue-
derived prompts, we apply DP-SGD to both the PPO actor
and critic, accounting for their privacy cost in εtotal. The DP-
trained reward model is kept fixed during alignment so that
its privacy budget is not further consumed.
Although some medical dialogue datasets are publicly re-
leased in de-identified form, we treat dialogue-derived super-
vision as sensitive due to residual identifiers, rare symptom
combinations, and adversarial linkage risks. The DP guaran-
tees reported here apply to the training records used for SFT
and reward modeling and limit the influence of any single
example on the released model parameters, not as a claim of
regulatory compliance.
To avoid costly clinician annotation, we construct prefer-
ence pairs using an expert-versus-non-expert strategy from
medical dialogue corpora. Specifically, we treat real physician
responses as “chosen” outputs and generate “rejected” alterna-
tives using a base LLM prompted to respond as a non-expert
assistant; we apply similarity and quality filtering to ensure
informative preference margins. This produces scalable, high-
signal preference data for reward modeling without requiring
additional human labeling.
Our primary contributions are as follows:
1) Annotation-free medical preference construction. We
present a scalable strategy to form preference pairs for
medical dialogue alignment by pairing physician re-
sponses with filtered non-expert generations, producing
reward-model training data without clinician labeling.
2) End-to-end DP-RLHF. We introduce a DP-protected
training pipeline that applies DP-SGD [9] to all three
stages—SFT, reward modeling, and PPO—ensuring for-
mal (ε, δ) privacy guarantees across the full alignment
pipeline.
3) Utility–safety–privacy evaluation under DP. We eval-
uate utility with automatic metrics, safety with targeted
heuristic checks, and privacy with membership inference
and canary-style probing, and we report results across
multiple privacy budgets using consistent accounting
across training stages.

A. Dataset and Preprocessing
We
utilize
the
medical
dialogue
corpus
OpenMed/MedDialog [13], which has been de-identified
using Presidio [14]. This de-identification process replaces
sensitive information with placeholders, including the removal
of email and phone number patterns and signature-like text.
The DP guarantees are defined with respect to individual
training examples derived from these dialogues. The raw
dialogues are transformed into structured input–output pairs
for SFT and preference learning. Additionally, we apply
lightweight text normalization and implement group-aware
dataset splitting based on conversation identifiers to prevent
cross-split leakage.
To support reward modeling without human annotation,
we generate “rejected” responses using a general-purpose
language model (meta-llama/Llama-3.1-8B) prompted
to simulate a non-expert medical assistant. We then apply
heuristic quality filtering to remove low-quality samples, dis-
carding outputs shorter than 10 words, containing explicit
refusal patterns (e.g., “I am an AI”), or exhibiting substantial
repetition. Because reward modeling benefits from a clear
margin between preferred and dispreferred outputs, we further
deduplicate preference pairs by semantic similarity. Specifi-
cally, each response is embedded using a sentence transformer
(all-MiniLM-L6-v2), and we remove pairs whose chosen
(expert) and rejected (generated) responses exceed a cosine
similarity threshold of 0.90.
Finally, we partition the dataset into training, validation,
and test splits using a group-aware strategy keyed by source
conversation identifiers, ensuring that turns from the same
multi-turn session do not appear across multiple splits. The
resulting corpus yields high-quality, de-duplicated supervision
and preference pairs that clearly separate expert medical
advice from generic assistant responses.
B. Preference Pair Construction
Reward model training relies on ranked preference pairs
(x, yw, yl) constructed via an expert-versus-non-expert strat-
egy that avoids manual annotation. The chosen response yw is
the original physician response provided in the dataset, while
the rejected response yl is generated by prompting a general-
purpose generator model (meta-llama/Llama-3.1-8B)
to behave as a non-expert assistant. Specifically, the gener-
ative mode is conditioned on the patient query along with
a system instruction to provide concise, high-level guidance
while explicitly avoiding detailed differential diagnosis. The
prompt takes the following form:
[System]: ... Avoid detailed differential
diagnosis ...
[Patient]: {patient_text}
[Doctor]:
We use nucleus sampling (p = 0.95) at a temperature 0.7,
and we restrict outputs to matched lengths with a maximum
of 256 tokens. This constraint ensures that rejected responses
remain plausible while being consistently less specific than the
expert reference.

C. Supervised Fine-Tuning
We adapt the base model to the medical dialogue domain
via supervised fine-tuning on the processed conversations.
Because these data may contain sensitive patient information,
we perform DP-SFT using DP-SGD [9]. Our implementation
uses Opacus [15] for privacy accounting and the Parameter
Efficient Fine Tuning (PEFT) technique for training. We freeze
the backbone model parameters and train only Low Rank
Adaptation (LoRA) [16] adapters inserted into the query, key,
value, and output projection modules.
Privacy Guarantees. We enforce per-example gradient
norm bounds using DP-SGD [9] with “ghost clipping,” con-
straining the ℓ2 norm of each per-sample gradient to at most
1.0. We then add Gaussian noise to the aggregated gradients
to achieve a strict privacy target budget, tracking privacy loss
using a R´enyi Differential Privacy (RDP) [17] accountant.
This mechanism limits the influence of any single training
example on the learned parameters, reducing the likelihood
that the model memorizes identifiable patient content while
still learning general medical dialogue patterns.
D. Differentially Private RLHF
Preference-based alignment is performed in two DP-
protected phases within the secure environment of Zone 1 ,
providing end-to-end privacy with respect to the sensitive
training corpus.
1) DP-Reward Modeling: We train a reward model by fine-
tuning a separate copy of the base model on the constructed
preference pairs using the same DP-SGD [9] stack (Opa-
cus [15] + LoRA [16]) employed in DP-SFT. The reward
model learns to assign higher scalar scores to expert physician
responses yw than to non-expert generations yl. Concretely, we
optimize a standard pairwise preference loss (Bradley–Terry /
logistic form):
LRM(ϕ) = −E(x,yw,yl) [log σ (rϕ(x, yw) −rϕ(x, yl))] ,
where rϕ(x, y) is the scalar reward assigned to response
y under prompt x and σ(·) is the sigmoid. The privacy
expenditure for this stage, denoted εRM, is accounted for
independently from SFT.
2) Policy Optimization with Firewall: We align the DP-SFT
policy using PPO [5]. We initialize the PPO actor from the DP-
SFT checkpoint and use the same DP-SFT checkpoint as the
reference policy πref for KL regularization. The private reward
model—trained under DP in the previous phase—provides the
optimization signal. We additionally regularize the policy to
remain close to a reference model πref (typically the SFT
model) via an explicit KL penalty:

Because the PPO prompts are derived from the same dia-

E. Evaluation and Deployment
Zone 3 (Evaluation and Deployment) operates outside the
DP-protected training pipeline and accesses no private patient
data or preference pairs. Evaluations are performed on held-
out test prompts and generated responses, while deployment
uses only user-provided inputs at inference time.
1) Evaluation: We evaluate PrivMedChat along three axes:
utility, safety, and privacy. Automatic utility metrics (ROUGE-
L [18], BERTScore [19], entity overlap, and perplexity) are
computed on a larger held-out subset (5,000 prompts) to
reduce variance in aggregate reporting. For safety checks and
privacy attacks, we use a focused evaluation subset of n = 500

5
prompts and report 1,000-iteration paired bootstrap confidence
intervals. All evaluation prompts are drawn from held-out test
data and are not used during training.
No private corpus is touched during these assessments; all
prompts are non-sensitive.
2) Deployment Considerations: In deployment, PrivMed-
Chat processes only real-time user prompts, inheriting the
formal (ε, δ)-DP guarantees from the DP-protected training
stages. Practical safeguards include real-time monitoring for
out-of-distribution inputs, human-in-the-loop escalation for
high-risk queries, audit logging, and uncertainty-aware refusal
mechanisms. The DP-SGD [9] framework applied across all
training stages provides formal privacy-by-design guarantees.
We do not frame DP guarantees as a standalone compliance
claim; real deployments still require institutional governance,
monitoring, and clinical safety processes.

A. Experimental Setup
We evaluate PrivMedChat on the held-out portion of test
split, focusing on three desiderata: clinical utility, safety, and
privacy protection. In all experimental conditions, we employ
the base model as the common backbone and maintain a fixed
LoRA [16] configuration to control for model capacity and
optimization. This design allows us to isolate the effects of
(i) the training objective (SFT vs. RLHF) and (ii) the privacy
mechanism and associated privacy budget.
Model variants and baselines. Our evaluation considers
five categories of models. First, we present results for the
off-the-shelf base model (without adaptation), which serves
as a reference point for zero-adaptation performance. Next,
we train non-private baselines using standard SFT and PPO-
based [5] RLHF without differential privacy; these models
represent a practical upper bound on achievable utility in
our training configuration. We also include a non-private SFT
model trained for 20 epochs as an intentional Overfit SFT
baseline (PPL≈198.6), designed to amplify memorization and
provide a stress test for MIA sensitivity.
To examine the utility–privacy trade-off under private learn-
ing, we train DP-SFT models with privacy budgets ε ∈
{1, 3, 5, 7} at fixed δ = 10−5. Finally, we evaluate PrivMed-
Chat using our full DP-RLHF pipeline, in which a DP-SFT
policy is aligned via PPO with a separately trained DP reward
model. We match the cumulative privacy budget to the DP-
SFT models (identical ε values and δ), allowing a direct
assessment of whether the RLHF stage improves performance
at equivalent privacy cost.
1) Evaluation Metrics: We evaluate response quality using
a set of complementary lexical, semantic, and domain-specific
metrics calculated on the held-out test set. Unless otherwise
specified, all metrics are derived by comparing the generated
responses with the reference responses provided by physicians.
a) ROUGE-L: We compute ROUGE-L [18], which mea-
sures the longest common subsequence overlap between the
generated response and the reference. We report ROUGE-L
(F1) with standard tokenization.
b) BERTScore:
We compute BERTScore(F1) [19], a
semantic similarity metric that uses contextual embeddings
to align tokens between candidate and reference texts and
combines precision and recall into an F1 score. We report
the mean F1 value across the test set.
c) Perplexity (PPL): Perplexity is computed by con-

d) Medical entity F1: To account for clinically relevant
information that may not be captured by surface-level overlap,
we extract medical entities—such as conditions, medications,
procedures, and anatomical terms—from both the generated
and reference texts using a domain-specific named entity rec-

F1. Unless otherwise specified, entity comparison is performed
using exact string matching following normalization, which
includes case folding and whitespace cleanup.
2) Safety Evaluation: To evaluate safety, we employ tar-
geted heuristic checks aimed at identifying common failure
modes in medical dialogue, including excessive diagnostic cer-
tainty, potentially harmful recommendations, and the omission
of appropriate escalation for urgent symptoms. These checks
are not a substitute for clinician evaluation, but they provide a
consistent proxy for comparing model variants under matched
prompts and decoding settings. We therefore interpret safety
results as comparative signals within our experimental setup,
and we discuss remaining limitations and failure cases in
Section V.
3) Privacy Evaluation: We evaluate privacy leakage using
two empirical attack protocols that capture complementary
notions of memorization and membership inference. First, we
conduct MIA using both loss-based signals and reference-
based variants. We report the Area Under the ROC Curve
(AUC) and the true positive rate at 1% false positive rate
(TPR@1%,FPR), which summarizes an attacker’s ability to
distinguish training members from non-members. Second, we
measure verbatim memorization using canary exposure [7],
in which synthetic canary strings are inserted into the training
data and the trained model is tested for its ability to reproduce
them under targeted prompting.

We evaluate PrivMedChat through four research questions
aimed at assessing whether differentially private RLHF can
produce a medical chatbot that remains clinically useful, safe,
and privacy-preserving:

2 Does the DP training pipeline resist empirical privacy
attacks relative to non-private fine-tuning? 3 Does privacy-
preserving training maintain or improve the safety profile of
generated medical responses? 4 At a fixed privacy budget,
does preference alignment via RLHF deliver measurable gains
over DP-SFT alone?

C. Safety
Safety results (Table VI) indicate that DP-RLHF improves
safety margins beyond both non-private baselines and DP-SFT.
Hallucination rates for DP-SFT range 1.2–3.2%, exceeding
non-private SFT (2.2%). In contrast, PrivMedChat achieves
the lowest hallucination rates overall (1.4–3.0%), with the
ε=7 variant at 1.4%. Harmful advice remains low across all
PrivMedChat variants (0.2–0.8%), comparable to non-private
baselines (0.2–1.2%). Medication validation and emergency
escalation rates stay low and stable (≤0.6% and 3.8–5.0%,
respectively), showing that DP noise does not impair recogni-
tion of urgent clinical scenarios.
D. RLHF Benefit under DP
At matched ε, PrivMedChat (DP-RLHF) outperforms DP-
SFT on multiple utility axes while preserving privacy. The ε=7
PrivMedChat variant achieves the highest ROUGE-L (0.156)
and Entity F1 (0.103) among all DP models. BERTScore
remains uniformly strong (0.836–0.839). Safety gains are no-
table: mean hallucination drops from 2.3% (DP-SFT) to 1.9%
(PrivMedChat). Harmful advice rates remain near-zero across
all models, with DP-SFT averaging 0.25% and PrivMed-
Chat averaging 0.50%—both well within confidence interval
overlap. MIA AUC values for PrivMedChat are statistically
indistinguishable from DP-SFT at the same ε, consistent with
DP-SGD [9] being applied across all training stages.
These results collectively demonstrate that DP-RLHF recov-
ers most of the utility lost to privacy noise, strengthens safety
alignment, and delivers formal privacy guarantees-advancing
the feasibility of privacy-preserving medical LLMs.
E. Ablation Studies
DP noise in the reward model does not degrade downstream
policy quality; the resulting PPO policies match or exceed
non-private PPO on ROUGE-L and BERTScore, underscoring
the robustness of pairwise preference learning. Varying ε

8
within {1, 3, 5, 7} produces only narrow performance bands
(ROUGE-L 0.146–0.156, BERTScore 0.836–0.839), allowing
practitioners to prioritize stronger privacy with minimal utility
penalty. The consistency of near-chance MIA performance
across six attack families confirms that observed privacy is
not attacker-specific.
V. DISCUSSION
Our findings establish DP-RLHF as a viable framework
for training privacy-preserving medical dialogue models.
PrivMedChat maintains competitive automatic utility met-
rics, delivers measurable safety improvements over both non-
private and DP-SFT baselines, and exhibits near-chance perfor-
mance under a comprehensive suite of membership inference
attacks. Among the evaluated privacy budgets, the ε = 7 vari-
ant of PrivMedChat provides the strongest overall operating
point, achieving the highest ROUGE-L (0.156) and Entity
F1 (0.103) among all DP models while preserving formal
(ε, δ = 10−5) guarantees and near-random MIA AUC values.
Privacy–Utility Trade-off. Utility metrics remain stable
across privacy budgets (ε ∈{1, 3, 5, 7}), with ROUGE-L
varying only between 0.146–0.156 and BERTScore between
0.836–0.839 for PrivMedChat. This narrow performance band
indicates that moderate DP noise, when combined with LoRA
and preference alignment, acts as effective regularization with-
out proportionally degrading downstream response quality.
Crucially, increasing the privacy budget does not yield mono-
tonic gains, suggesting that practitioners can select lower ε
values with minimal expected utility loss in this domain.
Safety and Clinical Relevance. DP-RLHF yields consis-
tent safety gains. PrivMedChat reduces hallucination rates to
1.4–3.0% (versus 1.2–3.2% for DP-SFT) and maintains low
harmful advice rates across all budgets (0.2–0.8%). These
improvements demonstrate that the RLHF stage successfully
internalizes preference-based safety constraints even under dif-
ferential privacy. Medication validation and emergency escala-
tion behaviors remain stable across all variants, indicating that
DP noise does not impair critical clinical reasoning capabili-
ties. Such enhancements are particularly relevant for clinician-
assistive tools and patient-facing applications where factual
accuracy and conservative risk management are paramount.
Privacy Guarantees. All DP models achieve AUC-ROC
values between 0.510–0.555 across six distinct MIA families,
statistically indistinguishable from random guessing. DP-SGD
applied consistently across all training stages ensures com-
prehensive privacy protection, as confirmed by identical MIA
performance between DP-SFT and PrivMedChat at matched ε.
Zero canary extractions across 25 inserted sequences further
corroborate the absence of detectable verbatim memorization.
While non-private baselines also show near-chance MIA sig-
nals under our limited-epoch regime, only DP models provide
the formal (ε, δ) guarantee that bounds worst-case influence
independent of attack strategy.
Limitations. Several limitations should be noted. First, DP-
SGD introduces substantial computational overhead due to
per-sample gradient clipping, noise addition, and multi-stage
privacy accounting. Second, the construction of the preference
data continues to depend on proxy reward models and a limited
amount of expert annotation. Third, although our evaluation is
extensive, it is primarily benchmark-driven and may not fully
capture real-world clinical distribution shifts or integration
into existing workflows. Fourth, our privacy analysis, while
comprehensive, is inherently empirical and constrained by
finite samples; the theoretical guarantees remain valid, but
adaptive or future attacks may require ongoing examination
Ethical and Deployment Considerations. Differential pri-
vacy significantly mitigates the risk of memorization but
does not provide a fully comprehensive solution. Responsi-
ble deployment requires complementary safeguards, including
human oversight, continuous monitoring, explicit escalation
protocols, institutional review board (IRB) approval where
applicable, and governance frameworks. Formal privacy ac-
counting should be regarded as one component of a broader
framework encompassing safety, regulatory compliance, and
clinical validation, rather than as a standalone certification.
Future Work. Promising research directions include stage-
specific privacy budget allocation, the development of adaptive
or more robust membership inference attacks, the extension
to multimodal medical inputs, and prospective studies with
clinician-in-the-loop evaluation. Improving uncertainty quan-
tification and refusal mechanisms under DP noise remains a
critical step toward safer real-world deployment of privacy-
preserving medical LLMs.
VI. RELATED WORK
We review prior work along three dimensions motivat-
ing PrivMedChat: (i) medical dialogue systems and domain
adaptation of large language models, (ii) preference-based
alignment methods such as RLHF, and (iii) formal privacy
mechanisms for language modeling. Existing medical chatbots
focus on utility and safety but lack end-to-end privacy guar-
antees. In contrast, differentially private fine-tuning for LLMs
rarely addresses preference optimization and reward model-
ing. This gap motivates our end-to-end DP-RLHF framework
for medical dialogue alignment, which integrates scalable
preference construction with rigorous privacy accounting and
empirical leakage evaluation.
A. Medical Dialogue Systems and LLM Fine-Tuning
The development of medical dialogue systems has ad-
vanced significantly with the adaptation of LLMs to clin-
ical applications. Initial research centered on task-specific
models trained on doctor-patient conversation datasets, such
as MedDialog [26], which provides large-scale de-identified
dialogues for supervised fine-tuning. Subsequent studies, in-
cluding MedAlpaca [27] and ChatDoctor [2], demonstrated
that domain-adaptive pre-training or instruction fine-tuning on
medical corpora—such as PubMed abstracts, clinical notes,
and online consultations—substantially improves diagnostic
reasoning and response quality. More recent models, including
BioMistral [3] and OpenBioLLMs [28], build upon open-
source backbones (Mistral, Llama-3) fine-tuned on biomedical
literature and synthetic dialogues, achieving strong results on
benchmarks such as MedQA [29] and HealthSearchQA [30].

9
These systems prioritize personalization by conditioning
responses on patient history and symptoms. However, they
depend heavily on SFT or instruction tuning on potentially
sensitive data, which raises significant privacy concerns that
remain largely unresolved.
B. RLHF in Healthcare
RLHF has become a central approach in aligning LLMs,
enabling them to generate outputs that are helpful, safe, and
aligned with user preferences [4], [31]. In general domains,
RLHF pipelines—comprising reward modeling from human
preferences and policy optimization via PPO [5] or Direct
Policy Optimization (DPO) [32]—have underpinned systems
such as InstructGPT [4] and Llama-3 [33].
In the healthcare domain, however, RLHF adoption re-
mains limited due to substantial costs and ethical complexities
associated with collecting clinician preferences data. Pro-
posed alternatives include reward modeling from synthetic
preferences [34] or rule-based signals, as demonstrated by
ClinicalCamel [35], as well as Reinforcement Learning from
AI Feedback (RLAIF) methods that employ LLM-as-judge for
ranking [36]. Efforts like PMC-LLaMA [37], and CareBot [38]
optimize medical instruction datasets to improve safety and
empathy. However, none provide formal privacy guarantees
during preference-based alignment.
C. Differential Privacy in Language Models
DP defends against memorization and inference attacks
in machine learning [8]. In LLMs, DP-SGD [9] has been
successfully applied in pre-training [9] and fine-tuning [10],
with ghost clipping and parameter-efficient methods (e.g.,
LoRA [16]) mitigating utility loss [39]. Recent advances
explore DP at various granularities—such as example-level,
user-level—and stages, showing viable privacy-utility trade-
offs in general text tasks [40], [11]. However, extending DP
to the full RLHF pipeline—particularly reward modeling and
PPO [5]/DPO [32]—remains underexplored due to heightened
noise sensitivity in preference optimization [41]. To our knowl-
edge, no prior work has applied end-to-end DP-RLHF in the
medical domain.
D. Privacy Attacks and Defenses in Health Informatics
LLMs trained on private data are vulnerable to MIA [42],
[7], attribute inference, and training data extraction [7], with
demonstrated risks in biomedical text [43]. In health infor-
matics, these threats are amplified by regulatory requirements
(HIPAA, GDPR) and the sensitivity of protected health in-
formation [44]. Defenses beyond DP include redaction, syn-
thetic data generation [45], and auditing frameworks [46],
but empirical evaluations in medical dialogue systems are
sparse. Benchmarks show substantial memorization in domain-
adapted medical LLMs [12], highlighting the need for rigorous
privacy during fine-tuning and alignment.
Our work bridges these areas by introducing the first dif-
ferentially private RLHF framework tailored for personalized
medical dialogue, with scalable annotation-free preference
construction and comprehensive clinical evaluation.
VII. CONCLUSION
We introduced PrivMedChat, an end-to-end differentially
private RLHF framework for privacy-preserving medical dia-
logue alignment. PrivMedChat applies DP-SGD to supervised
fine-tuning, reward modeling, and PPO policy optimization,
providing formal (ε, δ) guarantees with competitive utility and
enhanced safety. On the held-out test set, the ε=7 variant
achieves the best privacy-utility-safety balance among DP
models, with the highest ROUGE-L and Entity F1 scores,
reduced hallucinations, near-zero harmful advice, and robust
empirical privacy, including near-chance performance in six
membership inference attacks and zero canary extractions.
These results show that privacy-preserving alignment of clin-
ical LLMs is practical and effective, recovering most utility
lost to DP noise while strengthening safety-critical behaviors.
The framework is readily extensible to private preference
optimization in other high-stakes domains.

APPENDIX A
PRELIMINARIES
This section reviews the privacy and alignment concepts
underlying PrivMedChat.
A. Differential Privacy
Let D = {zi}n
i=1 denote a dataset of n records. In our
setting, each record zi corresponds to a training example de-
rived from a doctor–patient dialogue (e.g., a single supervised
input–output pair or a single preference pair). Two datasets
D and D′ are adjacent if they differ in exactly one record.
Unless stated otherwise, we consider example-level adjacency;
we discuss conversation-level adjacency in Sec. A-A2.
Definition 1 ((ε, δ)-Differential Privacy [8]). A randomized
mechanism M : D →R satisfies (ε, δ)-DP if for all adjacent
datasets D, D′ and all measurable sets S ⊆R,
Pr[M(D) ∈S] ≤eε Pr[M(D′) ∈S] + δ.
Intuitively, (ε, δ)-DP upper-bounds how much the presence
of any single record can change the distribution of the mecha-
nism’s output. Smaller ε implies stronger privacy; δ is typically
set to a negligible value (e.g., δ = 10−5 in our experiments).

11
1) DP-SGD for neural network training: We train our
models with differential privacy using DP-SGD [9]. In each
iteration, DP-SGD [9] computes per-example gradients, clips
each gradient to an ℓ2 norm bound (clipping norm C) to
control sensitivity, then adds Gaussian noise proportional to C
(scaled by the noise multiplier σ) to the aggregated minibatch
gradient before applying the update. We use Opacus [15] for
the DP-SGD [9] implementation and privacy accounting, and
report (ε, δ) using an RDP accountant [17].
2) Adjacency choices and clinical data granularity: While
our default definition uses example-level adjacency, health-
care deployments may demand stronger notions such as
conversation-level or user-level DP, where adjacency corre-
sponds to adding/removing all examples from a single patient
or a single dialogue session. Formally, let D = Sm
u=1 Du be
a partition of records by user/session u. User-level adjacency
treats D and D′ as adjacent if one entire group Du is added or
removed. This setting typically increases effective sensitivity
and thus requires either larger noise or fewer training steps
to achieve the same ε. In PrivMedChat, we mitigate cross-
session leakage in evaluation by split construction (group-
aware partitioning) and reserve user-level accounting as a
direct extension when deployment requirements demand it.
B. Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback
RLHF [4] aligns a generative policy πθ(y | x) with human
or clinical preferences. Let x denote an input prompt (e.g.,
patient message plus context) and y a candidate response.
Standard RLHF [4] proceeds via (i) supervised fine-tuning
(SFT), (ii) reward modeling from preference pairs, and (iii)
policy optimization with a KL-regularized RL objective.
1) Supervised fine-tuning: SFT initializes the policy by
maximizing the conditional likelihood of reference responses:

In medical dialogue, SFT primarily teaches domain style,
common clinical reasoning patterns, and appropriate tone.
2) Preference modeling and reward learning: Reward mod-
eling uses preference pairs (x, yw, yl) where yw is preferred
over yl (“winner” vs. “loser”). A reward model rϕ(x, y) ∈R is
trained to satisfy rϕ(x, yw) > rϕ(x, yl). A common objective
is the logistic (Bradley–Terry) loss:
LRM(ϕ) = −E(x,yw,yl) [log σ (rϕ(x, yw) −rϕ(x, yl))] ,
where σ(·) is the sigmoid. The learned reward model in-
duces an ordering over responses that approximates clinical
preferences (e.g., safety, correctness, empathy, appropriate
escalation).
3) Policy optimization with PPO: Given a fixed reward
model rϕ, we optimize the policy to maximize expected
reward while constraining deviation from a reference policy
πref (typically the SFT model). This constraint is critical in
medical settings to prevent reward hacking and regressions in
safety.

C. Parameter-Efficient Fine-Tuning
Large medical LLMs are expensive to fine-tune under
DP because DP-SGD [9] requires per-example gradients and
introduces noise. We therefore adopt Low-Rank Adaptation
(LoRA) [16] to reduce the number of trainable parameters
while preserving expressivity.
For a linear projection with weight matrix W ∈Rdout×din,
LoRA re-parameterizes updates as
W ′ = W + ∆W,
∆W = BA,
where A ∈Rr×din, B ∈Rdout×r, and r ≪min(din, dout) is the
rank. The pretrained W is frozen and only (A, B) are trained.
1) LoRA under DP-SGD: Training only LoRA [16] pa-
rameters reduces (i) memory footprint, (ii) per-step compute
for per-example gradients, and (iii) the dimensionality of the
parameter space into which DP noise is injected. While DP
guarantees are independent of parameter count, in practice
LoRA [16] can improve the privacy–utility trade-off by en-
abling larger effective batch sizes and more stable optimization
under clipping and noise.
