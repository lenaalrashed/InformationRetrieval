Abstract—Prior work has shown that large language models can
express diverse personality traits in open-ended text generation,
but it is unclear whether they can do so in goal-directed dialogue
without harming task completion, and whether adapting to a user’s
personality improves the interaction. We study these questions
in task-oriented dialogue (TOD) with a training-free framework
in which a personality-conditioned user agent interacts with a
system agent under three conditions: Neutral (no personality
information), Try (adapt to cues observed in the dialogue), and
Oracle (the user’s trait label is given explicitly). Across GPT-4o,
Qwen3-Next-80B, and Gemini 2.0 Flash, two Schema-Guided
Dialogue domains, and ten Big Five poles (9,000 dialogues), each
scored by two independent LLM judges, user agents express most
traits while task performance remains high (89.94% completion,
85.27% success). The exceptions are Introversive and Closed: they
manifest mainly as absent behavior and are rarely detected. Both
adaptive conditions improve pairwise user satisfaction and lower
truthfulness, revealing a personalization–grounding trade-off, but
only cue-responsive Try also improves constraint satisfaction and
inform rate. How adaptation is implemented matters: Oracle’s
gains depend on how visibly the trait is expressed, and for
Disagreeable users the label leads Qwen3 and Gemini to mirror
hostility. Try avoids this failure, its satisfaction gains do not depend
on trait visibility, and it is preferred over Oracle overall. A 175-
pair human study supports the reliability of the LLM judges:
on clear-winner cases, annotators agree with the satisfaction
judges more often than with each other. Our results suggest that
responding to observable behavior is a more robust basis for
social adaptation than conditioning on psychological labels. 1

I. INTRODUCTION
Large language models are increasingly used as assistants
in settings where the quality of the interaction matters as
much as the correctness of the final answer. Users want
systems that not only complete tasks but also communicate in
ways that feel appropriate, responsive, and personally aligned.
Personalization has therefore become a central goal for dialogue
agents, particularly in customer support, booking, and assistant
scenarios, where interaction style can shape user trust and
satisfaction [1], [2].
Prior work has shown that LLMs can express diverse
personality traits in open-ended text generation. They imitate
recognizable personality patterns in questionnaires, narratives,
role-play, and controlled generation [3]–[8], exhibit measurable
Big Five traits [9], and maintain persona-consistent patterns

in generated text. However, most evaluations study personality
expression outside goal-directed dialogue. It therefore remains
unclear whether LLMs can express personality while pursuing
a concrete task, and whether adapting to the user’s personality
actually improves the interaction.
This question is especially important in task-oriented dia-
logue (TOD), where a system helps a user accomplish a goal
through multi-turn interaction. Unlike open-ended conversation,
TOD systems must satisfy schema constraints, gather required
slot values, issue valid API calls, and complete the user’s
task [10]–[12]. Personality-aware behavior may improve the
interaction by making the system more responsive to the
user’s communication style, uncertainty, or preferences. It may
also introduce risks: adapting too strongly to personality cues
could weaken grounding, distort recommendations, or interfere
with task completion. Personality-aware TOD thus requires
balancing personalization against task-grounded behavior.
Motivated by this gap, we study three research questions.
RQ1: In a two-agent TOD interaction, can the user agent realize
a target personality trait while the system maintains schema-
guided task performance? RQ2: How does access to the user’s
personality affect system quality and pairwise user satisfaction?
RQ3: How does the strength of user trait realization shape the
satisfaction gains from personality conditioning, and does this
relationship differ between explicit (Oracle) and implicit (Try)
conditioning?
To answer these questions, we build a training-free frame-
work for testing how personality adaptation affects LLM
agents in schema-guided TOD. We compare three system
conditions: Neutral, where the system receives no personality
information; Try, where it is asked to adapt to the user’s
personality from dialogue cues alone; and Oracle, where it is
given the user’s target trait explicitly. This design isolates the
effect of personality access while keeping domain structure,
schemas, and task requirements fixed. We evaluate GPT-
4o, Qwen3-Next-80B, and Gemini 2.0 Flash on Hotel and
Restaurant dialogues from the Schema-Guided Dialogue (SGD)
dataset [10], covering the Big Five traits and their opposite
poles (9,000 dialogues). We assess the dialogues along five
dimensions: task outcomes, personality realization, system
quality, system adaptation, and pairwise user satisfaction. To
reduce dependence on a single evaluator, every judge-based
metric is scored by two independent LLM judges (Gemma-
4-31B and GPT-5-mini) and averaged. Figure 1 summarizes

Our findings answer the three questions as follows. For RQ1,
user agents express most traits while the system maintains
strong task performance (89.94% completion, 85.27% success);
only Introversive and Closed are rarely detected, consistent
with traits defined by absent behavior being hard to observe
in short task dialogue. For RQ2, personality access makes
users more satisfied: averaged over two judges, both Oracle
(57% vs. 39%) and Try (60% vs. 35%) are preferred over
Neutral, and Try is slightly preferred over Oracle (52% vs.
43%). Only Try significantly improves constraint satisfaction
and inform rate, while both adaptive conditions reduce truth-
fulness, revealing a tension between personalization and task
grounding. Task-success gains appear only in the preference-
rich Restaurant domain. For RQ3, the two strategies diverge.
Oracle’s satisfaction gains scale with how visibly the user
expresses the trait (Spearman ρ=0.69), whereas Try’s do not
(ρ=0.13). Oracle falls below Neutral when the trait is barely
visible (Introversive, Closed), and for Disagreeable users it
leads Qwen3 and Gemini to mirror hostility (Oracle loses to
Neutral 32% vs. 68%), while Try still beats Neutral in all three
cases. A condition-blind System Adaptation Score confirms that
both strategies change system behavior (Neutral 2.22, Try 2.70,
Oracle 3.40), mostly for expressive traits, and that Oracle does
not make the user’s trait itself more visible. A 175-pair human
study supports both satisfaction judges: annotators agree with
the judges more often than with each other, both including ties
(59.0% vs. 47.4%; κ=0.337 vs. 0.189) and on clear-winner
cases (76.3% vs. 65.1%; κ=0.525 vs. 0.304), rising to 83.2%
(κ=0.663) when the two judges agree with each other.
Together, these results support a design principle for social
agents: adapting to observable behavior is more robust than
conditioning on an explicit psychological label.
II. RELATED WORK
LLM-based Task-Oriented Dialogue. Task-oriented dialogue
(TOD) research has traditionally focused on systems that track
dialogue state, fill slots, predict system actions, and ground
responses in structured APIs or knowledge sources. Progress
in this area has depended heavily on high-quality annotated
datasets such as MultiWOZ and Schema-Guided Dialogue
(SGD), which support benchmarking across domains but also
highlight the cost and brittleness of manual annotation [10],
[11], [13], [14]. Benchmarks such as schema adherence, state
tracking, and completion of user goals, which formalize the
core requirements of TOD systems, remain central even when
the generator is an LLM. Recent work has increasingly replaced
fully supervised pipelines with LLM-based approaches that
use in-context learning or reduced annotation to generalize
to new domains more efficiently [12]. This shift improves
flexibility, but the primary focus remains task success, slot
accuracy, and robustness under domain shift rather than user-
specific adaptation. As a result, prior TOD work provides
strong foundations for structured interaction, but leaves open
how personality cues alter task completion, grounding, and
user satisfaction in schema-constrained settings.
Personality and Persona Control in LLMs. A separate
line of work studies whether large language models can
express stable personality traits or personas. Prior studies
evaluate personality signals through questionnaire-style probes,
narrative generation, and controlled prompting, showing that
LLMs can imitate recognizable trait patterns and exhibit
measurable variation across personality dimensions [3]–[7].
Related work on role-playing and agent simulation further
suggests that LLMs can maintain social roles and behavioral
framing over longer interactions [1], [2]. Other studies move
from measuring personality to actively inducing or editing
it, for example through prompt design, persona conditioning,
or model-level control mechanisms [8], [15]. However, these
studies mostly evaluate personality realization in open-ended
or non-task settings. They therefore show that personality can
be represented and manipulated in LLM outputs, but do not
establish how personality conditioning interacts with schema
constraints, API decisions, or multi-turn task completion.
Personalized Dialogue and User Modeling. Personalized
dialogue research studies how systems adapt to user character-
istics, preferences, or inferred conversational tendencies. Recent
surveys frame this direction as part of LLM personalization,
where models condition on user-specific context, such as
facts, style, persona descriptions, or trait-like profiles, to make
conversations more relevant and coherent over time [15]–[17].
Recent LLM-based work has also explored persona-aware
dialogue generation, where user profiles or dialogue history are
used to improve persona consistency across interactions [18].
This line of work is related to our focus on personality-aware
interaction, but it is not designed for schema-guided task-
oriented dialogue, where systems must satisfy slot constraints,
issue valid API calls, and complete user goals. In parallel,
LLM-based TOD user simulation uses prompted LLMs to
generate goal-driven user behavior for training or evaluating
dialogue systems [19], [20].
III. PROPOSED FRAMEWORK
We build a controlled framework for evaluating personality-
aware task-oriented dialogue on the Schema-Guided Dialogue
(SGD) dataset [10]. An LLM-based user agent interacts with
an LLM-based system agent under matched task settings. The
task schema, domain, demonstration dialogue, and generation
rules are held fixed across conditions; the only experimental
variable is the system agent’s access to personality information
(Neutral, Try, or Oracle; Section I).
A. Task-Oriented Dialogue Framework
Each dialogue is grounded in an SGD task schema that
specifies the domain, intents, slots, and API structure required to
complete the task. Both agents are prompted one-shot, without
fine-tuning: at each turn t, generation is conditioned on an
in-domain demonstration dialogue, the schema and API rules,
operational instructions, and the dialogue history up to t.

B. Personality Trait Specification
We model user personality with the Big Five framework,
which captures broad behavioral variation along five dimensions
[7], [9], [21]–[23]. To cover both poles of each dimension, we
use ten target traits: Extraversion and Introversive, Agreeable-
ness and Disagreeable, Conscientiousness and Unconscientious,
Neuroticism and Stable, and Openness and Closed. Trait
descriptions follow the Personality Prompting (P2) descriptions
of [6], for both the five canonical traits and their opposite
poles.
Each user agent is assigned exactly one target trait and is
instructed to express it implicitly through its behavior, without
naming it. This lets us test whether personality can be realized
in task-oriented interaction while the system still satisfies
schema and API constraints.
C. Prompting and Personality-Access Conditions
Both agents share the same prompt structure: task instruc-
tions, domain schema and API specification, an in-domain
demonstration dialogue, operational rules, and the dialogue
history. Personality information is added only where the
condition requires it.
The user agent receives the target trait and its description,
which guide how the user asks questions, reacts to recommen-
dations, handles uncertainty, and decides whether to book.
The system agent is always instructed to remain task-valid,
grounded, and schema-compliant: it issues API calls only
after the required slots are collected, uses only schema fields
and search results, and confirms key slots before finalizing
a reservation. The three conditions differ only in personality
access:
• Neutral: the system receives no personality information.
• Try: the system is instructed to adapt to the user’s
personality as revealed in the dialogue, without receiving
a label.
• Oracle: the system receives the user’s target trait and its
description.
In both personality-aware conditions, the system may adjust
its level of detail, reassurance, recommendation style, or
clarification strategy. We do not test whether Try recovers
the latent trait, so we interpret it as cue-responsive adaptation:
the system responds to observable behavior such as verbosity,
hesitation, or hostility.
IV. EXPERIMENTS
A. Datasets
We use the Restaurant and Hotel domains of the Schema-
Guided Dialogue (SGD) dataset [10]. Both involve schema-
guided search and reservation, but they differ on the axis most

relevant to personality adaptation. Restaurant is preference-
driven (cuisine, price range, ambiance), giving the system
latitude to tailor recommendations to the user, whereas Hotel
is constraint-driven (location, star rating, rooms), leaving less
room to adapt. This contrast lets us examine whether the
benefits of personality access depend on how much latitude
the task provides. Before generation, we remove duplicate
and inconsistent service records from both databases without
altering the task schemas.
B. Experimental Setup
We use the same experimental structure for GPT-4o, Qwen3-
Next-80B, and Gemini 2.0 Flash, with only minor model-
specific prompt formatting to ensure valid API calls. For each
combination of domain, model, access condition, and target
trait, we generate 50 dialogues, yielding 9,000 dialogues in
total. The user always initiates the interaction, and a dialogue
ends when the user produces the scripted closing phrase after
completing the task, or when it reaches 70 turns or 5 API
calls. Dialogue generation uses temperature 0.7, top-p 1.0,
and at most 250 output tokens per call. GPT-4o is accessed
through OpenAI, Gemini 2.0 Flash and GPT-5-mini through
OpenRouter, and Qwen3-Next-80B and Gemma 4 through
Ollama Cloud.
C. Evaluation Metrics
We evaluate the dialogues with automatic and LLM-judged
metrics. To reduce dependence on a single evaluator, every
judged metric is scored independently by two LLM judges,
Gemma 4 and GPT-5-mini, and we report the average of the
two.
1) Task outcomes. Both task outcomes are computed au-
tomatically from the dialogue, without an LLM judge.
Dialogue Completion equals 1 if the dialogue ends with
the scripted user phrase “Thanks a lot! That’s all I need”
before reaching the 70-turn or 5-API-call limit, and 0
otherwise. Task Success equals 1 if the final reservation
APICall is issued with the correct required slot values,
and 0 otherwise. The two can diverge: a dialogue may end
naturally without the user ever requesting a reservation
(Completion = 1, Success = 0), and a dialogue that
exhausts its turn or API-call budget has Completion = 0.
2) Personality realization. The User Trait Score measures
how visibly the user expresses the target trait. The judge
reads the dialogue, identifies the three most evident user
traits among the ten candidates, and rates each on 1–5. We
report the score of the intended trait, or 0 if it is not among
the top three, giving an effective range of 0–5. Near-zero
values mean the trait is rarely among the most visible
behaviors. We separately verify that each trait prompt
points in the intended Big Five direction with a 44-item
BFI probe [24], answered on a 1–5 Likert scale from the
trait description rather than from generated dialogues.
3) System quality. The judge scores three dimensions on
1–5 (higher is better). Constraint Satisfaction measures
whether the final recommendation or booking fulfills the
user’s active constraints. Inform Rate measures whether the
system provides the requested information correctly and as
supported by the dialogue and search results. Truthfulness
measures whether the system avoids unsupported or
fabricated claims. The judge is given the fields available
in each domain’s database, and any specific claim beyond
these fields that the dialogue does not support counts as
a hallucination.
4) Pairwise user satisfaction. We pair dialogues matched
on domain, model, and target trait but differing in access
condition (3,000 pairs per comparison), and the judge
selects which system the user would likely find more
satisfying, or a tie. We report win rates for Oracle vs.
Neutral, Try vs. Neutral, and Try vs. Oracle. The judge
is instructed to ignore task success (measured separately),
not to penalize empty search results unless the system
handles them poorly, not to favor shorter dialogues, and
not to treat the scripted closing phrase as evidence of
satisfaction. To control for position bias, both judges see
each pair in a randomly assigned A/B order. Because no
real user with the target personality rates the interaction,
this is an observer judgment of presumed satisfaction,
which we validate against human annotators in §V-D.
5) System adaptation. The System Adaptation Score (1–5)
measures how well the system adapts its tone, pacing,
reassurance, and recommendation style to the user’s target
trait. The judge sees the dialogue and the target trait but not
the access condition that produced it, so its score reflects
observable system behavior rather than the presence of
personality information in the system prompt.
V. RESULTS AND ANALYSIS
We answer the three research questions from Section I
by comparing Neutral, Try, and Oracle on task performance,
personality realization, system quality, and pairwise satisfaction
in both domains. Statistical tests are reported within each
RQ, and §V-D validates the satisfaction judges against human
annotators.

B. RQ2: Effect of Personality Conditioning on System Quality
and User Satisfaction
Table II compares system-quality metrics and pairwise
satisfaction preferences from two LLM judges across traits,
averaged over domains and LLMs. Try achieves the highest
average Constraint Satisfaction (4.530) and Inform Rate (4.720),
followed by Oracle (4.489 and 4.694), while Neutral is lowest
on both (4.409 and 4.638). Because conditions are simulated
independently, we test differences with Wilcoxon signed-rank
tests over the 60 matched domain × LLM × trait cells (Holm-
corrected). Try significantly improves Constraint Satisfaction
(p = .002) and Inform Rate (p = .004) over Neutral, whereas
Oracle’s gains are not significant. Both adaptive conditions
significantly reduce Truthfulness (Try p = .001; Oracle
p < .001), a drop that holds under each judge separately,
revealing a tension between personalization and task grounding.
Personality-aware conditions are also preferred in pairwise
comparisons. Averaged over two judges, Oracle outperforms
Neutral 57% to 39% and Try leads Neutral 60% to 35%
meaning that personality aware systems can make users more
satisfied. Comparing the two adaptive strategies directly, Try is
slightly preferred over Oracle overall (52% vs. 43%). Oracle
performs well against Neutral for Openness (82% vs. 17%),
Extraversion (73% vs. 26%), and Agreeableness (70% vs. 25%),
where the target style is socially clear and easy to support. Try
is preferred over Oracle for Introversive, Disagreeable, Closed,
Unconscientious, and Neuroticism. The Disagreeable trait is
the sharpest example: Oracle underperforms Neutral (32% vs.
68%), while Try remains competitive (55% vs. 44%) and is
strongly preferred over Oracle (77% vs. 23%). Under both
judges, this collapse under Oracle is driven by Qwen3 and
Gemini (O/N between 10/90 and 28/72 across domains), which
mirror the hostile user tone rather than adapting constructively,
a failure less pronounced under Try, which responds to observed
behavior rather than committing to an explicit label. GPT-4o
does not exhibit this failure (O/N 42/55 in Hotel and 71/28 in
Restaurant). Human raters agree with the judge’s preferences
on clear-winner cases (§V-D).
Domain characteristics modulate these gains. Personality
access improves Task Success in Restaurant (Neutral→Oracle:
78.07%→82.80%, with gains for both GPT-4o and Gemini) but
slightly reduces it in Hotel (91.53%→88.87%). We hypothesize
that this reflects preference latitude: Restaurant choices depend
on cuisine, price, and ambiance, giving the system room to
adapt, whereas Hotel choices are constrained by location, rating,
and rooms. With two domains, however, preference latitude
is confounded with other domain differences, and testing it
requires domains that vary along this axis.
Taken together, Try provides the strongest overall balance:
the highest Constraint Satisfaction, Inform Rate, and user
satisfaction over Oracle and Neutral, and no tone-mirroring
failure of the kind Oracle triggers for disagreeable users in
Qwen3 and Gemini. Gains are largest in Restaurant domain
which is a preference-rich domain.

Qwen3 and Gemini (two models × two domains) raises the cell-
level correlation to ρ = +0.633 (p < .001, n = 56), indicating
that mirroring is a model-specific prompt-following failure
rather than a general property of personality conditioning.

whether its tone, pacing, reassurance, directness, flexibility

A Friedman test rules out the alternative explanation that
Oracle gains simply reflect improved trait detectability. User
Trait Score, averaged over the two judges, does not differ
significantly across conditions at the trait×LLM×domain cell
level (n = 60; χ2 = 1.14, df = 2, p = 0.566; all pairwise post-
hoc p ≥0.56 after Holm correction; means: Neutral = 3.53,
Try = 3.50, Oracle = 3.52). The result holds for each judge
separately (Gemma 4: χ2 = 0.21, p = 0.902; GPT-5-mini:
χ2 = 2.85, p = 0.240). Oracle does not make the user’s trait
more visible — it only helps when the trait was already visible.
This is the label–behavior match effect: explicit conditioning
works best when user behavior already provides evidence for
the supplied label. Try’s satisfaction gains being insensitive to
realization strength is consistent with responding to observable
cues rather than to the trait label itself.
D. Human Evaluation
We validated the pairwise satisfaction judges, our most
subjective metric, through a human study on Prolific using
Qualtrics. We sampled 175 dialogue pairs stratified across
the three comparison types, ten traits, three LLMs, and both
domains, with two annotators per pair (350 ratings; $12/hour).
Annotators chose which system the user would find more
satisfying, or “About the same,” using the judges’ criteria:
helpfulness, tone, responsiveness, clarity, and fit to the user’s
communication style, excluding task success. Access condition
and LLM identity were hidden, and dialogue order was
randomized. We compare annotators with both LLM judges
(Gemma 4 and GPT-5-mini) and report the average over the
two judges.
Including ties, humans agreed with the LLM judges in
59.0% of ratings on average (κ=0.337; Gemma 4 59.7%,
κ=0.342; GPT-5-mini 58.3%, κ=0.331), above inter-annotator
agreement (47.4%, κ=0.189). Much of the remaining gap
reflects calibration: the judges rarely call a tie (Gemma 4 0.6%,
GPT-5-mini 7.4% of pairs), whereas annotators did so in 22%
of ratings. On the no-tie subset, agreement rises to 76.3% on
average (κ=0.525; Gemma 4 76.8%, κ=0.535, n=271; GPT-
5-mini 75.8%, κ=0.515, n=256), against 65.1% (κ=0.304)
between annotators. Where the two judges agree with each
other (74% of pairs), agreement with humans on the no-tie
subset reaches 83.2% (κ=0.663). Only 7–8 of 175 pairs (<5%)
had both annotators agreeing with each other against a judge,
with no systematic pattern across conditions or traits. Both
judges therefore track human observers when a clear winner
exists, supporting the trends in §V-B.
VI. CONCLUSION
We studied whether LLMs can express personality in
goal-directed task-oriented dialogue without weakening task
completion, and whether adapting to the user’s personality
improves interaction quality. We used a controlled, training-
free two-agent framework evaluated across three LLMs, two
SGD domains, and the Big Five traits with their opposite-pole
variants, and scored every metric with two independent LLM
judges (Gemma 4 and GPT-5-mini).
LLM agents can express personality while maintaining
strong task performance, but realization is uneven. Expressive
traits such as Neuroticism and Extraversion are detected
almost perfectly, whereas Introversive and Closed remain
near-undetectable. This is consistent with behavioral absence
carrying little lexical signal in short, schema-constrained
dialogue. Personality adaptation raises pairwise user satisfaction
under both adaptive conditions. Cue-responsive adaptation (Try)
also improves Constraint Satisfaction and Inform Rate, whereas
explicit conditioning (Oracle) does not. Both conditions reduce
Truthfulness, revealing a tension between personalization and
task grounding. The gains in task metrics and satisfaction are
larger in the preference-driven Restaurant domain, where the
system has more latitude to adapt.
How adaptation is implemented matters most. Explicit
conditioning in Oracle follows a label–behavior match effect:
Oracle does not make the user’s trait more visible, yet its
gains in both condition-blind adaptation scores and satisfaction
concentrate on traits whose behavior is already visible, as
do judge-free elaboration counts. For Disagreeable users, the

label triggers strong tone mirroring in Qwen3 and Gemini,
which judges rate as highly adapted but users would find
less satisfying. Cue-responsive adaptation in Try avoids this
failure and is preferred over Oracle overall. These findings
are consistent across both judges, and the judges are reliable
proxies for human observers: annotators agree with the judges
more often than with each other, both including ties (59.0% vs.
47.4%; κ=0.337 vs. 0.189) and on clear-winner cases (76.3%
vs. 65.1%; κ=0.525 vs. 0.304), rising to 83.2% when the
two judges agree. Overall, responding to observable behavior
offers a more robust and practically deployable route to
personality-aware task-oriented dialogue than conditioning on
psychological labels. Validating this principle with real users
and domains spanning a wider range of preference latitude is
the key next step for future research.
