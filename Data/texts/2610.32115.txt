EDWARD-NMT: Elastic Dimensions With Adaptive Routing and
Dual-consistency for Neural Machine Translation
Edward Nguyen*, Thai Tran*, Kiet Anh Hoang*, Duy Hoang*, Duy Vo, Khanh Le, Vu Le
UNEY, Switzerland
{edward.nguyen, thai.tt, kiet.ha, duy.hv, duy.vhb, khanh.ld, vu.lt}@uney.com
Abstract
Sparse Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) is a pow-
erful paradigm for scaling Neural Machine
Translation (NMT) and managing language
heterogeneity. However, practical limitations
hinder its deployment on resource-constrained
devices: the entire expert pool must remain
in memory, and the per-token compute bud-
get is statically fixed during training by rigid
top-k routing. We introduce EDWARD-NMT
(Elastic Dimensions With Adaptive Routing
and Dual-consistency for NMT), a novel MoE
encoder-decoder architecture designed for a
“train once, deploy anywhere” paradigm that
exposes a family of deployable sub-networks
at inference time. EDWARD-NMT integrates
three key innovations: (i) Matryoshka-style
elastic dimensions to flexibly reduce the mem-
ory footprint; (ii) an adaptive sigmoid router
with a learned, task-conditioned threshold, al-
lowing each token to dynamically determine its
active expert count; and (iii) a dual-consistency
objective to maximize the performance of the
extracted sub-networks. Extensive experiments
on PhoMT (En↔Vi), WMT-14 (En↔De),
multilingual OPUS-16, and a multi-domain
De↔En benchmark validate our approach.
The full-capacity architecture matches or ex-
ceeds strong MoE baselines with improved
parameter efficiency. Furthermore, the sub-
networks seamlessly extracted from a single
checkpoint consistently outperform compara-
bly sized dense Transformers without any re-
training.

Introduction
Managing language and domain heterogeneity is a
fundamental challenge in Neural Machine Transla-
tion (NMT) (Britz et al., 2017; Koehn and Knowles,
2017; Pham et al., 2021), as training corpora rou-
tinely mix diverse lexical and stylistic distributions
(Stahlberg, 2020; Gain et al., 2026). The Mixture-
*Equal contribution.
of-Experts (MoE) paradigm addresses this by rout-
ing tokens to conditionally activated experts, scal-
ing capacity to specialize in distinct patterns while
keeping per-token computation tractable (Shazeer
et al., 2017; Fedus et al., 2022; Dai et al., 2022;
Zhang et al., 2024; Liang et al., 2025).
Despite state-of-the-art performance, MoEs face
deployment bottlenecks in resource-constrained en-
vironments. First, the entire expert pool must reside
in memory (Hoffmann et al., 2022; Xu et al., 2023).
While lightweight adapters reduce this overhead
(Zhang et al., 2024), they still yield static, hardware-
specific models. Second, standard MoEs rely on
rigid top-k routing, applying an identical compute
budget regardless of token complexity (Jawahar
et al., 2023; Guo et al., 2025). Although Neural
Architecture Search can design heterogeneous sub-
networks (Jawahar et al., 2023), it requires complex
offline phases. Consequently, NMT practitioners
lack a unified framework to dynamically adjust
memory and compute footprints on the fly.
To address these gaps, we propose EDWARD-
NMT (Elastic Dimensions With Adaptive Routing
and Dual-consistency for NMT), an encoder-
decoder architecture enabling a train-once, deploy-
anywhere paradigm through two coordinated forms
of adaptivity.
To overcome memory bottle-
necks, we apply Matryoshka representation learn-
ing (Kusupati et al., 2022) to expert dimensions,
allowing the MoE to be deterministically sliced
into weight-sharing sub-networks at inference. To
eliminate rigid compute constraints, we replace top-
k selection with an adaptive sigmoid router using a
learned, task-conditioned threshold, empowering
tokens to dynamically dictate their active expert
count. Finally, to ensure aggressively truncated
sub-networks maintain translation quality without
post-hoc fine-tuning, we introduce a novel in-place
dual-consistency objective that explicitly aligns pre-
dictive distributions across nested structural scales.

Related Work
Static vs. Elastic Mixture-of-Experts.
Sparse
MoEs dominate sequence model scaling (Shazeer
et al., 2017; Fedus et al., 2022; Dai et al., 2022;
Jiang et al., 2024; Liu et al., 2024) and multi-
domain NMT (Costa-Jussà et al., 2022; Zhang
et al., 2024), yet produce static models with
fixed memory footprints. Post-hoc compression
(Lagunas et al., 2021; Li et al., 2025) yields
rigid, hardware-specific models, while multi-sub-
network architectures (Cai et al., 2020; Yu et al.,
2019; Hou et al., 2020) require explicit fine-tuning.
Existing elastic designs either restrict flexibility
to dense networks (Kudugunta et al., 2024) or
limit it to token-level granularity (Cappellazzo
et al., 2026). While concurrent work (MoSE (Tas-
tan et al., 2026)) introduces slimmable experts,
it retains rigid top-k routing and employs test-
time training for width adaptation. In contrast,
EDWARD-NMT embeds Matryoshka-style elas-
ticity directly into sparse experts. By introducing
a variance-preservation scaling factor, we stabilize
activations across nested widths, allowing a sin-
gle trained MoE to yield a family of deployable
sub-networks without any post-hoc adaptation.
Dynamic Routing and Adaptive Compute.
Tra-
ditional MoEs employ rigid top-k routing, applying
identical compute budgets regardless of token com-
plexity and relying on restrictive load-balancing
losses (Clark et al., 2022). Relaxations include
soft (Puigcerver et al., 2024) and expert-choice
routing (Zhou et al., 2022), while recent methods
adapt per-token compute dynamically via null ex-
perts (AdaMoE (Zeng et al., 2024)), auto-tuned
activations (DynMoE (Guo et al., 2025)), or dif-
ferentiable ReLU routing (ReMoE (Wang et al.,
2025)). EDWARD-NMT is concurrent but distinct:
by coupling elastic experts with an adaptive sig-
moid router, our framework uniquely enables dy-
namic adjustment of both memory and compute
footprints at inference.
Self-distillation and Consistency Regularization.
To enhance model generalization and robustness,
typical consistency regularization aligns predic-
tions across perturbed forward passes or augmented
data views (Xie et al., 2020; Liang et al., 2021; Yao
et al., 2025), while self-distillation transfers knowl-
edge between layers or temporal states (Furlanello
et al., 2018; Lan et al., 2018; Mobahi et al., 2020).
Although in-place distillation successfully trains
dense elastic networks by aligning sub-networks
with a full-capacity teacher (Yu and Huang, 2019;
Cai et al., 2020), it remains confined to stati-
cally routed architectures. We extend this via a
novel dual-consistency objective tailored for elastic
MoEs. Rather than aligning augmented views, we
dynamically transfer knowledge between nested
widths using a symmetric Kullback-Leibler (Jef-
freys) divergence. This bidirectional regularization
anchors the full-capacity network while preserving
the quality of aggressively truncated variants.
Shared Experts.
To prevent sparse experts from
redundantly learning general linguistic patterns, ar-
chitectures like DeepSeek-MoE (Dai et al., 2024)
and Qwen3 (Yang et al., 2025) isolate common
knowledge into a dense, universally shared expert.
EDWARD-NMT extends this by dynamically con-
ditioning the shared expert on the translation di-
rection via FiLM modulation (Perez et al., 2018),

EDWARD-NMT
EDWARD-NMT builds upon the Transformer
encoder-decoder architecture (Vaswani et al., 2017;
Shazeer, 2020), selected over decoder-only alter-
natives for its superior parameter efficiency and
low inference latency, making it highly practical
for resource-constrained deployment. We replace
conventional feed-forward sublayers with a novel
MoE block comprising three jointly optimized in-
novations: (i) elastic experts encoding a family
of nested inner widths, (ii) an adaptive sigmoid
router dynamically allocating the optimal expert
count per token, and (iii) a dual-consistency objec-
tive aligning predictions across capacity views. Ad-
ditionally, a lightweight Direction-Aware Shared
Expert (DASE) is computed in parallel. Figure 1
provides a schematic overview.

A central challenge in elastic MoEs is ensuring
aggressively truncated sub-networks remain perfor-

capacity views together.

Experimental Setup
4.1
Datasets
We evaluate EDWARD-NMT on four benchmark
families (full dataset and preprocessing details
are provided in Appendix A.1):
(1) PhoMT
(En↔Vi) (Doan et al., 2021), (2) the standard
WMT-14 (En↔De) (Bojar et al., 2014) evaluated
on newstest2014, (3) the multi-domain De↔En
benchmark (Zhang et al., 2024) spanning five di-
verse domains evaluated via macro-averaging, and
(4) the multilingual translation OPUS-16 dataset
following Zhao et al. (2024), with 16 languages
divided into high (> 0.9M pairs), low (< 0.1M
pairs) and medium resource.

8 layers, dmodel = 512, eight attention heads, E =

dimension M = 2048, governed by the nested
family: D = {2048, 1024, 512, 256}. This yields
the four variants in Table 1. Total parameters vary

4.3
Baselines
We compare against standard dense Transformers
(BASE/BIG) (Vaswani et al., 2017; Ott et al., 2018),
mBART (Liu et al., 2020), and MoE methods: the
Switch Transformer (Fedus et al., 2022) with top-1
token-based routing (ST-MOE) and GShard (Lep-
ikhin et al., 2021) with top-2 token-based routing
(GS-MOE). Both MoE models use TRANSFORM-
ERS BIG backbone for multi-domain benchmark.
All models are trained jointly on all translation
directions to match our setup. For OPUS-16 we re-
port baseline results from Zhao et al. (2024), more
MoE baselines are provided in Appendix B.2.
4.4
Implementation Details
Training.
All models are trained on a single TPU
v5-64 pod using the AdamW optimizer (Loshchilov
and Hutter, 2019) (β1=0.9, β2=0.98). The learn-
ing rate warms up linearly from 10−6 to 10−2 over
the first 1k steps. We use an effective batch size of
8,192 sequences (max length 128), a dropout rate
of 0.1, and label smoothing of 0.1. Training spans
16k steps for the multi-domain, 30k for OPUS-16,
40k for PhoMT, and 60k for WMT-14. Complete
hyperparameters are listed in Appendix Table 10.
Inference and Evaluation.
We decode via beam
search (width 4, length penalty 0.6). Performance
is measured using case-sensitive sacreBLEU (Post,
2018) for PhoMT, WMT-14, and OPUS-16, and
tokenized BLEU (Papineni et al., 2002) for the
multi-domain dataset. We also report COMET (Rei
et al., 2020) for semantic fidelity on WMT-14 and
multi-domain dataset. chrF2 (Popovi´c, 2016) on
WMT-14 and TER (Snover et al., 2006) on PhoMT
results are detailed in Appendix B.6 and B.7. All
reported improvements over baselines are statisti-
cally significant (Appendix A.4). Edge inference
latency and throughput are profiled using compiled
LiteRT checkpoints (Appendix B.1). Crucially, all
four EDWARD-NMT variants are extracted and
evaluated from the same checkpoint.

Impact of Shared Expert Routing.
Table 7
isolates the effect of the shared expert strategy.
Compared to a standard MoE without shared ex-
perts (No SE), introducing a Common Shared Ex-
pert (Common SE) provides modest gains (+0.2
to +0.3 BLEU). However, our Direction-Aware
Shared Expert (DASE) unlocks substantial im-
provements across the board, yielding approxi-
mately +1.0 BLEU on XL and +0.6 BLEU on
S over the No SE baseline. This confirms that in-
telligently routing common knowledge is vital for
maximizing multi-domain translation quality.
Choice of Consistency Loss.
Our proposed Dual-
Consistency (DC Loss) introduces a bidirectional
alignment mechanism critical for elastic architec-
tures.
As shown in Table 8, standard R-Drop
isolates identical widths, which limits cross-scale
knowledge transfer and degrades the flagship ca-
pacity (−0.6 BLEU on L). A symmetric Jensen-

Shannon Divergence (Sym. JSD) (Lin, 1991) is
similarly ineffective, providing negligible gains
for smaller sub-networks. While unidirectional
distillation (Asym. KL) mitigates this by treating
the larger sampled width as a detached teacher, it
lacks mutual regularization. Furthermore, anchor-
ing strictly to the full-capacity model (Asym. KL
Max) yields sub-optimal intermediate variants. By
coupling capacity views - with larger variants serv-
ing as distillation targets and smaller cores acting
as regularizers - our DC Loss establishes a balance
that maximizes quality across all sub-networks.
Elasticity of Model Capacities.
EDWARD-
NMT’s structural elasticity enables fluid extrac-
tion of sub-networks at arbitrary granularities.
As detailed in Table 14 (Appendix), despite be-
ing optimized on only four discrete views (D =
{2048, 1024, 512, 256}), the capacity space seam-
lessly interpolates.
Unseen intermediate sub-
networks (e.g., D = 1536 or 768) maintain robust
translation quality and strictly follow a monotonic
compute-performance trade-off without post-hoc
fine-tuning. However, this elasticity is bounded by
the trained envelope; extrapolating below the mini-
mum trained threshold (D = 128) triggers a severe
performance breakdown, confirming that structural
integrity requires optimized geometric bounds.
Effect of Variance Preservation.
Removing the
deterministic rescaling factor ψ(Dk) consistently
degrades translation quality across all extracted
sub-networks (Table 15, Appendix).
This per-
formance drop becomes significantly more pro-
nounced at smaller capacities (−0.37 BLEU for
D = 256), confirming that explicitly stabilizing
down-projected activation magnitudes is crucial for
the robust joint training of nested structures.
6
Conclusion
We introduced EDWARD-NMT, an elastic MoE
architecture realizing an efficient train-once,
deploy-anywhere paradigm for NMT. By synergiz-
ing Matryoshka-style elastic experts, an adaptive
sigmoid router, and a direction-aware shared ex-
pert, our framework decouples the static memory
footprint from the dynamic computational budget.
Crucially, a symmetric dual-consistency objective
aligns predictive distributions across nested widths,
ensuring truncated sub-networks retain high qual-
ity without post-hoc fine-tuning. Extensive eval-
uations across PhoMT, WMT-14, multilingual
OPUS-16, and a multi-domain benchmark validate
our approach. The full-capacity EDWARD-NMT
matches or exceeds strong MoE baselines, while
the seamlessly extracted smaller sub-networks re-
main highly competitive, outperforming compara-
bly sized dense Transformers. Overall, coupling
structural elasticity with dynamic routing offers a
practical, scalable path to resource-aware sequence
modeling and adaptable on-device deployment.
Limitations
Despite its effectiveness, EDWARD-NMT has sev-
eral limitations that offer avenues for future re-
search:
Architectural Scope.
We evaluate EDWARD-
NMT strictly on encoder-decoder architectures to
demonstrate its efficacy for machine translation
targeted at resource-constrained edge devices. Ex-
tending this elastic formulation to massively multi-
lingual settings, decoder-only large language mod-
els, or broader sequence generation tasks remains
an important direction for future work.
Dimensions of Elasticity.
Our design prioritizes
structural flexibility within the feed-forward net-
works, as they constitute the vast majority of param-
eters in MoE models. Although EDWARD-NMT
achieves robust elasticity for these capacities within
the optimized training envelope, we have not yet
explored elasticity along orthogonal architectural
axes. Future work will investigate integrating early
exiting (dynamic depth) and variable hidden state
dimensions (dmodel) to realize a fully elastic, multi-
dimensional architecture capable of simultaneous
depth-and-width adaptation.
Extreme Compression.
Although EDWARD-
NMT interpolates robustly within its trained en-
velope (D), performance inherently degrades when
extrapolating below the minimum trained capacity.
Investigating continuous width formulations or dy-
namically expanding these lower bounds without
destabilizing the shared backbone remains a key
direction for extreme-compression scenarios.
Ethical Considerations
This work uses only publicly available NMT bench-
marks with no personally identifiable or sensitive
data, and all baselines are cited and reproduced un-
der their respective licenses. Like any NMT system,
EDWARD-NMT may propagate biases or inaccu-
racies from training corpora and should not inform

high-stakes decisions without human review. We
foresee no direct harmful applications; by improv-
ing parameter and compute efficiency, our work
lowers the barrier to deploying translation systems
on resource-constrained devices, improving acces-
sibility to translation technology.

Multi-domain De↔En.
Following Zhang et al.

compassing five distinct domains: KORAN, IT,
MEDICAL, LAW, and SUBTITLES. The total fil-
tered size is approximately 1.3M sentence pairs.
The tokenization and normalization process follows
the exact same Moses-based pipeline as WMT-14.
We aggregate the training sets from all five domains
across both languages to learn a single, unified BPE
model with 32, 000 merge operations. After apply-
ing the identical length and ratio filtering (maxi-
mum length of 250, length ratio of 1.5), the filtered
data from all domains is concatenated into a single
combined training set and a combined validation
set. The models are trained using a joined dictio-
nary capped at 32, 768 tokens, while evaluation
is conducted separately on the test sets (each has
2000 samples) of each domain.
Multilingual OPUS-16.
The OPUS-16 bench-
mark (Zhao et al., 2024) comprises 16 diverse lan-
guages. We prepare the training, validation, and
test data following Zhao et al. (2024). The training
and validation data are filtered to remove sentences
exceeding 100 tokens. We train a joint Sentence-
Piece unigram model with a base vocabulary of
32, 000 tokens on the training set and subsequently
apply it to encode all data splits.
A.2
Architecture
The encoder-decoder backbone has Lenc=Ldec=8
layers, dmodel=512, and h=8 heads (dh=64). We
use a Pre-Norm setup with RMSNorm (Zhang
and Sennrich, 2019) applied to token embed-
dings, layer inputs, and attention queries/keys
(QK-norm). RoPE (Su et al., 2024) position em-
beddings are used. MoE blocks with SwiGLU
3https://github.com/pytorch/fairseq/tree/
master/examples/translation
(Shazeer, 2020) non-linearities replace every feed-
forward layer, each containing E=4 routed Ma-
tryoshka experts with elastic inner dimensions
D = {256, 512, 1024, 2048} plus one Direction-
Aware Shared Expert (DASE). Both the router and
DASE are conditioned on a 64-dimensional direc-
tion embedding. Source and target embeddings are
tied with the output projection, and final logits are
soft-capped at 30.0.

A.4
Model Selection and Evaluation
We compute validation BLEU every 1,000 steps
and select the checkpoint with the highest vali-
dation BLEU averaged over the two translation
directions as the final model for evaluation. For
sub-network selection, we apply the same cri-
terion independently to each Dk ∈D on the
same validation set. For evaluation on PhoMT
and WMT-14, we use sacreBLEU4 v2.4.0 (sig-
nature nrefs:1|case:mixed|eff:no|tok:13a|
smooth:exp|version:2.4.0). For multi-domain,
we use tokenized BLEU with multi-bleu.pl5
matching the conventions of prior work.
Significance Testing.
We conducted paired ap-
proximate randomization (10,000 trials) alongside
paired bootstrap resampling (1,000 resamples) to
establish 95% confidence intervals. These tests con-
firm that the performance gains of the EDWARD-
NMT sub-networks over the baselines are highly
robust and statistically significant, consistently
achieving p < 0.001 across our primary bench-
marks.

A.6
Software and Hardware Settings
All of our main experiments on EDWARD-NMT
are implemented in JAX8 (0.9.2) and Flax9
(0.12.5).
Training uses FSDP-style sharding
with jax.distributed and gradient accumula-
tion through jax.lax.scan so that the effective
batch of 8192 sequences fits on a single TPU v5-
64 pod. The dual-consistency objective is imple-
mented by sampling two width masks per step in-
side the scanned step function, so the two forward
passes share both the parameter slice and the data
batch. All training and inference was performed
on 64 chips TPU v5e, with total training times of
approximately 6 hours for the multi-domain bench-
mark, 9 hours for OPUS-16, 12 hours for PhoMT,
and 18 hours for WMT-14.
Training Cost and Efficiency.
Profiling natively
on a TPU v5e pod reveals that EDWARD-NMT
requires ∼1160 ms per training step, roughly 2.2×

9https://github.com/google/flax
the time of standard MoE baselines (ST-MOE, GS-
MOE; ∼523 ms) and 3.3× the time of TRANS-

half the cost of training a standard MoE (3.63×).

A self-contained reproduction of the primary em-

Section B requires the following steps. All artefacts
are publicly available, and no proprietary code or
weights are needed.
1. Prepare data. Following Section A.1 for

2. Implement architecture.
Construct an

3. Implement router. Use the sigmoid router
with jitter, learned threshold, and STE as de-
fined in Section 3.3, with the target sparsity
ρ(t)
target defined in Section A.3. Add the auxil-
iary losses Lsp with weight λsp=0.01.
4. Dual-consistency loss. At every optimization
step, sample two widths DA, DB with replace-
ment from D, compute two forward passes
that share parameters, and add DC Loss de-
fined in Equation 13 to the cross-entropy loss
with λdc=1.
5. Train. Pre-tokenized the data with Tensor-

Additional Results
B.1
Computational Results
To evaluate deployment viability, we benchmarked
the extracted sub-networks across both edge and
server regimes (Table 11). For edge deployment,
we profiled models on a consumer-grade CPU (Ap-
ple M4, 4 threads, FP16) using compiled LiteRT
checkpoints at a batch size of B = 1. As shown
in Table 11, the most compact variant, EDWARD-
NMT-S, activates only 57M parameters and re-
quires just 6.28 GFLOPs. At B = 1, the sys-
tem is inherently memory-bound; the sparse rout-
ing machinery introduces unavoidable memory-
movement overhead, resulting in an on-device
10https://www.tensorflow.org/datasets
throughput (104.9 TPS) that is highly competi-
tive with, though slightly below, the dense TRANS-
FORMER BASE (117.7 TPS). However, it still dras-
tically outperforms the TRANSFORMER BIG base-
line (48.0 TPS) and delivers a 2.2× speedup over
our full XL variant (47.6 TPS).
In server-side deployment (profiled natively via
JAX on a single NVIDIA H100 GPU), this rout-
ing overhead is efficiently amortized across larger
batches, allowing the theoretical FLOP advantages
to materialize dramatically.
From B = 4 on-
wards, EDWARD-NMT-S strictly outperforms
TRANSFORMER BASE, ultimately delivering a
3.2× throughput speedup at B = 128 (70,372
vs. 21,755 TPS). Similarly, the full-capacity XL
variant is 5.3× faster than TRANSFORMER BIG
at B = 128 while activating significantly fewer
parameters. Ultimately, our train-once architecture
effectively balances high representational capac-
ity with the strict latency requirements of edge
deployment, while concurrently offering massive
scalability for high-throughput servers.

Additional Ablations on Consistency Strategies.
Table 19 provides additional ablation results for the
consistency loss variants on the PhoMT benchmark
(averaged over En↔Vi), complementing the multi-
domain findings in the main text. The observed
trends align perfectly with our primary results: ap-
plying the symmetric R-Drop objective degrades

performance across all scales. While asymmetric
KL distillation (using the largest variant as a de-
tached teacher) mitigates this severe degradation, it
still falls slightly short of the baseline. Consistent
with the multi-domain experiments, our proposed
Dual-Consistency (DC Loss) provides the optimal
regularization strategy, successfully outperforming
the “No loss” baseline and achieving the highest
scores across all model capacities (XL, L, M, S).
Additional Ablations on Contribution.
Ta-
ble 20 incrementally adds the EDWARD-NMT
components on top of a standard Top-2 MoE base-
line on PhoMT (noting that all settings use DASE).
Adding Matryoshka Experts (ME) yields immedi-
ate improvements across all widths (e.g., +0.59
BLEU on XL and +0.18 on S over the Top-2 base-
line). The gains are further amplified by the Dual-
Consistency (DC) objective, which acts as a cru-
cial regularizer to keep the smaller sub-networks
competitive (+0.38 BLEU on S over ME alone).
Finally, replacing the fixed Top-k=2 routing with
our proposed Adaptive Sigmoid Router provides
the best overall performance for the larger variants
(XL, L, M).
