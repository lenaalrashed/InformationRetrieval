Abstract
Neologism-aware machine translation1 aims
to translate source sentences containing neol-
ogisms into target languages. This field re-
mains underexplored compared with general
machine translation (MT). In this paper, we
propose an agentic framework, NeoAMT, for
neologism-aware machine translation equipped
with a Wiktionary-based search toolkit. Specif-
ically, we first construct a dedicated dataset
for neologism-aware machine translation and
build a search toolkit grounded in Wiktionary.
The dataset covers 16 languages and 75 trans-
lation directions in total, derived from approxi-
mately 10 million records of an English Wik-
tionary dump.
The retrieval corpus of the
search toolkit is also constructed from around
3 million cleaned records of the same dump.
We then leverage the dataset and toolkit to train
a translation agent via reinforcement learning
(RL) and to evaluate the accuracy of neologism-
aware machine translation. Furthermore, we
propose an RL training framework featuring
a novel reward design and an adaptive rollout
generation strategy that exploits “translation
difficulty” to further improve the translation
quality of translation agents using our search
toolkit2.

2.1
Construction of Neko Dataset
The first challenging issue in neologism-aware ma-
chine translation is that there is no solid testbed
which contains high-quality translation pairs and
neologism definitions. Although the previous study
Neo-bench (Zheng et al., 2024) has a neologism-
aware machine translation subset, it is relatively
small (240 in total) and contains only one language
pair and not publicly available as shown in Table 1.
Wiktionary3 is a free and multilingual project
that hosts a large number of word entries containing
annotated tags including “neologism”, word defi-
nitions and optional example sentences and their
translations. Therefore, we select the English Wik-
tionary as our data source to build our new dataset,
Neko, which is short for Neologism Example Sen-
tences from WiKtiOnary. Note that the English
Wiktionary is a multilingual dictionary in which
glosses are written in English. Sixteen languages

2.2
Search Toolkit
Dictionary.
Unlike question answering (QA) and
mathematical tasks, an MT-oriented dictionary is
required for translation agents. Specifically, we col-
lect word definitions from our cleaned 3,312,877
word entries. Each word definition includes “part-
of-speech”, “etymology”, “senses”, and optional
“word translations” in other languages. These word
definitions are compiled into a dictionary for later
retrieval by translation agents. The distribution of
word definition counts for different languages in
the constructed dictionary is presented in Figure 3b.
To better capture word semantics, we encode the
entire word definition including “part-of-speech”,
“etymology”, “senses”, and optional “word transla-
tions” into a semantic vector. This is because some
headwords share the same form but differ in their
glosses (senses) and etymologies; encoding head-
words alone would be insufficient to distinguish
3

these distinct lexical entries.
Retrieval Engine.
Multilingual embedding mod-
els (Sturua et al., 2024; Zhao et al., 2024; Chen
et al., 2024; Miao et al., 2024; Zhang et al., 2025)
have demonstrated superior performance across a
variety of retrieval tasks. We utilize the widely used
multilingual embedding model bge-m35 (Chen
et al., 2024) to obtain dense representations of
items in the constructed dictionary. The reason why
we choose bge-m3 is that it is one of SOTA embed-
ding models for retrieval and the pretraining data
of bge-m3 covering 194 languages contains a large
amount of translation-related corpora, for example,
NLLB (Team et al., 2022), CCMatrix (Schwenk
et al., 2021), which should reduce the possible bias
or domain mismatch. We use cosine similarity to
measure the relevance of two items. The faiss li-
brary6 (Johnson et al., 2019; Douze et al., 2024) is
leveraged to retrieve relevant items given a query.
The search toolkit that we proposed based on the
Wiktionary is quite flexible. You can add custom
words or replace ambiguous ones at any time to
further refine the dictionary knowledge base. This
framework can also be used with other dictionaries
in many different languages because we use a mul-
tilingual embedding model, bge-m3. The bge-m3
can also be replaced with the latest multilingual em-
bedding models, MT-specific or dictionary-specific
embedding models.

3.1
Reward Design
Outcome reward.
The outcome reward incor-
porates three components: a neologism-specific
reward, a neural model reward, and a format re-
ward.
We propose a neologism-specific reward Rneo to
encourage accurate translation of neologisms. This
reward is adapted from the terminology success
rate metric used in the WMT Terminology Transla-
tion Track (Semenov et al., 2023). We lemmatize
the generated translation ˆy to obtain ˆyl, along with
the corresponding spans of neologisms in the tar-
get language. The reward is then computed by
checking whether the lemmatized neologism spans
exist in ˆyl. The detailed computation procedure
for Rneo is presented in Algorithm 1. The neural
model reward Rneural is designed to ensure seman-
tic fidelity between x and ˆy. It combines scores
from XCOMET-XL (Guerreiro et al., 2024) and
CometKiwi-DA-XL (Rei et al., 2023) as follows:
Rneural = δ·sXCOMET+(1−δ)·sCometKiwi-DA, (1)

3.2
Adaptive Sampling based on Translation
Difficulty
The source texts in the Neko dataset exhibit varying
levels of difficulty. Difficult source texts require
LLMs to engage in intensive reasoning and explore
a wider range of trajectories prior to translation, re-
sulting in a comparatively slower learning process.
To effectively capture the distinction, we define
a metric “translation difficulty” based on relative
quality estimation scores (RQE) to measure how
hard it is to translate source texts. The definition is
as follows:
Definition 1 (Translation Difficulty)

4.2
Evaluation
We evaluate translation quality from two perspec-
tives: neologism-specific quality and overall qual-
ity.
Neologism-Specific Metrics.
For neologism-
specific metrics, we use four metrics, exact trans-
lation success rate sexact, fuzzy translation success
rate sfuzzy, lemma-exact translation success rate
slem-exact, and lemma-fuzzy translation success rate
slem-fuzzy. These scores use different string match-
ing approaches, such as lemmatization, exact string
matching and fuzzy string matching7. The four
metrics aim to check if the translations of neolo-
gisms can be found in the reference translations or
not. These metrics can complement each other to
ensure the stability and accuracy of the evaluation.
The detailed computation procedures of neologism-
specific metrics are presented in Appendix C.3.
Overall Quality Metrics.
For overall quality, we
mainly use LLM-as-a-judge evaluation. In this eval-
uation, we use the GEMBA-DA prompt (Kocmi
and Federmann, 2023) with gpt-5-2025-08-07
to evaluate the translations.
In addition to the
GEMBA evaluation, we also provide additional
neologism gloss information in the prompt to aid

5.4
Sanity Check about Thinking Paths of
NeoAMT
We analyze the success and failure patterns by
carefully investigating the thinking paths of our
model NeoAMT from three perspectives: (1) how
many queries are related to neologisms? (2) do
the retrieved results contain the neologism defini-
tions? (3) does NeoAMT use the retrieved results
for thinking? We randomly sample 100 model re-
sponses from the Chinese-English translation pairs.
Human volunteers are asked to check the model
responses from the above three points. The analy-
sis shows that 86.90% of search queries are related
to neologisms. 76.71% of retrieved results given
related neologism queries contain the neologism
definitions. 96.43% of the model thinking paths af-
ter receiving the retrieved results use the retrieved
neologism information for reasoning. This indi-
cates that (1) most of the model queries are related
to neologisms; (2) the retrieval component still
has room for improvement and is likely the domi-
nant performance bottleneck; (3) NeoAMT actively
utilizes the retrieved information in its reasoning
paths.

Related Work
Improving Machine Translation via Reinforce-
ment Learning.
Recent studies on this direction
mainly focus on how to design the reward em-
powered with test-time scaling. For example, MT-
R1-Zero (Feng et al., 2025) explores the effect of
mixed rule-based and neural model-based metric
score as rewards. SSR-Zero (Yang et al., 2025b)
utilizes self-evaluation score as the reward for train-
ing. TAT-R1 (Li et al., 2025b) employs word align-
ment (Jalili Sabet et al., 2020; Nagata et al., 2020;
Wu et al., 2023; Latouche et al., 2024; Miao et al.,
2025) to design rewards. DeepTrans (Wang et al.,
2025b) uses DeepSeek-V3 (Liu et al., 2024) to gen-
erate format, thought and translation rewards.
Neologism Translation.
Neologism translation
is a challenging task because of rapid change of
languages and the insufficient data. Lerner and
Yvon (2025) try to solve this issue using term defi-
nitions and in-context learning. Neo-bench (Zheng
et al., 2024) provides a benchmark dataset about
neologisms in which one sub-task is machine trans-
lation. However, the machine translation subset
in this benchmark is relatively small and not pub-
licly available. Other similar studies include social-
media, culture-aware and poetry translations (Yao
et al., 2024; Zhao et al., 2026; Chen et al., 2025)
Agentic Search.
Recent work explores the in-
tegration of search tools into reasoning paths of
LLMs to enhance accuracy and factuality on QA
and math tasks (Jin et al., 2025; Dong et al., 2025;
Li et al., 2025a). For example, Search-R1 (Jin et al.,
2025) uses Wikipedia articles as the search source
to improve the performance of models on QA tasks.
ARPO (Dong et al., 2025) utilizes the entropy to
control the branching sampling during reasoning
with external tools. These works mainly focus on
QA and math tasks. In this paper, we focus on
an MT-specific domain, neologism-aware machine
translation, which remains underexplored.
7
Conclusion
In this paper, we first construct a new multilin-
gual neologism-aware machine translation dataset
named Neko that covers 16 languages and contains
solid neologism definitions. The test split of this
dataset provides a testbed for neologism-aware ma-
chine translation. Furthermore, to fill in the gap that
it remains unclear that whether reasoning-based
RL with search tools improves neologism-aware
machine translation, we conduct experiments to
analyze the results of various methods and propose
a new RL training framework, NeoAMT, consist-
ing of a novel reward design and an adaptive sam-
pling approach based on the translation difficulty
to train the neologism-aware machine translation
agent. The results of experiments demonstrate the
effectiveness of our method.
9

Limitations
We use a general-purpose emebdding model,
bge-m3, for retrieving word entries in this paper.
This model is not specifically designed for machine-
translation related retrieval tasks though its training
dataset contains translation-related corpora and the
human analysis in § 5.4 also shows that there is
room for improvement about the retrieval perfor-
mance. Another point is that NeoAMT does not
follow the output format exactly specified in the
user instruction prompt during the thinking pro-
cess. The reason might be that our reward design
only imposes a constraint on the final translation
format.
Ethical Considerations
This research aims to investigate the effect of agen-
tic RL with search tools on the neologism-aware
machine translation. Our new dataset Neko is con-
structed from the English Wiktionary dump. Some
of the word entries may be derogatory and related
to politics and subcultures. We process the data
in compliance with the license of Wiktionary. The
views contained in the dataset are independent of
the authors. We have tried our best to remove such
words. In addition, we use various LLMs in this
paper adhering to the respective model licenses.
We used ChatGPT and Claude Opus 4.6 to improve
the writing of certain sections of this paper while
preserving the original meaning.
