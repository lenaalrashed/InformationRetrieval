Abstract
Progress in question answering (QA) over tab-
ular data has enabled reliable factual retrieval
from relational tables. However, many real-
world questions are probabilistic, requiring rea-
soning under uncertainty and latent conditional
dependencies that are not directly stored in
individual cells. We introduce LUCARIO, a
large-scale benchmark for probabilistic QA
over real-world tabular datasets, covering di-
verse domains, dependency patterns, and natu-
ral language variations. We propose Auto-BN,
a fully automatic neuro-symbolic framework
that induces a Bayesian Network from raw ta-
bles, translates natural-language questions into
formal probabilistic queries, and performs ex-
act inference.
Experiments across multiple
LLM backbones show that Auto-BN consis-
tently outperforms NL2SQL, retrieval-based,
and premise-based baselines, while maintain-
ing a near-zero error rate. LUCARIO fills a rel-
evant gap and provides a practical and scalable
testbed for advancing probabilistic reasoning
over structured data.

Introduction
Tabular data is frequently used to support decision-
making across enterprise, scientific, and web appli-
cations, from CRM systems and healthcare records
to logistics and financial analysis. With the rapid
advancement of large language models (LLMs),
question answering (QA) over structured data has
emerged as a critical research frontier.
Recent
progress in natural language to SQL (NL2SQL or
Text2SQL) systems has enabled accurate retrieval
of factual information from relational tables (Talaei
et al., 2024; Shen et al., 2024; Li et al., 2024). Fac-
tual queries such as “What is the customer’s signup
date?” can now be reliably answered by mapping
natural language to SQL queries that extract precise
values directly from the database.
Yet, many real-world information needs extend
far beyond factual retrieval. Questions such as
“What is the likelihood that a customer will churn
given their recent activity?” or “How probable is
a delayed shipment if the warehouse is in Region
A?” require reasoning under uncertainty. The cor-
rect answers to these probabilistic queries involve
incomplete evidence, latent and conditional depen-
dencies that are usually not directly encoded in a
single table cell, neither fully available in LLMs’
parametric knowledge. Thus, current SQL-based
and LLM-only approaches fall short in this setting,
often hallucinating answers (Huang et al., 2025) or
generalizing poorly when tasked with probabilistic
reasoning (Chakraborty et al., 2025). The answers
to these questions are estimated from the records
of a specific table, so the same churn question can
have a different answer for a different CRM table.
Prior work (Jin et al., 2023; Schrader et al., 2024;
Nafar et al., 2025) has explored probabilistic graph-
ical models and causal inference frameworks for
addressing uncertainty and probabilistic reasoning
jointly with LLMs. These approaches are con-
strained by scalability and practicality: they typ-
ically operate only on small or synthetic datasets
and rely on expert-curated structures for the un-
derlying models. In contrast, real-world tabular
data may span hundreds of attributes and tens of
thousands of rows, rendering manual construction
of probabilistic models infeasible. Compounding
this challenge is the absence of benchmarks for
probabilistic QA over large-scale tabular data. Ex-
isting benchmarks overwhelmingly target factual
QA, leaving no reliable way to evaluate, compare,
or systematically advance systems designed for
probabilistic reasoning.
In this paper, we introduce LUCARIO, a large-
scale benchmark and a framework for probabilistic
QA over tabular data. Our main contributions are:
1. A new benchmark with 10 real-world datasets,
designed to capture diverse probabilistic reasoning
patterns beyond factual retrieval and realistic scale.
2. Auto-BN, a fully automatic neuro-symbolic

on methods that must reason under uncertainty.

Related Work
Question Answering over Tabular Data: Ques-
tion answering (QA) over tabular data has become
an increasingly active research area, with mod-
els such as TAPAS (Herzig et al., 2020), TURL
(Deng et al., 2022), and Chase-SQL (Pourreza et al.,
2024) demonstrating strong performance on fac-
tual queries by translating natural language into
structured queries like SQL. These systems excel
when answers can be directly retrieved from table
cells, e.g., “What is the customer’s signup date?”.
Nonetheless, they struggle with queries requir-
ing reasoning beyond direct retrieval—particularly
those involving uncertainty or probabilistic depen-
dencies.
Recent systems have incorporated more complex
reasoning pipelines. TQAgent (Zhao et al., 2025)
integrates knowledge graphs and tree-structured
reasoning paths to handle multi-step queries across
diverse table schemas.
TableBench (Wu et al.,
2025) spans fact checking, numerical reasoning,
data analysis, and visualization, and TReB (Li et al.,
2026) defines 26 table-reasoning sub-tasks; neither
targets conditional-probability questions answered
from a joint distribution induced from the table.
While such efforts extend the scope of tabular QA,
they remain ill-suited for probabilistic reasoning
tasks that demand modeling latent dependencies
and conditional relationships not explicitly repre-
sented in the data.
Benchmarks for Probabilistic Dependency Rea-
soning: Evaluating how language models reason
about uncertainty and dependencies among vari-
ables has motivated the development of several
recent benchmarks.
Some benchmarks explicitly focus on causal rea-
soning. CausalQA (Bondarenko et al., 2022) and
QRData (Liu et al., 2024) evaluate models’ abil-
ity to identify causal structures and reason about
interventions and counterfactuals. These datasets
target reasoning tasks grounded in causal graphi-
cal models and structural assumptions about data-
generating processes.
Other benchmarks emphasize probabilistic rea-
soning more broadly, often relying on Bayesian
networks or textual descriptions of probabilistic
relationships. BLInD (Nafar et al., 2025) provides
natural language queries grounded in predefined
Bayesian Networks (BNs), requiring models to rea-
son over structured uncertainty and produce nu-
meric probability estimates. CLADDER (Jin et al.,
2023) evaluates reasoning across different levels of
dependency queries, including associational and
causal questions derived from symbolic graphs.
QUITE (Schrader et al., 2024) tests Bayesian rea-
soning using textual descriptions of probabilistic
relationships, assessing models’ ability to quantify
uncertainty from structured descriptions.
While impactful, these benchmarks share no-
table limitations. They typically rely on small,
predefined Bayesian Networks (e.g., QUITE net-
works average only 6 nodes and 3 states per node,
yielding fewer than 50 unique one-hop reasoning
paths on average). This restricts both the diversity
of reasoning patterns and the difficulty of infer-
ence. Moreover, they are often domain-specific
(e.g., healthcare) and require significant human cu-
ration, hindering scalability and generalization.
Positioning Our Work:
Distinct from prior work, LUCARIO is con-
structed through a scalable, data-driven and human-
validated process that enables probabilistic reason-
ing directly over tabular data. From real-world re-
lational tables spanning diverse domains, Bayesian
Networks are semi-automatically induced (not re-
quiring manual structure design, while incorpo-
rating human knowledge and validation to ensure
reliability and directional dependency). Each table
is thus paired with its corresponding probabilistic
graph. LUCARIO’s scale allows the benchmark to
capture a broader range of dependency patterns,
including simple conditional relationships, multi-

LUCARIO Benchmark
We introduce LUCARIO, a benchmark built upon
the BIRD dataset (Li et al., 2023), designed to
evaluate probabilistic question answering (QA)
over tabular data. While BIRD focuses on trans-
lating factual queries into SQL, our benchmark
introduces probabilistic queries requiring reason-
ing under uncertainty. The benchmark comprises
three primary components: relational tables, proba-
bilistic queries, and ground-truth answers, supple-
mented by auxiliary reasoning artifacts (Bayesian
Networks, premises, and insights).
3.1
Data Sources & Benchmark Artifacts
LUCARIO contains 10 real-world, large-scale re-
lational databases from diverse domains (e.g., fi-
nance, healthcare, sports) sourced from BIRD and
selected for meaningful probabilistic dependen-
cies, feature richness, and statistical sufficiency
(Appendix A.1). To support downstream neuro-
symbolic reasoning, we pair each table with three
auxiliary artifacts:
Bayesian Network (BN): A graphical model rep-
resenting the directional statistical dependencies
(Pearl, 1988) among the table’s columns. A BN
factorizes their joint distribution into local condi-
tional probabilities, enabling exact computation of
P(X=x | C=c) for any target and evidence.
Premises: Premises are a set of textual statements
characterizing the statistical relationships inherent
in the tabular data. These statements function as
probabilistic rules, serving as a foundation for rule-
based reasoning and explanation (Schrader et al.,
2024). Formally, a single premise corresponds to
a one-hop reasoning step that represents the condi-
tional distribution of a target node given the specific
states of its parent nodes.
Insights: A primary challenge of using premises
as the core mechanism for the probabilistic reason-
ing, prediction, and explanation is the size of the
premise set. Because it exhaustively covers all pos-
sible combinations of attribute states, the premise
space grows exponentially for tables with ten or
more attributes, introducing significant computa-
tional latency and complexity. To mitigate this,
LUCARIO includes a set of insights—a compact
subset of premises identified as having the most
substantial impact on the data’s behavior. We de-
fine the Insight Saliency of a variable X under
a conditioning context C (the observed states of
other columns, e.g., a customer’s recent activity in
the churn question of §1) as the Kullback–Leibler
(KL) divergence between the conditional distribu-
tion PX|C and the base marginal distribution PX:
Saliency(X, C) = DKL(PX|C ∥PX)

3.2
Diversity in Queries and Questions
A key differentiator of LUCARIO compared to
prior probabilistic reasoning benchmarks is the
structural and linguistic diversity of its evalua-
tion set. Because our semi-automatic construction
pipeline (§3.3) is specifically engineered to gen-
erate these complex structures, we first define the
target reasoning patterns and natural language vari-
ations that the benchmark encompasses.
Probabilistic Query Types (Reasoning Patterns).
To enable systematic evaluation of probabilistic
QA, LUCARIO includes a diverse set of probabilis-
tic queries (Figure 2) covering distinct patterns of
conditional dependence among table attributes: (i)
Forward Conditional Queries: Probability of an
outcome given observed conditions (e.g., the proba-
bility of high sales given that a marketing campaign
was launched). (ii) Diagnostic Queries: Inferring
the likelihood of an explanatory variable given ob-
served evidence (e.g., the probability of a disease
given a set of symptoms). (iii) Interdependent
Evidence Queries: Multiple correlated variables
provide alternative explanations for the same ob-
servation, such that evidence supporting one expla-
nation reduces the probability of competing ones.
(iv) Multi-Evidence Aggregation Queries: Han-
dling scenarios with multiple pieces of evidence
that may be overlapping or provide corroborative
information.

Dataset Creation
LUCARIO is constructed via a three-stage semi-
automatic pipeline incorporating expert knowledge
and validation.
Stage 1: BN Construction. For each selected
table, continuous numerical columns are first dis-
cretized into semantically meaningful bins (e.g.,
mapping a raw timestamp to morning time range).
We then apply the PC algorithm (Kalisch and
Bühlman, 2007) to induce an initial Directed
Acyclic Graph (DAG) representing the probabilis-
tic dependencies between columns. To align the
benchmark with domain logic and filter spurious
statistical correlations, two domain experts man-
ually review and refine these DAGs (adding, re-
moving, or reversing edges), injecting semantic
knowledge beyond what is observable in the raw
data. When they disagreed or were uncertain about
an edge, they consulted conditional-independence
tests and the underlying records and resolved the
issue through discussion. We subsequently fit Con-
ditional Probability Distributions (CPDs) to these

Following established protocols in probabilis-

Stage 2: Question Generation. Using the ground-
truth BNs, we employ stratified sampling to se-

graph to identify parent, child, and Markov-Blanket
structures that align with our five defined proba-
bilistic query reasoning patterns (Figure 2). We
then prompt an LLM with the table descriptions,
columns statistics, BN schema, selected anchor/-
context nodes, and the target reasoning pattern to
generate candidate natural language questions.
To ensure high linguistic quality and logical co-
herence, every candidate question undergoes man-
ual screening for fluency, correctness, and contex-
tual relevance; sub-optimal formulations are dis-
carded. Finally, each accepted question is expanded
into four distinct linguistic variants (Figure 3)—to
evaluate model robustness against surface-level
phrasing variations.
Stage 3: Answer Computation. To establish the
ground-truth answers, we compute exact probabili-
ties by performing inference directly on the expert-
validated BN. For a given target variable X and
conditioning context C, the ground-truth proba-
bility is formally computed as P(X=x | C=c).
Exact inference makes each answer analytically
correct with respect to the expert-validated BN and
its fitted CPDs, grounded in the table’s data distri-
bution, and fully reproducible.

3.5
Benchmark Quality Evaluation
To ensure the reliability of LUCARIO, we con-
ducted both structural and linguistic evaluations
of the benchmark components.
Graph Structure Evaluation. To evaluate the
finalized benchmark artifacts, three researchers in-
dependent of the benchmark curation assessed the
plausibility of a sampled subset of directional edges
from the Ground-truth BNs. Provided with the
graphs and underlying tabular data, the experts
judged all sampled edges to be valid. Specifically,
84% of the edges were evaluated as intuitively con-
sistent with domain knowledge, while the remain-
ing 16% represented more subtle correlations that
required inspection of the underlying data distribu-
tions to confirm.

3.6
Evaluation Metrics
Given a natural language question on a table and
its ground-truth probability, we evaluate model per-
formance using the following metrics:
Error Rate: The percentage of predictions ei-
ther outside the valid probability range [0.0, 1.0] or
missing entirely. A fallback value of 1.0/numstates
is assigned, assuming a uniform prior. This gener-
alizes the 0.5 fallback commonly used in existing
binary-state benchmarks.
Mean Absolute Error (MAE): The average abso-
lute difference between predicted and true proba-
bilities, measuring overall estimation accuracy.
Root Mean Squared Error (RMSE): The square
root of the average squared error, which penalizes
large deviations more heavily than MAE.
Accuracyx: The proportion of predictions within
±x of the ground-truth. We report Acc0.02 and
Acc0.05 as thresholds for near-correct predictions.

4.1
Automatic BN Construction (Offline)
In contrast to the expert-involved Gold BNs used
for benchmark evaluation, Auto-BN induces a data-
driven BN B from the raw table T via a fully auto-
mated, four-step offline pipeline: (i) LLM-assisted
column classification (binary, categorical, or nu-
merical) based on value distributions, data types,
and schema descriptions; (ii) K-means discretiza-
tion of continuous columns, utilizing an LLM to
assign semantically meaningful boundary labels;
(iii) directed dependency graph induction via the
PC algorithm, with algorithmic post-processing to
enforce acyclicity and prune low-confidence edges;
and (iv) Conditional Probability Table (CPT) fitting
via Bayesian estimation directly over the tabular
records. Discretization lets parameter learning and
exact inference use finite-state CPTs without the
distributional assumptions (e.g., linear-Gaussian)
required by continuous or hybrid BNs. It also lets
one BN handle categorical and numeric columns
uniformly. Executed once per table, this pipeline
yields a purely data-driven probabilistic model. By
decoupling the induced graph from the human-
verified ground-truth BNs, we ensure that subse-
quent evaluations rely exclusively on original data,
thereby precluding any ground-truth leakage.
4.2
Robust Query Translation
At inference time, an LLM parses q into a struc-
tured node-mapping query q′ = ⟨ˆn, ˆs, C⟩. Draw-
ing inspiration from execution-guided Text-to-SQL
pipelines (Pourreza et al., 2024; Talaei et al., 2024),
Auto-BN mitigates single-pass schema-linking er-
rors via a three-phase fault-tolerant mechanism:
Multi-Path Cross-Consistency Voting.
Auto-
BN concurrently samples N (default: 3) diverse
parse candidates. Each is validated against the BN
schema to ensure all nodes and states strictly exist
in BN B. The plurality tuple among the valid can-
didates is selected, surfacing semantic ambiguity
as structured uncertainty rather than a silent failure.
Execution-Guided Iterative Refinement. If all
N candidates fail validation, Auto-BN extracts the
specific error trace (e.g., invalid target node) from
a fresh, zero-temperature parse. This execution
feedback is appended to the prompt, allowing the
LLM to self-correct its schema linking for up to
Kcorr rounds (default: Kcorr = 2), recovering the
6

vast majority of initial vocabulary mismatches.
Soft Fallback and Typed Error Taxonomy. Upon
depleting the correction budget, Auto-BN assigns
a typed error (e.g., invalid_state). If the ini-
tial voting phase produced any parseable candi-
date, this best-effort mapping is forwarded to the
inference fallback chain (§4.3) instead of being
discarded, maximizing structural recovery before
abstention. Together, these mechanisms reduce the
parser error rate from 5.6–20.8% under single-pass
parsing to near zero across three LLM backbones
(Appendix D.6, Table 14).
4.3
Inference with Multi-Level Relaxation
A structurally valid query q′ is executed via vari-
able elimination (Eq. 2). To handle queries suf-
fering from persistent vocabulary mismatches or
execution-time logical exceptions (including those
forwarded via the soft fallback mechanism), Auto-
BN applies a four-level heuristic relaxation chain:
(i) Semantic Node Relaxation replaces an un-
mapped ˆn with the closest valid BN node via cosine
similarity using sentence embeddings; (ii) Seman-
tic State Relaxation similarly maps invalid states ˆs
or states within C to their nearest valid neighbors;
(iii) Context Pruning iteratively strips conflicting
evidence variables from C; and (iv) Marginal Fall-
back returns the unconditioned prior P(ˆn = ˆs) if
all conditioned variants fail. Appendix F describes
Auto-BN-Tool, an agentic tool-calling extension
of this pipeline, and Appendix G provides the full
prompt templates.

Experimental Evaluation
We evaluate the Auto-BN framework and baselines
on the 10 datasets in LUCARIO benchmark.
5.1
Baselines
We compare our framework against a diverse set
of baselines that reflect factual and probabilistic
approaches to probabilistic QA over tabular data:
NL2SQL: Apply recent NL2SQL work (Gao et al.,
2023) in probabilistic setting, where the model gen-
erates a SQL query from the question, executes
it on the database, and uses the result to infer a
probabilistic answer.
LLM + Table: A direct prompting strategy where
the question is answered using the table description
and a random sample of rows (until reaching the
maximum context window size), without structured
reasoning or symbolic modeling.
Reasoning Models: Same strategy as LLM + Ta-
ble, but using advanced reasoning models and their
inherently internal CoT.
TableRAG: TableRAG (Chen et al., 2024) is a
large table understanding approach by LLM with
retrieval-augmented generation strategy.
LLM + Premise: This baseline resembles Causal-
CoT (Jin et al., 2023) extended with large-scale
probabilistic premise retrieval. It identifies nodes
and states from the question using LLM, retrieves
probabilistic premises, then uses LLM to answer
the question with all information. Three premise
retrieval strategies (Appendix B) are evaluated.
LLM + Premise + Insights: Extends the previous
approach by also incorporating a small set (20)
of high-impact insights generated by our proposed
Markov-Blanket-based insight generation approach
(see Appendix C for details).
Auto-BN: Our proposed fully automated neuro-
symbolic pipeline (§4). To ensure a strict, real-
world evaluation, Auto-BN does not access the
human-verified Gold BNs, premises, or insights
provided by the benchmark.

Despite being designed for large-table under-
standing, TableRAG underperforms even the ba-
sic LLM+Table baseline (7.6% vs. 11.1% Acc0.02
with GPT-4o). This exposes a fundamental mis-
match: TableRAG optimizes for localized fact
lookup (“needles in a haystack”). By retrieving
a bounded set of discrete cell values without their
underlying frequency statistics, it cannot aggre-
gate the global evidence required to reconstruct
the dense marginal and conditional distributions
necessary for probabilistic reasoning.
NL2SQL-style models perform poorly in this set-
ting, as expected. They frequently fail to produce
valid SQL under zero-shot prompting, especially
Mixtral. More fundamentally, deterministic query
execution cannot express conditional uncertainty.
For instance, retrieving zero matched records does
not equate to a zero probability—highlighting a
core limitation of factual QA pipelines when ap-
plied to probabilistic tasks.
Premise-Based Inference:
Inspired by ap-
proaches like CLADDER that use LLMs for one-
hop probabilistic reasoning, we evaluated whether
our benchmark could support similar derivations.
However, due to the significantly larger and more
complex premise sets in LUCARIO (79k premises
per table), LLMs struggled to combine the rele-
vant evidence into coherent probabilistic deriva-
tions. Among premise retrievers, vector-based re-
trieval consistently outperformed BM25. Hybrid
retrieval (combining both methods) yields marginal
improvement over individual retrieval.
Incorporating Insights: Across all retrieval
strategies, augmenting retrieved premises with a
compact set of 20 high-impact insights substan-
tially improved performance. By distilling the large
premise space into a concise and informative sub-
set, insights reduce cognitive overhead, improve
8

answer calibration, and mitigate hallucinations in
downstream reasoning.
Auto-BN: Auto-BN substantially outperforms
all baselines across core metrics. With GPT-4o, it
achieves 28.0% Acc0.02 and MAE of 0.107, while
successfully maintaining a near-zero error rate.
This demonstrates the effectiveness of integrating
probabilistic graphical model reasoning with ro-
bust LLM-based query understanding. By learning
BNs directly from data and executing fail-safe se-
mantic parsing, Auto-BN provides highly accurate
answers grounded in exact probabilistic inference.
Agentic Extension (Auto-BN-Tool). To some-
how relax the limitations of the pipelined approach
and allow LLM-based self recovery, we extend
Auto-BN into a callable tool for multi-turn LLM
agents. This formulation enables the LLM to au-
tonomously invoke probability calculations, itera-
tively decompose compositional queries, and syn-
thesize comprehensive answers from multiple prob-
abilistic sub-steps. We ran Auto-BN-Tool with
GPT-4o and its performance is very close to Auto-
BN (more details in Appendix F).
Probability Hallucination vs. Grounded Evi-
dence: While LLM baselines artificially achieve
near-zero error rates by forcing formatted outputs,
they frequently hallucinate uniform priors when un-
certain (e.g., predicting 0.2 for 5 candidate states).
Auto-BN strictly precludes this by grounding every
prediction in exact Bayesian inference. Its own
near-zero error rate stems from robust query trans-
lation, execution-guided refinement and semantic
relaxation rather than blind guessing, guaranteeing
a transparent, verifiable reasoning trace.

formance breakdowns, running time, multi-hop
premises and ablation study are in Appendix D.

Conclusion
We introduce LUCARIO, a benchmark for proba-
bilistic question answering over real-world tabu-
lar data, and Auto-BN, a framework for answer-
ing such questions. Unlike prior work that relies
on small, curated Bayesian Networks or synthetic
templates, LUCARIO supports large-scale, data-
driven reasoning by inducing and human-validating
Bayesian Networks from relational tables. It in-
cludes diverse query types, natural language varia-
tions, and structured artifacts such as premises and
insights. Auto-BN combines probabilistic infer-
ence with robust LLM-based query translation. Ex-
perimental results show that our framework outper-
forms retrieval-based and probability-based base-
lines, highlighting the value of integrating proba-
bilistic graphical models with neural models for
reasoning under uncertainty.
Limitations
While our framework effectively learns Bayesian
network structures from tabular records, it operates
in a purely data-driven manner and does not incor-
porate external knowledge or semantic priors be-
yond human validation of the benchmark artifacts.
Consequently, the learned statistical dependencies
are limited to patterns present in the observed data.
We emphasize that our framework evaluates ob-
servational probabilistic reasoning. While we oc-
casionally employ terminology from the causal dis-
covery literature (e.g., the PC algorithm) to com-
pare with related work, we do not claim the discov-
ery of true causal mechanisms or the estimation of
interventional effects (do-calculus). Some directed
edges in the induced Bayesian Networks represent
strict statistical correlations (Markov Equivalence)
rather than verified causal mechanisms.
Finally, while we introduced a new benchmark
9

and demonstrated strong baseline methods, we do
not claim our proposed Auto-BN pipeline to be the
optimal solution for this benchmark. The bench-
mark is intended as a platform to foster further
exploration at the intersection of tabular reason-
ing, uncertainty quantification, and probabilistic
modeling.
Ethical Considerations
Our framework automatically fit given tabular
data across domains, which may propagate un-
derlying biases in the data.
The learned struc-
tures reflect statistical estimations based solely on
observed records, without incorporating broader
world knowledge. As such, they may not repre-
sent ground truth and should not be used as generic
justification or reliable decision making, such as
investigations or healthcare applications.

Benchmark Details
We provide detailed statistics for the LUCARIO
benchmark, covering data source selection, the
structural properties of the Bayesian Networks, and
the distribution of questions across reasoning pat-
terns.
A.1
Data Source Selection
LUCARIO is built on 10 real-world relational tables
drawn from the BIRD benchmark (Li et al., 2023).
These 10 tables were carefully selected from hun-
dreds of candidate sources in BIRD according to
three criteria that are necessary for constructing a
meaningful probabilistic QA benchmark:
Meaningful Probabilistic Dependencies:
The
schema must exhibit logical, directional associa-
tions between attributes (e.g., departure delay in-
fluencing arrival delay), enabling non-trivial con-
ditional reasoning.
11

Feature Richness:
The table must feature a high
density of categorical or discretizable numerical
columns. Databases consisting primarily of unique
identifiers, proper names, or unstructured locations
were excluded.
Statistical Sufficiency:
The table must contain
a sufficient volume of records to ensure that em-
pirical probability distributions and the induced
statistical models (e.g., Bayesian Networks) are
robust, reliable, and representative of real-world
complexity.
The resulting 10 tables span diverse real-world
domains, from transportation and finance to sports
and social media. Table 5 summarizes the key
properties of each source table.
A.2
Detailed Statistics
Bayesian Network Properties.
As shown in Ta-
ble 6, the Bayesian Networks induced in LUCARIO
are substantially larger and more structured than
those used in prior benchmarks. On average, each
network contains over 12 nodes, with approxi-
mately 6,000 probabilistic premises per node. This
scale highlights a key challenge: methods that re-
quire injecting all premises into LLM prompts,
such as CLADDER-style symbolic chains, are fun-
damentally infeasible due to context length limits
and reasoning bottlenecks. This motivates our de-
sign of insight-driven filtering and structured infer-
ence via Auto-BN (§ 4).
Question Distribution.
Table 7 summarizes the
distribution of question types across five proba-
bilistic query types. For each probabilistic query,
four variations in question formulation are created:
Exact, Shifted, Certain, and Natural, yielding an
equal number of questions per type (table rows).
The probabilistic reasoning query types include for-
ward conditional query and diagnostic query, as
well as more complex reasoning patterns such as
interdependent evidence query and mixed queries,
with or without redundant context. The benchmark
is also balanced across query types (table columns),
ensuring broad coverage and structural diversity.
Evaluation Splits.
Following the protocol of
zero-shot benchmarks such as BIG-Bench (Srivas-
tava et al., 2023), we treat each table as an indepen-
dent evaluation unit, with no train/test split. This
setup assesses inherent reasoning capabilities with-
out fine-tuning. An optional train/test split is pro-
vided for users interested in training/fine-tuning
based experiments.
Privacy and Licensing.
The LUCARIO bench-
mark does not include names or any information
that uniquely identifies individuals. In accordance
with its data sources, the benchmark is released
under the CC BY-SA 4.0 license.

C.2
Auto-BN Evaluation
While the technical mechanisms of the Auto-BN
pipeline are detailed in §4, we emphasize a criti-
cal aspect of its evaluation protocol here. In our
main experiments, Auto-BN operates in a strictly
automated, structure-free setting. It accesses only
the raw tables and their descriptions, without any
access to the ground-truth BNs. Consequently, the
data-specific Bayesian Networks induced by Auto-
BN act purely as observational probabilistic repre-
sentations of the underlying tabular data, and do
not benefit from the domain experts’ manual refine-
ment or injection of semantic knowledge.
By contrast, the premise- and insight-augmented
baselines leverage artifacts derived directly from
the expert-curated ground-truth BNs. We inten-
tionally adopt this asymmetric evaluation proto-
col to prevent structural data leakage and rigor-
ously test Auto-BN’s fully automated induction
capabilities. For an analysis of Auto-BN’s upper-
bound performance when provided with the human-
verified ground-truth BNs—which isolates query
translation effectiveness from structure induction
challenges—please refer to the ablation study in
Appendix E.
C.3
NL2SQL (Factual Retrieval)
This baseline tests whether deterministic SQL fac-
tual retrieval can support probabilistic inference.
Following Gao et al. (2023), we apply a text-
to-SQL model that generates a SQL query from
the natural language question, using the database
schema (same with Gao et al. (2023)) and table de-
scription. The result of executing this query (e.g.,
a count, average, or conditionally filtered subset) is
then passed back to the LLM alongside the original
question to estimate the desired probability. This
tests whether factual retrieval systems can be repur-
posed for uncertainty estimation, despite lacking
probabilistic semantics.

C.5
TableRAG (Retrieval-Augmented Neural)
To evaluate state-of-the-art retrieval-augmented
generation on tabular data, we implement the
TableRAG baseline (Chen et al., 2024) using the of-
ficial Google Research codebase. Unlike the LLM
+ Table baseline, which relies on row sampling to
fill the context window, TableRAG leverages query
expansion and dense cell retrieval to dynamically
extract the most semantically relevant tabular data.
To accommodate the massive scale of the
LUCARIO datasets (which average 486k rows and
scale up to 4.4M rows), we made two necessary
adaptations to the default TableRAG configuration.
First, we pre-calculated all cell embeddings offline
to construct the CellDB, ensuring efficient dense
retrieval during online inference. Second, because
probabilistic QA fundamentally requires aggregat-
ing broad statistical evidence rather than locating a
single factual “needle in a haystack,” we increased
the top-k cell retrieval limit by 10× over the de-
fault setting to maximize the evidence provided to
the LLM. To ensure strict experimental fairness,
TableRAG was evaluated on the exact same hard-
ware configuration as Auto-BN and all other base-
lines.
C.6
LLM + Premise (Retrieval-Augmented
Neural)
To evaluate whether symbolic rules can improve
LLM-based reasoning, we follow the CLAD-
DER (Jin et al., 2023) strategy of retrieving premise
statements and prompting the LLM to chain them
in multiple steps (CoT). Given a question, the LLM
first identifies target and evidence variables. We
then retrieve up to 40 relevant premises from a
pre-generated store, using either BM25, dense vec-
tor search, or a hybrid of both (see Appendix B).
These premises, alongside the question and table
metadata, are passed to the LLM to calculate the
answer. This setup tests the model’s ability to sim-
ulate multi-hop probabilistic derivation from one-
hop rules.
Premise retrieval is implemented via LlamaIn-
dex (Liu, 2022) using Sentence-BERT (Reimers
and Gurevych, 2019) and BM25. We retrieve up to
40 premises per query.
C.7

Retrieval)

by introducing a focused retrieval mechanism that
incorporates only the most informative conditional

high-saliency context shifts.

the Insight Saliency quantifies the extent to which
a context c shifts the belief distribution over a
variable X. For each variable, we compute this
saliency score across candidate contexts generated

C.8
ProbLog-Based Symbolic Baseline
Following QUITE (Schrader et al., 2024), we
attempted to encode retrieved premises into
ProbLog (De Raedt et al., 2007) programs and
evaluate queries symbolically. Conceptually, this
baseline is similar to Auto-BN in that both aim to
compute calibrated probabilities using a structured
probabilistic engine, rather than relying on LLMs
to approximate probabilistic reasoning. While the-
oretically appealing, this method relies on the LLM
to perfectly translate each premise needed into
ProbLog queries. In QUITE, this led to a failure
rate exceeding 90% even under simplified WEP
settings with only 40 premises. Due to the high
brittleness and low success rate of this approach in
LUCARIO, we exclude it from final comparisons.
C.9
Model Details
Three types of LLM are used in our experiments:
GPT-4o (Hurst et al., 2024) (unknown size), Llama-
3.3-70B (Grattafiori et al., 2024; Meta, 2024) (70
billion parameters), and Mixtral-8x22B (Mistral
AI, 2024) (141 billion total and 39 billion active pa-
rameters), a sparse mixture-of-experts model (Jiang
et al., 2024), via API. During benchmark cura-
tion, GPT-4o is used to generate natural language
text. All baselines involving LLMs use consistent
prompting templates, with GPT-4o serving as the
primary model during development. LLaMA-3.3-
70B and Mixtral-8x22B are evaluated using the
same prompts without additional fine-tuning.
The Bayesian Networks training for Auto-BN
uses PGMPY(Ankan and Textor, 2024) library on
an r5.4xlarge AWS instance (8 vCPUs, 128 GB
RAM, Amazon Linux 2023 system); training time
is reported in Appendix D.5
D
Additional Experimental Results
D.1
Performance by Question Type
Figure 5 presents the performance of various base-
lines across different question types. For each type,
we report three metrics: Acc0.02 (↑), Acc0.05 (↑),
and MAE (↓), capturing both accuracy and error
magnitude.
Across all question types, Auto-BN consistently
outperforms the other baselines, achieving the high-
est accuracy and lowest error. Even in the more
challenging Natural questions, Auto-BN maintains
superior performance, highlighting its robustness
under linguistic variability. The four variants stress
different pipeline steps. Natural questions replace
16

numeric conditions with semantic descriptors (e.g.,
“high temperature”), which are ambiguous when
mapped to table values and BN bin boundaries.
Certain questions state a single value that must
be mapped to its containing BN state (e.g., “ex-
actly 7 goals” →bin (4, 10]); few or no records
match such a point conjunction exactly, so record-
counting methods such as NL2SQL break down
(Figure 7). Shifted questions use an alternative
valid range (e.g., “between 4 and 7” when the BN
state is (4, 10]), which the model must recognize as
a sub-range of an existing state. LLM mathematical
reasoning has been shown to degrade when numer-
ical values move outside typical ranges (Shrestha
et al., 2025), and the narrower range again reduces
the number of matching records.
The LLM+Table baseline performs relatively
well on the Certain question type. This is likely be-
cause questions specifying a single numeric value
can be more directly matched against sampled rows,
allowing the LLM to approximate probabilities
more reliably than with binned numeric states.
In contrast, the NL2SQL baseline degrades as
the question type becomes more natural or para-
phrased, since such formulations are harder to trans-
late into valid and expressive SQL clauses. This
confirms the brittleness of deterministic symbolic
pipelines under linguistic variation.
Adding Insights consistently improves the
LLM+Premise baselines across all question types.
This highlights the importance of retrieving high-
impact, distributional evidence rather than relying
solely on shallow one-hop premises, as it leads to
better reasoning fidelity and calibration.
D.2
Performance by Probabilistic Query Type
Table 10 reports zero-shot GPT-4o performance
(Acc0.02 and RMSE) across the five probabilistic
reasoning types, averaged over all linguistic ques-
tion variants.
Overall, Auto-BN achieves the strongest per-
formance in 8 of 10 metrics, demonstrating ex-
ceptional robustness across Forward Conditional,
Diagnostic, and Mixed queries. This proves that
exact mathematical inference over an induced
graph consistently outperforms purely generative
probability estimation. Auto-BN is only outper-
formed in Interdependent Evidence (Acc0.02) and
Mixed+Redundant (RMSE) by insight-augmented
baselines. This stems from an inherent information
asymmetry: Insights are derived directly from the
human-verified Gold BNs. In highly complex or
redundant contexts, these oracle-calibrated signals
help the LLM filter noise more effectively than
Auto-BN’s fully automated, purely observational
graph.
Among retrieval-based methods, Insights con-
sistently outperform raw premises by distilling
massive premise spaces into compact, high-impact
conditional shifts. Conversely, factual (NL2SQL,
LLM+Table) and premise-only baselines fail uni-
formly on composite queries (Mixed types). Such
queries require chaining heterogeneous statistical
cues and ignoring distractors—capabilities that de-
terministic SQL or shallow text retrieval fundamen-
tally lack.
D.3
Per-Dataset Results
Table 12 reports MAE by dataset for the main
baselines. Auto-BN achieves the lowest or tied-
lowest MAE on 8 of 10 datasets. The two excep-
tions, coinmarketcap and college completion, favor
LLM+Premise(H)+Ins (0.100 vs. 0.117; 0.142 vs.
0.184). These datasets may be harder for automatic
structure learning or may benefit more from the
Gold-BN-derived premises and insights that Auto-
BN does not use. Auto-BN’s MAE also varies
more across datasets (0.038–0.184) than that of the
premise- and insight-based baselines. Adding in-
sights to retrieved premises improves MAE on all
10 datasets.
D.4
Extending the One-Hop Premise
Definition
While most prior benchmarks operate on one-hop
premises (grounded conditional probability state-
ments of the form P(Y |X) representing direct
parent–child dependencies in the underlying con-
ditional graph), we examine whether incorporat-
ing multi-hop premises (specifically two-hops) can
improve performance by capturing richer contex-
tual dependencies. However, extending to multi-
hop contexts increases combinatorial complexity
and may introduce noisy or redundant informa-
tion. To focus the analysis, we restrict our study
to two-hop premises and evaluate their impact on
the cookbook nutrition dataset. We construct two-
hop premise representations by extracting all valid,
unique two-step conditional dependencies and com-
bining them with existing one-hop premises to
form an augmented premise vector store. Both the
LLM+Premise(Vector) and its Insight-augmented
variant are then evaluated under this expanded evi-
dence setup.

pendency graph discovery and BN parameter learn-

translation and exact probabilistic execution). Ta-
ble 13 summarizes the computational footprint of
both phases across the benchmark.

As shown in Table 13,
the one-time offline induction scales naturally with
dataset complexity.
It ranges from 94 seconds

CPU-only process executed exactly once per table.
When amortized over the lifespan of a deployed
database, this offline cost becomes negligible.

1.90 seconds per query across the benchmark. To
maintain this low overhead despite the rigorous

A critical bottleneck in neuro-symbolic pipelines
is the inherent brittleness of LLM-based semantic
parsing. To demonstrate how Auto-BN overcomes
this limitation, we ablate our robust query transla-
tion and multi-level relaxation mechanisms (§4.2,
§4.3) and analyze the resulting failure modes. We
define a Base Auto-BN configuration that relies
exclusively on a single zero-shot parse, completely
removing multi-path voting, execution-guided re-
finement, and all inference-level fallbacks. Figure 6
illustrates the exact error distribution for this uncor-
rected baseline across all benchmark datasets.
The dominant failure categories are: State In-
valid for Node (62.3%), where the LLM predicts a
state that does not belong to the queried node; State
Not Found (28.3%), where the predicted state does
not exist anywhere in the BN vocabulary; Variable
Conflict (3.0%), where the same variable appears
as both target and evidence; Node Not in Graph
(2.2%); Node Name as State (1.1%); JSON Parsing
errors (1.1%); and Other Inference Errors (1.9%).
Over 90% of these baseline failures stem from
node–state vocabulary mismatches, confirming that
without structural safeguards, LLMs systematically
hallucinate states outside the BN schema. These
findings directly motivate our proposed pipeline:
(i) multi-path cross-consistency voting to surface
schema-invalid parses early, (ii) execution-guided
iterative refinement to self-correct mismatches, and
(iii) semantic relaxation fallbacks to recover the
nearest valid node or state when strict parsing fails.

F
Auto-BN-Tool (Agentic BN Inference)
To facilitate the integration of our proposed sys-
tem into modern agentic workflows, we intro-
duce Auto-BN-Tool, a tool-calling extension of
the base Auto-BN framework. By wrapping the
exact probabilistic inference engine into an exe-
cutable tool, Auto-BN-Tool allows the LLM to
operate within a multi-turn agent loop. The model
can iteratively invoke the tool, analyze execution
results or error messages, and synthesize a final an-
swer from multiple sub-queries. This formulation
demonstrates the flexibility of our modular design,
showing that Auto-BN can seamlessly serve as a
foundational probabilistic reasoning engine for au-
tonomous LLM agents.
In practice, at each turn, the LLM is prompted
with the natural language question alongside the
derived BN metadata (i.e., node and state vocab-
ularies) and the history of prior tool executions.
The LLM is explicitly instructed to decompose
complex questions into manageable sub-queries. It
then either emits a <tool_call> block to compute
a specific probability on the BN or outputs the fi-
nal calculated answer. The agentic loop terminates
when the LLM produces a final answer or reaches a
predefined budget (Tmax=5 turns, with a maximum
of Cmax=3 tool calls per run).
This iterative formulation offers two primary ad-
vantages. First, it enables the LLM to handle com-
plex, multi-step reasoning by issuing sequential
sub-queries (e.g., verifying a marginal distribution
before querying a conditional one). Second, it acts
as an intrinsic error-correction mechanism. If the
LLM hallucinates an invalid node or state, the tool
returns an execution error. The agent can then
observe this feedback, consult the provided BN vo-
cabulary, and self-correct its semantic mapping in
the subsequent turn.
We evaluate Auto-BN-Tool using GPT-4o as the
backbone LLM. As detailed in Table 17, the agen-
tic framework delivers strong overall performance,
achieving an Acc0.05 of 47.6%, a MAE of 0.105,
and an RMSE of 0.166. Furthermore, its strict
accuracy (27.8% Acc0.02) remains highly com-
petitive with the base Auto-BN pipeline (28.0%)
while successfully maintaining a near-zero error
rate (0.1%). Ultimately, these results demonstrate
that integrating Auto-BN into an autonomous agent
loop not only preserves the rigorous, hallucination-
free grounding of exact inference, but also main-
tains system robustness and calibration through
multi-turn self-correction and compositional query
decomposition.
