Abstract
Task-oriented dialogue (ToD) systems aim
to help users accomplish goals through nat-
ural language interaction. Beyond task suc-
cess, effective ToD systems must also main-
tain positive emotional interaction and ac-
curately convey information in inherently
noisy and ambiguous conversational envi-
ronments.
Recent advances in large lan-
guage models (LLMs) have substantially
improved conversational fluency and con-
textual understanding. However, although
emotion has been incorporated into exist-
ing ToD systems, its integration is typi-
cally confined to limited roles, restricting
its influence on overall dialogue behaviour
and user experience.
To address this, we
propose a novel end-to-end framework that
combines fully lexicalised representations
with an LLM backbone for improved se-
mantic accuracy and employs online rein-
forcement learning with short-term emo-
tional and long-term task-success rewards
for joint optimisation of emotional interac-
tion and task success. We further develop
a challenging and realistic simulation envi-
ronment that enables systematic evaluation
across diverse ToD set-ups.
Experiments
show that incorporating coarse affective sig-
nals improves task success and sentiment
rating in aggregate. Furthermore, integrat-
ing emotional interaction throughout the di-
alogue system using our proposed frame-
work results in more pronounced improve-
ments in both task success and user emo-
tional experience.1

Related Work
2.1
Modular Systems
Traditional ToD systems adopt a modular pipeline
consisting of natural language understanding
(NLU), dialogue state tracking (DST), dialogue
policy
learning,
natural
language
generation
(NLG), and database querying supported by an
ontology (Young, 2002). These components are
typically trained independently, with NLU, DST,
and NLG optimised via supervised learning (SL)
and the dialogue policy trained using reinforce-
ment learning (RL, Young et al. 2013 through in-
teraction with real (Gaši´c et al., 2013) or simulated
users (Lin et al., 2022).
To manage the complexity of the action space,
policies commonly output delexicalised actions,
which are later realised into natural language re-
sponses using database values.
While this ab-
straction simplifies policy learning, it separates
decision-making from surface realisation and lim-
its joint optimisation across components.
Emotion integration in modular systems pri-
marily occurs at the policy level.
Prior work
introduces emotion signals as additional policy
inputs (Zhu et al., 2024) or auxiliary RL re-
wards (Zhang et al., 2021), enabling policies to
adapt to users’ affective states. However, optimi-
sation remains confined to the policy, while other
modules are fixed after independently training. As
a result, emotional signals influence behaviour lo-
cally without reshaping system-wide representa-
tions, leading to misalignment among modules
during full-system interaction.
Recent work, such as EmoLoop (Feng et al.,
2024a), partially mitigates this limitation by op-
timising an emotion-aware policy using natural-
language interaction (Figure 2b).
Nevertheless,
only policy parameters are updated, leaving up-
stream and downstream components unchanged.
The long-standing problem of error propagation
across modular pipelines remains unresolved.
2.2

to-end ToD systems unify dialogue modelling
within a single learnable architecture. Early work
builds fully trainable neural models (Wen et al.,
2017), while recent approaches leverage decoder-
only language models to autoregressively map dia-
logue history to belief states, dialogue actions, and
system responses (Hosseini-Asl et al., 2020; Peng
et al., 2021) (Figure 2c).

to-end systems retain design choices inherited
from modular pipelines, particularly the use of
delexicalised placeholders that are later replaced

End-to-end ToD systems are predominantly

on overall dialogue behaviour.
2.3
Agentic Systems
The emergence of general-purpose LLMs has
enabled agentic ToD systems that perform dia-
logue reasoning through prompting rather than
task-specific training.
These systems adapt to
new tasks using instructions or demonstrations in
zero- or few-shot settings, often employing tech-
niques such as chain-of-thought prompting or self-
refinement to improve reasoning consistency (Wei
et al., 2022; Yao et al., 2023).
Such capabilities have been applied to system
components (slot filling (Sun et al., 2024), dia-
logue state tracking (Heck et al., 2023), emotion
recognition (Feng et al., 2024b), ontology con-
struction Vukovic et al. (2024)) and fully prompt-
driven ToD agents (Li et al., 2024).
While
highly flexible, agentic systems remain sensitive
to prompt design, exhibit variability across inter-
actions, and lack the robustness achieved by task-
specific optimisation (Hudeˇcek and Dusek, 2023).
Emotion in agentic systems is incorporated pri-
marily through implicit alignment or prompting.
RL from human feedback (RLHF) encourages
generally polite or empathetic behaviour (Ouyang
et al., 2022b). However, such preferences consti-
tute multiple reward factors and are not explicitly
aligned with the core objectives of ToDs, namely
short-term user sentiment and long-term task suc-
cess.
Alternatively, emotional behaviour can
be specified through prompt instructions, making
emotion a behavioural guideline imposed at infer-
ence time rather than a learnable optimisation ob-
jective. Hence, emotional interaction is not explic-

itly optimised during dialogue learning in existing
agentic ToD systems.
Prior dialogue systems have used categorical
affect or sentiment labels for emotion recogni-
tion, response style control, and policy optimi-
sation.
Across modular, end-to-end, and agen-
tic paradigms, however, such signals typically in-
fluence behaviour locally rather than serving as
system-level optimisation objectives. This moti-
vates our development of LUSTER, a unified end-
to-end ToD framework that incorporates coarse af-
fective signals directly into optimisation. Our con-
tribution is therefore not the use of categorical af-
fect itself, but a framework for treating such affec-
tive signals as optimisation targets alongside task
success, and an empirical study of how this de-
sign interacts with task completion, post-dialogue
user sentiment, semantic expression, and observ-
able dialogue behaviour. This distinction is impor-
tant because affective style alone does not guaran-
tee task success, while task success alone does not
guarantee a positive user experience.
3
LUSTER: LLM-based Unified System
for Task-oriented dialogue with
End-to-end Reinforcement Learning
LUSTER integrates emotional interaction into
both inference and optimisation, enabling dia-
logue behaviour to be jointly shaped by task suc-
cess and affective outcomes.
The framework
adopts an end-to-end architecture built on a pre-
trained LLM backbone, operates on fully lexi-
calised sequences, and employs online RL with
short-term emotional and long-term task-success
rewards.
Section 3.1 describes the sequential inference
process, while Sections 3.2 and 3.4 present the key
design choices distinguishing LUSTER from prior
LM-based end-to-end ToD systems. Section 3.3
describes the scope of emotion used in this work.
3.1
Inference with LUSTER
LUSTER models ToD as a sequence of struc-
tured generation stages in which intermediate se-
mantic and affective representations are explicitly
generated and reused to guide subsequent deci-
sions (Figure 3). Beyond traditional ToD subtasks
(DST, policy learning, NLG), the framework in-
corporates user emotion recognition and system
conduct selection as intermediate decisions, al-
lowing affective information to influence down-
stream reasoning and response generation.
Unlike prior LM-based end-to-end systems
that separate generation stages using special to-
kens (e.g., <|state|>), LUSTER adopts a
lightweight prefix-based format inspired by Li
and Liang (2021), where each task begins with
a natural-language cue (e.g., “state :”) and ter-
minates with a standard end-of-sequence token.
This design improves compatibility with pre-
trained LLM tokenisers and stabilises training un-
der parameter-efficient fine-tuning.
At each dialogue turn, LUSTER generates in-
termediate representations in the following fixed
sequence.

3.2
Fully Lexicalised Representation
LUSTER adopts a fully lexicalised representa-
tion in which both intermediate reasoning and
final responses are expressed directly in natural
language. Unlike modular and prior end-to-end
systems that rely on delexicalised placeholders,
LUSTER preserves concrete slot values through-
out inference, leveraging the world knowledge and
generative capabilities of pretrained LLMs (Fig-
ure 2d). Specifically, (1) dialogue actions include
explicit values alongside intents and slots, (2)
responses are generated directly without place-
holder substitution, and (3) database results are
represented as structured, value-rich entity de-
scriptions rather than abstract summaries (e.g., “3
options found”).
This design removes the need for post-hoc lex-
icalisation and avoids modifying the LLM vocab-
ulary or resizing embedding and output layers, re-
ducing the number of trainable parameters even
under parameter-efficient fine-tuning. Such effi-
ciency is critical for enabling online RL in inter-
active environments, which would otherwise be
computationally prohibitive for large LLMs.
Beyond efficiency, lexicalised representations
improve modularity and forward compatibility, al-
lowing LUSTER to adopt newer LLM backbones
without redesigning symbolic interfaces or output
vocabularies.
3.3
User and System Affective States
LUSTER uses affective annotations derived from
EmoWOZ (Feng et al., 2022) rather than introduc-
ing a new emotion taxonomy. Both the user simu-
lator and the system-side user-emotion recognition
component use the seven user emotion categories
defined in EmoWOZ, namely Neutral, Fearful,
Dissatisfied, Apologetic, Abusive, Excited, and
Satisfied. In addition, the system’s emotional con-
duct follows the five conduct categories introduced
in EmoWOZ 2.0 (Feng et al., 2024a): Neutral,
Compassionate, Apologetic, Enthusiastic, and Ap-
preciative. These labels are predicted and gener-
ated as part of the dialogue trajectory, allowing
LUSTER to represent not only what the user is
trying to achieve, but also the affective state ex-
pressed by the user and the emotional conduct se-
lected by the system.
3.4
Training Objectives
LUSTER is trained in two stages: SL provides be-
havioural grounding from human demonstrations,
followed by RL that jointly optimises emotional
experience and task success through online inter-
action.

Experimental Set-up
4.1
ToD Systems
We evaluate emotion as an independent design
dimension across agentic, modular, and end-to-
end ToD paradigms. For each system, we com-
pare a non-emotional variant with an emotion-
aware counterpart whenever applicable, allowing
us to isolate the effect of emotion integration while
keeping the architecture and training unchanged.
We consider the following systems.
FnCTOD
(Li et al., 2024) is an agentic system
that performs prompt-based zero-shot generation

for DST, database querying, and response genera-
tion. We follow the original prompts and use GPT-
4o-mini (2025-04-16) as the backbone. Its emo-
tional variant augments the prompt with instruc-
tions to consider user emotions and generate emo-
tionally appropriate responses.
Modular-base
is a modular pipeline consisting
of SetSUMBT DST (van Niekerk et al., 2021),
DDPT policy (Geishauser et al., 2022), and SEC-
BART NLG (Feng et al., 2024a). The policy is
trained via RL under semantic interaction with
EmoUS (Lin et al., 2023), using task-success re-
wards (Figure 2a). Modular-emotion extends this
system by adding user emotion as a policy in-
put via an emotion recogniser (Feng et al., 2023),
producing system conduct decisions and receiving
turn-level sentiment rewards.
SimpleLoop
(Feng et al., 2024a) uses the same
modular architecture as Modular-base but per-
forms RL optimisation within a natural-language
interaction loop using langEmoUS (Feng et al.,
2024a) (Figure 2b).
Its emotional counterpart,
EmoLoop, additionally incorporates user emotion
input, system conduct output, and sentiment-based
rewards.
SimpleLLAMA
(Stricker and Paroubek, 2024)
is an end-to-end system that autoregressively gen-
erates dialogue state, dialogue action, and re-
sponse using a 7B Llama-2-chat backbone (Tou-
vron et al., 2023). It produces delexicalised ac-
tions and responses and is trained with SL. EmoL-
LAMA extends this model with two additional
intermediate tasks: user emotion recognition and
system conduct selection.
LUSTER
is our proposed end-to-end frame-
work described in Section 3, trained via RL
through natural-language interaction with langE-
moUS (Figure 2d).
Its non-emotional coun-
terpart, LUSTER-base, removes emotion-related
tasks and RL. Unless otherwise specified, LUS-
TER uses Llama-3.2-3b-instruct (Grattafiori et al.,
2024) as the backbone. Additional backbone ex-
periments are reported in Section 5.3.

Supervised Learning
All
systems
and
sub-modules
are
trained
on

an
extension
of

8,438

Systematically characterising this

4.3
Reinforcement Learning and Interactive
Evaluation
The RL of LUSTER and the subsequent inter-
active evaluation are conducted using ConvLab-
3 (Zhu et al., 2023).
We use the natural-
language user simulator langEmoUS together with
T5NLU (Zhu et al., 2023) for dialogue under-
standing, creating a realistic noisy interaction en-
vironment.
This set-up is substantially more demanding
than standard policy optimisation and evaluation
settings with user simulators.
In conventional
RL set-ups, interaction typically occurs at the se-
mantic level (Geishauser et al., 2022). Even in
natural-language settings, user simulators often
still receive semantic actions directly from the pol-
icy (Feng et al., 2024a), so user goals can be up-
dated unambiguously. By contrast, in real interac-
tion, users only observe the system’s natural lan-
guage response and do not have access to the un-
derlying semantic action. As a result, successful
communication depends entirely on whether the
intended semantics are correctly conveyed through
natural language.
To better reflect natural-language interaction,
we do not pass semantic actions directly to the
simulated user for goal updates or success eval-
uation. Instead, the user side relies on the NLU
module to parse the system response and recover
the underlying semantics for internal state updates.
This introduces an additional layer of uncertainty
and noise, making evaluation more sensitive to er-
rors in surface realisation.
For each system, we simulate 3000 evaluation
dialogues.
These simulated dialogues are sepa-
rate from the EmoWOZ SL split: they are gen-

erated during RL and evaluation, and are not
used as supervised training data. Implementation
details, hyperparameters, training configurations,
and computational resources are provided in Ap-
pendices A.1 and A.5.
The challenging set-up partly explains the sub-
stantially lower performance of baseline systems
in Table 1 that were originally developed or eval-
uated under cleaner semantic-interaction settings.
At the same time, LUSTER’s stronger perfor-
mance under the same protocol highlights the ro-
bustness of its fully lexicalised end-to-end optimi-
sation.
4.4
Evaluation Metrics
We evaluate each system along three complemen-
tary dimensions: task completion, affective inter-
action, and semantic faithfulness.
Task success.
Task success measures whether
the dialogue fulfils the user’s goal. The user sim-
ulator parses the semantic information expressed
by the system and compares it against its inter-
nal goal.
A dialogue is considered successful
if the system provides the requested information,
the returned entity satisfies the user’s search con-
straints, and a booking is completed when re-
quired. Task success is therefore a dialogue-level
metric: it evaluates whether the interaction as a
whole achieves the user’s task, rather than whether
each individual system response is semantically
perfect.
User sentiment.
User sentiment measures the
affective trajectory of the interaction from the sim-
ulator’s perspective. At each user turn, the sim-
ulator estimates the user’s emotion and maps it
to a scalar score based on valence polarity: +1
for positive, 0 for neutral, and −1 for negative.
The reported user sentiment score is the average
of these turn-level sentiment values across the di-
alogue. This metric captures whether the interac-
tion tends to leave the simulated user in a more
positive or negative affective state, but it should
not be interpreted as a complete measure of hu-
man emotion.
Concept error rate.
Concept error rate mea-
sures the semantic faithfulness of the generated
system response with respect to the system’s inter-
nal semantic action. It detects missing concepts,
where semantic values specified by the action are
omitted from the surface response, and halluci-
nated concepts, where the response mentions se-
mantic values that are not licensed by the action.
Concept error rate is therefore a response-level di-
agnostic of semantic expression, rather than a di-
rect measure of dialogue-level task completion.
These metrics are related but not interchange-
able.
A system may achieve low concept error
while still failing the task because of policy-level
mistakes, invalid database choices, missing book-
ing actions, or failure to provide all requested in-
formation.
Conversely, a system may complete
the task while still producing occasional semantic
omissions or hallucinations. Similarly, user sen-
timent reflects the affective quality of the interac-
tion, but positive sentiment alone does not guar-
antee task success. We therefore report all three
metrics to distinguish task performance, affective
user experience, and semantic faithfulness.

Model Performance

observable impact of emotion-aware modelling.

5.3.1
Supervised Learning
We configure all models to include emotion-
modelling intermediate tasks and train them with
SL. We aim to examine scaling behaviour and
identify a strong backbone for subsequent RL.
Figure 4a and 4b visualise the trend of each metric
against model size.
Task success generally improves with model
size across modern LLM families, although gains
diminish at larger scales.
This motivates our
choice of Llama-3.2-3B for the main experiments,
as it provides a balanced trade-off between perfor-
mance and computational cost.
Sentiment shows a more nuanced trend. For
Llama, Qwen, and Phi, sentiment tends to improve
with scale, suggesting that larger models capture
affective cues and generate emotionally appropri-
ate responses better. In contrast, GPT-2 exhibits a
non-monotonic trend: sentiment declines at inter-
mediate scales before partially recovering at 1.5B.
One plausible explanation is that smaller GPT-2
models rely heavily on surface-level heuristics or
templated responses that may appear emotionally
consistent, whereas mid-sized variants lack suffi-
cient representational capacity to robustly model
both task semantics and emotional nuance. Only
at larger scales does GPT-2 regain enough capacity
to stabilise emotional behaviour, although it still
underperforms compared to modern LLMs.
Notably, the divergence between success and
sentiment trends highlights that sentiment is not
strictly correlated with task success. This suggests
that emotion constitutes an additional optimisation
dimension, emphasising the need to explicitly in-
corporate emotional objectives into dialogue sys-
tem training rather than assuming they will emerge
from the optimisation for task success.

whereas the simulator more often produced fuller,
more explicit utterances (see Appendix B.1). This
distinction is important for interpreting the qual-

5.5
Scope, Limitations, and Future
Directions
LUSTER models coarse affective interaction sig-
nals rather than emotion in its full psychologi-
cal complexity. The EmoWOZ user-emotion cat-
egories and EmoWOZ 2.0 system-conduct cate-
gories provide a structured way to incorporate af-
fect into ToD optimisation, but they do not cap-
ture mixed emotions, appraisal, personality, long-
term affective trajectories, or broader social con-

text. Similarly, our human evaluation measures
perceived task success, overall performance, and
self-reported post-dialogue sentiment; this score
is affect-relevant, but should not be interpreted as
a direct measure of emotion recognition or emo-
tional intelligence.
These limitations define the scope of the work
rather than undermine its contribution. Our goal is
not to provide a complete psychological account
of emotion, but to study whether structured af-
fective signals can be integrated into end-to-end
ToD optimisation and how they interact with task
success, user experience, semantic expression, and
observable dialogue behaviour. Our results show
that affective optimisation is most useful when
combined with task-oriented objectives, while also
revealing where current affective modelling re-
mains insufficient.
Our ablations isolate the effects of emotion
modelling and reward design within a matched
LUSTER architecture. They do not rule out the
possibility that similar RL objectives could im-
prove other ToD systems. However, applying the
same RL procedure to large agentic systems or ex-
isting delexicalised end-to-end baselines is non-
trivial: it would require different interaction in-
terfaces, expanded lexical vocabularies, retrained
embedding and output layers, and substantial ad-
ditional computation. We therefore leave broader
RL adaptation of other architectures to future
work.
More broadly, LUSTER connects affect-aware
ToD to richer user modelling. By estimating both
the user’s task-related state and affective state, the
system models what the user is trying to achieve
and how the user appears to feel during the in-
teraction.
These two dimensions are related to
theory-of-mind-inspired accounts of interaction,
but LUSTER does not yet claim to model the
user’s beliefs, intentions, or latent mental states
in a psychologically complete sense. Future work
could extend this connection by incorporating
richer models of user belief, uncertainty, prefer-
ence, and affective trajectory.
6
Conclusion
This work revisits the role of emotion in ToDs.
We show that affective signals can improve ex-
isting ToD systems, but their impact remains lim-
ited when they are integrated only locally, such as
through module-level prediction or response-style
control.
To address this, we introduced LUSTER, a
lexicalised end-to-end ToD framework that inte-
grates user-emotion modelling, system emotional-
conduct prediction, and joint optimisation of task
success and user sentiment.
Experiments show
that LUSTER achieves strong task performance,
improved user sentiment, and low concept error
under a noisy natural-language interaction pro-
tocol. Ablation studies further show that affec-
tive and task-oriented rewards play complemen-
tary roles, and backbone replication confirms that
the main pattern is not tied to a single model fam-
ily.
Human evaluation, behavioural analysis, and
LLM-based preference judgements suggest that
affect-aware optimisation leads to perceptible im-
provements in conversational quality, especially in
clarification, repair, and affective closure. These
findings show that emotional interaction is an im-
portant dimension of ToD performance: task suc-
cess alone does not guarantee positive user expe-
rience, while affective style alone does not guar-
antee reliable task completion. LUSTER there-
fore provides a practical step toward ToD systems
that jointly optimise task effectiveness and human-
centric interaction quality.
In a broader context, this work signifies an im-
portant step towards incorporating an explicit the-
ory of mind into the operation of LLMs. Theory
of mind relates to modelling of cognitive and emo-
tional states of others and, as such, represents an
integral component of intelligence (Perner, 1999).
While LLMs do not by design model the theory of
mind, they can imitate it quite well (Chen et al.,
2025). In this work, we show how modelling of
user goal through dialogue states and modelling
user affective state through emotion classes can be
represented in an LLM backbone, both modelled
by SL and RL and achieve overall better perfor-
mance. This opens the door to the further devel-
opment of such architectures in high-risk appli-
cations necessitating emotion modelling, such as
psychotherapeutic contexts.

Complementary Results
B.1
Examples from Human Trial
Compared with the simulated user, participants in the paid human study tended to express their requests
as concisely as possible in order to complete the assigned goal efficiently. This reflects a different interac-
tion style from that of the simulator. Below, we present several dialogue openings from similar scenarios
to illustrate how different systems respond to underspecified or ambiguous user requests at the start of
an interaction. These examples are not intended as a controlled head-to-head comparison or as definitive
evidence that one system is superior; rather, they serve as realistic snippets that highlight qualitatively
different response patterns.
User : Train day sunday.
EmoLoop : There are 10 trains that leave at 05:00 from london kings cross.
User : Train.
LUSTER-base :i can certainly help you. where would you like to depart from and what is your destination and
travel date and time?
User : TrainID.
LUSTER : i dont know what day youre traveling. you should tell me the day and then i can look it up for you. i
also dont know what your departure and destination locations are.
The modular system, EmoLoop, immediately returns the number of available options, reflecting a
strong prior over the dialogue state. This may hinder task progression when the user later specifies dif-
ferent constraints, but it can be effective when the user’s initial request already aligns with those assump-
tions. By contrast, both LUSTER and LUSTER-base attempt to clarify the missing constraints needed
to narrow the search space, although they do so using different strategies, largely due to differences in
the users’ initial requests.
B.2
Examples from Behavioural Analysis
We provide representative examples from the human-evaluation logs to illustrate the behavioural cate-
gories discussed in Section 5.4.2. The examples show both useful affective behaviour, such as repair
and warm closure, and remaining failure modes, such as over-apology, redundant clarification, and task
inconsistency.
Over-apology.
The following example shows that surface-level apology is not sufficient for natural
affective competence.
Context: The system apologises for confusion.
User: why are you apologising?
LUSTER-base: i am a bot and i am programmed to apologize even when i dont have any reason to do so.
Failure triggering user frustration.
The following example illustrates how task-level inconsistency
can lead to strongly negative user feedback.
Context: The user wants a train to arrive by 9:45, but the system provides a train leaving at 10:45.
User: how can it arrive by 9:45?
LUSTER-base: i do not understand. are you saying you need to arrive before you leave? User: you are useless
Repair after confusion.
The following example shows repair behaviour after the system carries over
irrelevant information from a previous task.
Context: The user asks for a train after an attraction-search task.
LUSTER: id love to help you find a train, but i will need more information first. thats the postcode and phone,
right?
User: why are you talking about postcode and phone???
LUSTER: i apologize for the confusion. i have train tr8224 going from cambridge to bishops stortford at 11:29.
would you like me to book 1 ticket for you?

Redundant clarification.
The following example shows that clarification behaviour can become re-
dundant when the user has already received sufficient information.
Context: The user has already received the requested information.
User: ok cool thanks!
LUSTER: what day would you like to travel, where are you departing from, and what is your destination?
